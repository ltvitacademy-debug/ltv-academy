# Lesson 9 — Presenting the Dashboard Back to the Stakeholder

**Chapter 1 · Beginner Capstone: Regional Sales Snapshot · Lesson 9 of 10**

## What you'll learn

- Why presenting a dashboard is a skill in its own right, separate from building one
- A simple structure for a two-minute walkthrough: answer first, detail second
- How to handle a question you can't answer on the spot, without losing the stakeholder's trust

## Building it was the easy part

Everything from Lessons 1 through 8 — the requirement, the query, the model, the measures,
the formatting, the publish — exists to support about two minutes of conversation on
Monday. That conversation is where the project either lands or doesn't. A technically
correct report, presented badly, still fails the stakeholder; a modest report, presented
well, succeeds. This lesson is about that two minutes.

## Lead with the answer, not a tour

The biggest mistake in presenting any dashboard is walking through it visual by visual, top
to bottom, narrating what each one is before saying what it means. For a time-pressed
stakeholder, that's backwards. Instead, open by answering their actual question — the one
from Lesson 1 — directly, in one sentence, before pointing at anything:

> "Overall, this quarter is up compared to last — total sales are [X], across [Y] orders,
> and the Southeast is tracking with the rest of the territories."

Only after that headline does the walkthrough move to supporting detail, and only as much of
it as the conversation calls for:

1. **The headline** (10–15 seconds): the KPI cards, stated as a sentence, not just pointed
   at.
2. **The "why"** (30–45 seconds): the bar chart — which regions or product lines are driving
   or dragging the headline number.
3. **The detail, on request** (remaining time): the table, opened only if the stakeholder
   asks a follow-up that needs it — "which product line in the Southeast specifically?" —
   not narrated by default.

## Using the slicer live, not just describing it

Since Lesson 7 built a `Territory` slicer, use it during the conversation instead of just
mentioning it exists: click the Southeast territory live, and let the stakeholder watch the
KPI cards and bar chart update in front of them. Seeing the report respond is more
convincing than being told it can.

## When you don't know the answer

A stakeholder will sometimes ask something the report wasn't built to answer — "how does
this compare to the same quarter last year?" This project deliberately didn't include a
year-over-year comparison (Lesson 1's scoping call). The right response isn't to guess or
to apologize at length — it's a short, honest answer that keeps the conversation moving:

> "That's not in this version — it was scoped to this quarter only. I can add a
> year-over-year comparison as a follow-up if that's useful for next time."

This both protects your credibility and turns an unanswered question into the start of the
next iteration, rather than a gap that undermines the whole report.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Headline-first structure | Stating the answer before showing the supporting visuals |
| Live demonstration | Using a slicer or interaction during the conversation, not just describing it |
| Scoping response | A short, honest answer to an out-of-scope question that opens the door to a follow-up |

## Lab

Write out, word for word, the one-sentence headline you would say first for this report
(using placeholder numbers), then outline the 30–45 second "why" you'd give right after it.
Practice saying both out loud in under one minute total.

## Check yourself

You're ready for Lesson 10 when you can explain why a dashboard walkthrough should lead with
the answer rather than a tour of the visuals, and how to respond to a question the report
wasn't scoped to answer.
