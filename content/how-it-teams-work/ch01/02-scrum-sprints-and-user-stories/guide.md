# Scrum, Sprints & User Stories

Scrum is the specific, named framework most teams mean when they say "we're Agile." It names the
roles, the meetings, and the vocabulary you'll actually hear in a standup — so this lesson puts
real names on the ideas Lesson 1 introduced in the abstract.

## What you'll learn

- The three roles on a Scrum team, and what each one actually owns
- How a sprint moves from backlog to review
- What a user story and acceptance criteria really are

## The three roles

Scrum names exactly three roles, and each one owns something different:

![Three roles, one sprint: the Product Owner owns the backlog and decides what gets built and in what order; the Scrum Master facilitates ceremonies and removes blockers for the team; the Developers build, test, and deliver the sprint's working increment.](/courses/how-it-teams-work/ch01/02-scrum-sprints-and-user-stories/scrum-team-roles.png)

The **Product Owner** is the single person accountable for the backlog's priority order — they
decide what gets built next, usually by talking to the business and balancing competing requests.
The **Scrum Master** doesn't manage the team's work; they facilitate the Scrum ceremonies and
clear whatever is blocking the Developers, from a stuck approval to a flaky test environment. The
**Developers** are everyone actually building the increment — engineers, analysts, testers,
whoever's hands are on the work.

## A sprint, start to finish

A sprint is one fixed-length cycle, almost always one or two weeks. It opens with **Sprint
Planning**, where the team picks a sprint goal and pulls in backlog items they believe they can
finish. Every day during the sprint, the team holds a short **Daily Scrum** (often called
standup) — not a status report to a manager, but the team synchronizing with itself on progress
and blockers. The sprint closes with a **Sprint Review**, where the team demos the finished
increment to stakeholders, and a **Retrospective**, where the team privately discusses what to
improve next sprint.

## User stories and acceptance criteria

Work in the backlog is written as **user stories** — a short description of a need from the
user's point of view, typically in the shape "As a [role], I want [capability], so that
[benefit]." For example: "As a sales manager, I want a dashboard filtered by region, so that I can
spot underperforming territories without asking an analyst."

A story on its own is often too vague to know when it's actually done, which is what
**acceptance criteria** are for — specific, checkable conditions the finished work must satisfy.
For the story above, acceptance criteria might include "the region filter defaults to the
viewer's assigned territory" and "changing the filter updates every chart on the page in under
two seconds." A story isn't complete until its acceptance criteria are all met — that's the
difference between "I think this is done" and a shared, checkable definition of done.

## From backlog to something real

Put the whole chain together and a story's actual journey looks like this:

![From backlog to shippable increment: the Product Backlog holds every idea and request in priority order, Sprint Planning chooses a subset into the Sprint Backlog for this sprint only, Sprint Work runs analysis, design, build, test, and review over 1-2 weeks with a daily 15-minute sync, producing a Shippable Increment — working software ready to release, every single sprint.](/courses/how-it-teams-work/ch01/02-scrum-sprints-and-user-stories/backlog-to-increment.png)

Notice the last box: **shippable increment**, not "finished feature" or "code that compiles."
Scrum's actual promise is that at the end of every sprint, the team has something that could be
released right now, even if the business chooses to hold it for a later launch — "could ship
today" is a higher bar than "the ticket is marked Done."

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Product Owner | Owns and prioritizes the backlog — decides what gets built next |
| Scrum Master | Facilitates Scrum ceremonies and removes blockers for the team |
| Sprint | One fixed-length cycle (usually 1-2 weeks) of planning, building, and reviewing |
| User story | A requirement written from the user's point of view: "As a [role], I want [capability], so that [benefit]" |
| Acceptance criteria | Specific, checkable conditions a user story must satisfy to count as done |
| Retrospective | The end-of-sprint meeting where the team discusses what to improve next sprint |
