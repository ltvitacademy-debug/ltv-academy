## One project, three audiences

You'll present the same capstone — the LTV Customer Management System, built for the fictional "Cascade Foodservice Equipment Co." — differently depending on who's listening:

- **A recruiter** wants the business problem and outcome in plain language, in under a minute. They are not evaluating your sharing model.
- **A hiring manager** wants to hear decisions and tradeoffs — why you built it this way, what it demonstrates about how you'll work on their team.
- **A technical panel** wants the detail: the actual security model, the actual automation, and the reasoning behind both, because they can evaluate it directly.

Have all three versions ready. Using the technical-panel version on a recruiter screen wastes your limited time; using the recruiter version on a technical panel reads as unprepared.

## A structure that scales to any audience

1. **The business problem.** What was Cascade Foodservice Equipment Co. actually struggling with — not "I built a CRM," but the specific operational pain the project addresses.
2. **The solution design.** Accounts, Contacts, Leads, Opportunities, the custom objects you added, and why each one exists.
3. **The decisions that mattered.** Your security model (why these org-wide defaults, why this sharing rule), your automation (why a validation rule here and a Flow there), and the reasoning behind each — this is where Lessons 11 and 17's "explain your decisions" material gets used for real.
4. **The result.** What reports and dashboards came out of it, and what they'd actually tell a sales or operations leader.
5. **What you'd do differently at scale.** A brief, honest note on what this design assumes and where it would need to change for a much larger org — this signals maturity more than pretending the design has no limits.

## Demo tips

- **Narrate while you click.** Silent clicking makes a reviewer focus on your mouse instead of your reasoning.
- **Have a backup plan.** A live org demo can break — a login hiccup, a slow network, a field that got changed since you last checked. Keep screenshots or a short recording of the key screens ready so a technical failure doesn't derail the whole conversation.
- **Know your own project cold.** If you're asked "why didn't you use a Flow here instead," you should have an actual answer, not a guess made up on the spot.

## Connecting back to your portfolio

Lesson 4 covered building a portfolio around this capstone. The presentation structure above is the live, spoken version of that same README and case-study write-up — consistent framing across your portfolio page, your resume bullet, and what comes out of your mouth in the interview room makes the whole story easier for an interviewer to follow and remember.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Business problem framing | Describing what the project solves in terms of the operational pain it addresses, not just the features built |
| Audience-tiered presentation | Adjusting technical depth to who's in the room, without changing the underlying facts |
| Backup plan (demo) | Screenshots or a recording kept ready in case a live demo fails |
