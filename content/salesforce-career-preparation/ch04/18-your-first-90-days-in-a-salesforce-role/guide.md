# Lesson 18 — Your First 90 Days in a Salesforce Role

**Chapter 4 · Presenting Your Work · Lesson 18 of 19**

## What you'll learn

- A realistic arc for the first 30, 60, and 90 days in a new Salesforce role
- The habits that build trust early, and the ones that burn it
- The most common mistakes new admins and developers make in their first weeks
- Why "get a quick win" matters more than trying to prove everything at once

## Days 1-30: learn the organization before you touch it

Your first month is mostly about understanding what already exists, not changing it:

- **Meet the people who touch Salesforce** — sales, marketing, support, finance, operations. Ask what they do and where Salesforce helps or gets in their way.
- **Run the setup audit trail** and save it somewhere you can reference later, so you have a record of what's happened in the org before you arrived.
- **Study the real data model** — the standard and custom objects actually in use, their relationships, and the fields that matter day to day.
- **Study profiles, permission sets, and sharing** — understand who can see what *before* you change any of it.
- **Shadow existing tickets or a support rotation** if one exists, so you see real day-to-day issues before you're the one fixing them solo.

## Days 30-60: small, well-scoped wins

Once you understand the org, start taking small tasks — a new report, a minor field addition, a simple automation fix. The goal in this window isn't to prove you can rebuild the org; it's to build trust by listening to real pain points and fixing something real, visibly, without breaking anything else in the process.

## Days 60-90: own something, propose carefully

By the third month, you should be ready to own a small project end to end, and to start proposing improvements — carefully, and grounded in what you learned in the first 60 days, not in a generic "best practice" you read somewhere. Document what you build as you go; a habit started here pays off for the rest of your time in the role.

## Common early mistakes

- **Changing production directly** because sandbox testing felt like an unnecessary step for "something small." It rarely is.
- **Adding automation on top of automation you don't fully understand yet** — stacking a new Flow onto a trigger nobody explained to you first is how recursion and conflicting logic get introduced.
- **Trying to re-architect everything in week one.** Even a genuinely bad existing design usually has a reason it ended up that way, and you won't know that reason until you've been there long enough to ask the right people.
- **Going quiet instead of asking.** New hires who ask "why does this work this way?" build more trust than ones who guess and get it wrong.

## Why the "quick win" matters

A real, visible improvement early on does more for your credibility than a long list of things you're "planning to fix." It shows stakeholders you can be trusted with bigger things, and it gives you something concrete and specific to point to the next time you're explaining your own work — in a future interview, or in your next performance review.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Setup Audit Trail | A log of configuration changes made in an org, useful for understanding its history |
| Quick win | A small, real, visible improvement delivered early to build trust |
| Shadowing | Observing existing work (tickets, support rotations) before taking it on independently |
