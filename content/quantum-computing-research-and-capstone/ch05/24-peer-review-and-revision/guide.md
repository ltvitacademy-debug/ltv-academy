# Peer Review & Revision

The capstone's content is done. This closing lesson of Chapter 5 is about the step that turns a finished draft into a genuinely stronger piece of work: having someone else read it critically, and actually revising based on what they find.

## What you'll learn

- Why peer review is a core part of real research practice, not an optional extra step
- A structured self-review pass you can run before anyone else sees your work
- What to actually ask a reviewer for, so their feedback is useful rather than vague
- How to triage feedback once you receive it — what to act on versus what to note and set aside
- A worked example applying Lesson 2's own "reading a paper critically" skill to your own report

## Peer review is how real research actually gets checked

Every paper you read critically in Lesson 2 went through some form of review before (or as part of) publication — a second set of eyes checking methodology, claims, and clarity. Treating this as optional for your own capstone, just because there's no formal journal requiring it, skips the single step most likely to catch the gap between what you think you wrote and what you actually wrote.

## A structured self-review pass, before anyone else reads it

Re-read your own report using Lesson 2's own reading pass, as if it were someone else's paper: does the abstract/summary's claim match what the results section's numbers actually support? Is every hypothesis claim from Lesson 12 addressed explicitly in the conclusions, including any that weren't fully supported? Does the limitations section name every confound and scope boundary you know about, not just the convenient ones? This self-review alone typically catches a meaningful fraction of the issues a peer reviewer would otherwise flag.

## Asking a reviewer for useful feedback

Vague requests ("can you look this over?") tend to produce vague, unhelpful feedback. Ask specifically: "Does my methods section give you enough detail that you could attempt to replicate this?" (tests Lesson 8-style specificity, turned on your own writing); "Does my conclusion claim anything my results don't actually support?" (tests Lesson 20's scoping discipline); "Is there anywhere you got confused or had to re-read a sentence?" (catches clarity problems you can't easily see in your own familiar writing). Naming the specific thing you want checked gets you a specific, actionable answer.

## Triaging feedback once you receive it

Not all feedback should be acted on identically. Sort incoming comments into: **must-fix** (a factual error, an unsupported claim, a genuinely unclear methods step — fix these before considering the report final), **worth considering** (a suggestion that would improve clarity or framing but isn't strictly required — use your judgment and remaining time), and **noted but not changing** (a stylistic preference you disagree with, or a suggestion outside your project's actual scope — it's fine to note you considered it and chose not to act on it, rather than either ignoring it silently or treating every comment as mandatory).

## A worked example

Applying Lesson 2's reading pass to your own running-example report: does the conclusion ("mitigation improves fidelity at both qubit counts, with a smaller improvement at 5 qubits") match what the results section's actual numbers show (0.155 vs. 0.120 reduction)? Yes — this passes. Does the limitations section mention the calibration-window confound from the original, uncorrected runs? If a self-review reveals you forgot to mention it (easy to do, since by the time you're writing conclusions, the corrected numbers feel like "the result"), that's a must-fix: omitting it would mean a careful reader couldn't properly evaluate how sensitive the finding is to experimental timing — exactly the kind of omission Lesson 2 taught you to catch in other people's work.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Self-review pass | Re-reading your own work using the same critical framework you'd apply to someone else's paper |
| Feedback triage | Sorting reviewer comments into must-fix, worth considering, and noted-but-not-changing categories |

## Check yourself

A reviewer tells you: "Your conclusion says mitigation 'significantly' helps, but I don't see a significance test anywhere in your results section." Using this lesson's triage categories, which category does this comment belong in, and what specific action (if any) should you take?
