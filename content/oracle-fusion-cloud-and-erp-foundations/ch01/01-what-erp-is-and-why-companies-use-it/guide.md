## The problem before ERP

Picture a fictional company, **Blue Harbor Manufacturing**. Its sales team tracks orders in one spreadsheet. The warehouse tracks inventory in another. Accounting re-types sales totals into a bookkeeping program at month end. HR keeps employee records in a filing cabinet and a separate payroll service. Nothing talks to anything else.

This works, barely, at ten employees. At two hundred employees it falls apart. The warehouse sells inventory that's already been promised to another customer because nobody updated the spreadsheet in time. Accounting closes the books three weeks late because they're chasing numbers from five different places. The CFO can't answer "how much cash do we have right now" without a day of manual reconciliation.

## ERP: one system, one source of truth

**ERP** stands for **Enterprise Resource Planning**. Despite the dense name, the idea is simple: instead of separate tools for sales, inventory, accounting, and HR, a company runs all of it inside one connected system. A sales order placed in the morning is instantly visible to the warehouse. When the warehouse ships it, accounting already has what it needs to invoice the customer, with no retyping.

That connected system is called the company's **system of record** — the one place where a transaction's details are considered authoritative. If the ERP says a customer owes $4,200, that is the number the company uses, not whatever a spreadsheet on someone's laptop says.

## Why companies adopt one

Companies don't adopt ERP because it's fashionable. They adopt it because, past a certain size, the alternative becomes genuinely dangerous to the business:

- **Eliminating duplicate data entry.** The same order, customer, or invoice no longer gets typed into three different places by three different people, each a chance for a mismatch.
- **One accurate picture of the business.** Leadership can see sales, inventory, and cash in near-real time instead of waiting for someone to stitch spreadsheets together.
- **Standardized processes.** Every department follows the same steps to approve a purchase or close a sales order, instead of each office inventing its own.
- **Audit and compliance.** A single system with controls and an audit trail is far easier to defend to an external auditor than a web of spreadsheets with no history of who changed what.
- **Scaling without adding headcount for every new location.** Blue Harbor Manufacturing can open a second plant and plug it into the same ERP rather than build a parallel set of spreadsheets from scratch.

## Modules: the building blocks of an ERP

An ERP isn't one single program — it's a family of connected pieces, called **modules**, each covering one business function: a Financials module for accounting, a Procurement module for purchasing, a Supply Chain module for inventory and shipping, an HR module for employee data. Lesson 3 covers these in more depth. For now, the important idea is that these modules are **integrated**: a transaction in one (a purchase order, say) automatically feeds what another module needs (the invoice, and eventually the accounting entry), without a human retyping it in between.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ERP | Enterprise Resource Planning — one connected system running a company's core business processes |
| System of record | The single authoritative source for a piece of business data |
| Module | One functional piece of an ERP (Financials, Procurement, Supply Chain, HR, and others) |
| Integration | Modules automatically sharing data with each other, without manual retyping |
