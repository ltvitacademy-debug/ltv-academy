# Script — Formulating a Research Question

## Segment 1 (title)

A capstone built on a hunch isn't a research project. Before writing a line of code, SR-5 needs a research question and a hypothesis stated precisely enough that the data could actually prove it wrong. This lesson formalizes both, and explains why this project tests sectors, not individual stocks.

## Segment 2 (steps)

The question, stated formally: over 2007 to 2025, does a dollar-neutral cross-sectional 5-day reversal strategy across the 11 sector SPDRs generate risk-adjusted excess returns that survive realistic costs and walk-forward validation — and does conditioning it on the VIX improve it? The hypothesis is that short-term sector overreaction mean-reverts over about five trading days, and that the effect is stronger when the VIX is elevated than when markets are calm.

## Segment 3 (steps)

Why sectors, not single stocks? A single stock's five-day return is dominated by idiosyncratic noise — earnings, analyst calls, lawsuits — that has nothing to do with a mean-reverting mechanism. Sector ETFs solve that: the eleven Select Sector SPDRs are among the most liquid ETFs in the world, each one already diversifies away single-company noise, and eleven names is a clean, manageable cross-section to rank into terciles.

## Segment 4 (steps)

To keep the project honest, state both sides before seeing results. The null hypothesis: past five-day sector returns have no relationship to forward five-day returns — any apparent pattern is noise. The alternative: past returns are negatively related to forward returns, reversal rather than momentum, and that relationship is stronger in high-VIX regimes than low-VIX regimes. Lesson five runs the actual tests against both.

## Segment 5 (outro)

The question and hypothesis are now on paper, precise enough to be proven wrong. Next lesson: the four-phase project plan, the tooling stack, and the success criteria we commit to before we've seen a single result.
