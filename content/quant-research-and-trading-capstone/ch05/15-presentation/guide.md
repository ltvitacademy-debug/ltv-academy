# Presentation

Chapter 5 closes the SR-5 project with the last deliverable: a live presentation that compresses 14 lessons of research into roughly 10-15 minutes, followed by a Q&A you've already rehearsed in Lesson 14. This is the moment the report and the prepared answers earn their keep.

## What you'll learn

- A slide-by-slide structure for a 10-15 minute systematic-strategy presentation
- How much time to spend on each section, and why the risk slide gets more time than the headline-result slide
- How to open and close in a way that respects the audience's time
- Handling Q&A without losing control of the room

## The slide structure

A systematic-strategy presentation is shorter than the written report and leaves out most of the detail — the report is there for anyone who wants to go deeper. A reasonable 10-15 minute structure:

| # | Slide | Time | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Title & hypothesis | 1 min | SR-5, the research question from Lesson 2, the one-sentence hypothesis |
| 2 | Data & method | 2 min | 11 sector ETFs + SPY + VIX, 2007-2025, Ridge regression, purged walk-forward validation |
| 3 | Headline result | 2 min | Gross Sharpe 0.76, net Sharpe 0.42 — net number stated clearly, not glossed over |
| 4 | Risk profile | 3 min | Sortino 0.58, Calmar 0.27, VaR/CVaR, beta ~0.04 normally |
| 5 | Stress tests | 3 min | The four windows, SR-5 vs. SPY in each, the correlation-to-one caveat on COVID |
| 6 | Costs & capacity | 2 min | Turnover ~44x/year, ~$150M capacity ceiling |
| 7 | Limitations & next steps | 1-2 min | What wasn't tested, what you'd do with more time or capital |

Notice that the risk and stress-test slides get more combined time (6 minutes) than the headline-result slide (2 minutes). That allocation is deliberate: a result without its risk context is the easiest thing in the room to attack, and the easiest thing to mistrust.

## Opening: state the net number early, not at the end

Don't build to the result like a reveal — state it in the first 90 seconds, then spend the rest of the time explaining why it should be believed. A strong opening for SR-5 sounds like:

> "SR-5 is a dollar-neutral sector-reversal strategy that posts a 0.42 net Sharpe after realistic costs, with meaningfully positive relative performance in every major stress window we tested, including COVID. I want to walk through how we got that number, what it took to make it realistic, and where it's weakest."

That opening does three things at once: it gives the net result immediately, it signals that the rest of the talk is about *earning trust in* that number, not building suspense toward it, and it previews the limitations slide so nobody feels anything was held back.

## Pacing the risk and stress-test sections

This is where most of the audience's real attention goes, so don't rush it. Walk through the VaR/CVaR distinction concretely (Lesson 12: VaR95 is the threshold, CVaR95 is the average loss beyond it) rather than reading numbers off a slide. For the stress tests, don't just show the table — say the one sentence that makes it land: "in two of these four windows, SPY lost between 19 and 34 percent, and SR-5 was flat to positive." Then immediately add the caveat before anyone can ask for it: "the COVID window is also where our market beta spiked from near zero to about 0.25 — dollar-neutral reduced the damage, it didn't eliminate market exposure."

## Closing: limitations, then a clear ask

End with the limitations from Lesson 13-14 stated as a list, not apologized for, and then a single, concrete closing line that tells the room exactly what you're asking for — more research time, a paper-trading period, or simply feedback. A vague ending ("so, yeah, that's SR-5") wastes the credibility the first nine minutes built.

## Handling Q&A

The questions in a live Q&A are almost always the six questions rehearsed in Lesson 14, sometimes in different words. Three habits keep control of the room: repeat the question back in one sentence before answering, so everyone heard the same question you did; answer the actual question asked before adding context, instead of pivoting immediately to a prepared talking point; and if you don't know something, say so and name what you'd need to find out — a confident "I don't know, here's how I'd check" lands better than a guess that turns out wrong.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Headline result | The single most important number a presentation leads with — here, the net Sharpe of 0.42 |
| Q&A control | Techniques (repeating the question, answering directly, naming unknowns) that keep a presentation's question period focused and credible |

## Recap

A 10-15 minute SR-5 presentation opens with the net Sharpe of 0.42 in the first 90 seconds, spends the most time on risk and the four stress-test windows rather than the headline number, closes with limitations stated plainly and a concrete ask, and handles Q&A by repeating questions back and naming unknowns honestly. This closes Chapter 5 and the SR-5 project itself. Chapter 6 shifts focus entirely: from presenting this one project to preparing for the quant job search and interview process.
