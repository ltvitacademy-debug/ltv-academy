# The Cash Flow Statement

A profitable company, under accrual accounting, can still run out of cash. This sounds contradictory at first, but by now you have every piece needed to understand exactly why — and the cash flow statement exists specifically to answer the question the income statement and balance sheet don't: where did the cash actually go?

## What you'll learn

- Why net income (accrual-based) and cash flow are not the same number
- The three categories every cash flow statement is organized around
- A worked example showing how a profitable company can still have falling cash
- Why this statement matters enormously to lenders

## Why net income isn't cash

Recall accrual accounting from lesson 19: revenue is recorded when earned, expenses when incurred — not when cash moves. A business can report strong net income on its income statement while simultaneously having growing Accounts Receivable (revenue earned, cash not yet collected) and shrinking cash. Net income is an accrual-basis number; the cash flow statement translates that back into "what actually happened to cash."

## The three categories

Every cash flow statement organizes cash movement into three sections:

- **Operating activities**: cash from the core, day-to-day business — collecting from customers, paying suppliers and employees. Usually starts from net income and adjusts for non-cash items (like adding back depreciation, which reduced net income but never used any cash) and changes in working capital accounts (like Accounts Receivable and Accounts Payable).
- **Investing activities**: cash spent on or received from long-term assets — buying equipment, selling a building, making an investment.
- **Financing activities**: cash from or paid to owners and lenders — borrowing money, repaying a loan, issuing stock, paying dividends.

## Worked example

A fictional retailer, **Pemberton Craft Supply Co.**, reports $92,000 of net income (from lesson 25's example), but its Accounts Receivable grew by $30,000 during the year (customers owe more, meaning less cash was actually collected than revenue earned), and it bought $50,000 of new equipment, financed by a $20,000 new loan.

```
Operating Activities
  Net Income                            $92,000
  + Depreciation (non-cash expense)     $18,000
  - Increase in Accounts Receivable    ($30,000)
Net Cash from Operating Activities                  $80,000

Investing Activities
  Purchase of Equipment               ($50,000)
Net Cash from Investing Activities                 ($50,000)

Financing Activities
  Proceeds from New Loan                $20,000
Net Cash from Financing Activities                  $20,000

Net Increase in Cash                                $50,000
```

Notice net income ($92,000) and the actual cash result ($50,000 net increase) are two different numbers, each telling a true but different part of the story.

## Why lenders care about this statement enormously

A bank considering a loan cares less about "is this company profitable on paper" and more about "does this company actually generate enough operating cash to make its loan payments." A company can look profitable on an accrual basis while genuinely struggling with cash — which is exactly the gap the cash flow statement is designed to expose.

## Why this matters for Oracle Fusion

Oracle Fusion Cash Management specifically supports cash positioning and forecasting, and cash flow statement generation draws on the same underlying ledger data as the income statement and balance sheet, reorganized around cash movement rather than accrual-basis recognition. Understanding why this reorganization is even necessary — the accrual/cash gap from lesson 19 — is exactly what makes this report make sense rather than feeling like a redundant fourth version of the same numbers.

## Recap

The cash flow statement reorganizes the same underlying activity into operating, investing, and financing categories, reconciling accrual-basis net income back to the actual change in cash. A company can be profitable and still cash-constrained, which is exactly why lenders study this statement closely.
