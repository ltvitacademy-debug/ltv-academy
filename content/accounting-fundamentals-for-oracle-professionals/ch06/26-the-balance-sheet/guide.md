# The Balance Sheet

If the income statement is a video, the balance sheet is a photograph: a snapshot of exactly what a business owns and owes at one specific instant. It is, quite literally, the accounting equation from lesson 2, formatted as a report.

## What you'll learn

- What the balance sheet reports, and why it's dated differently than the income statement
- Its basic structure, organized by the accounting equation
- How current/noncurrent classifications (Chapters 3) show up here
- Why the balance sheet is called that for a very literal reason

## What the balance sheet reports, and when

The **balance sheet** reports a business's assets, liabilities, and equity **as of a single specific date** — not over a period. This is why a balance sheet is always dated "as of [date]," while an income statement is dated "for the [period] ended [date]." They answer fundamentally different questions: the income statement asks "how did the business perform during this stretch of time?"; the balance sheet asks "what does the business own and owe right now, at this instant?"

## Basic structure

```
                    [Company Name]
                   Balance Sheet
                As of December 31, 2026

ASSETS
  Current Assets
    Cash                            $120,000
    Accounts Receivable              $65,000
    Inventory                        $40,000
  Total Current Assets                          $225,000
  Noncurrent Assets
    Equipment (net of depreciation)              $178,000
Total Assets                                                $403,000

LIABILITIES
  Current Liabilities
    Accounts Payable                 $38,000
    Wages Payable                     $7,000
  Total Current Liabilities                       $45,000
  Noncurrent Liabilities
    Notes Payable (long-term)                     $56,000
Total Liabilities                                            $101,000

EQUITY
  Common Stock                                    $100,000
  Retained Earnings                               $202,000
Total Equity                                                 $302,000

Total Liabilities and Equity                                 $403,000
```

## Why it's called a "balance" sheet, literally

Notice the bottom line: Total Assets ($403,000) exactly equals Total Liabilities and Equity ($101,000 + $302,000 = $403,000). This is the accounting equation from lesson 2, Assets = Liabilities + Equity, directly formatted as a financial report. If a balance sheet doesn't balance, something upstream is definitively wrong — this is the single easiest sanity check anyone can run on any set of financial statements, and it directly echoes the trial balance check from lesson 17.

## Worked example, connecting the whole course together

The Retained Earnings figure of $202,000 isn't invented — it's the output of exactly the roll-forward calculation from lesson 11 and lesson 25: a beginning balance, plus net income from the income statement, minus dividends. Every number on this balance sheet, in fact, traces back through a subledger or adjusting entry (Chapter 4-5), into an account type (Chapter 3), built from a debit and a credit (Chapter 2), governed by the accounting equation (Chapter 1). This single report is the destination of literally everything this course has taught so far.

## Why this matters for Oracle Fusion

Oracle Fusion General Ledger generates the balance sheet directly from current account balances as of the report date, pulling current/noncurrent classifications from how accounts are set up in the chart of accounts. Lenders and investors read this report constantly to judge a company's financial position — its liquidity, its leverage, its overall health at a glance.

## Recap

The balance sheet reports assets, liabilities, and equity as of a single date, with Total Assets always equaling Total Liabilities plus Equity — the accounting equation, formatted as a report. Next up, lesson 27: the cash flow statement, the third core financial statement.
