# Expenses

Expenses close out Chapter 3 and complete the set of five account types. Like revenue, expenses are tied to a specific accounting idea about *when* they should be recorded — and that idea, the matching principle, is one of the most quietly powerful concepts in all of accounting.

## What you'll learn

- The formal definition of an expense
- The matching principle: how expenses connect to the revenue they helped generate
- The difference between an expense and a cash payment
- Common expense accounts, and a preview of why "when" questions keep recurring

## What counts as an expense

An **expense** is the decrease in equity that results from a business's ordinary, core operations — generally, the cost of generating revenue. Rent, salaries, utilities, supplies used up, and advertising are all expenses: each reduces equity because it represents resources consumed in running the business.

## The matching principle

This is the single biggest idea in this lesson: **expenses should be recorded in the same period as the revenue they helped generate**, not necessarily in the period they were paid for. This is called the **matching principle**, and it's why a business selling a $200 product that cost $80 to make should recognize that $80 cost of goods sold *in the same period* as the $200 sale — matching the expense directly to the revenue it produced — regardless of when the $80 was originally spent on materials and labor.

## Expense is not the same as cash paid

Exactly like revenue and cash collected, an expense can be recorded in a different period than when cash is actually paid:

- A company uses electricity in March but doesn't pay the bill until April. The **expense** (utilities) belongs in March — that's when the cost was actually incurred, benefiting March's operations — even though the cash goes out in April.
- A company prepays $12,000 for a full year of insurance in January. Only 1/12th ($1,000) is an expense in January; the rest sits as a **Prepaid Expense asset** until it's used up month by month. We'll return to this exact case in lesson 22.

## Worked example: matching an unpaid bill

A fictional landscaping company, **Fernhollow Grounds Co.**, uses $600 of electricity in March, and the bill doesn't arrive until April.

```
March 31 (expense incurred, not yet paid):
  Debit  Utilities Expense   $600
  Credit Utilities Payable            $600

April 15 (bill paid):
  Debit  Utilities Payable   $600
  Credit Cash                         $600
```

Notice the expense is recorded in March — matched to the period the electricity was actually used — while the cash payment, a separate event, happens in April and creates no new expense.

## Common expense accounts

- **Rent Expense**, **Salaries/Wages Expense**, **Utilities Expense** — straightforward operating costs
- **Cost of Goods Sold** — the direct cost of the specific products a business sold
- **Depreciation Expense** — the gradual expensing of a long-term asset's cost (its own full lesson, 23, is coming in Chapter 5)
- **Interest Expense** — the cost of borrowed money

## Why this matters for Oracle Fusion

The matching principle is exactly why accrual accounting (lesson 19) and adjusting entries (lesson 15) exist as formal processes rather than being optional extras. Oracle Fusion's period-close process is built entirely around making sure every expense that belongs in a period actually gets recorded in that period before the books are closed — which is precisely the discipline this lesson is building toward.

## Recap

An expense is the decrease in equity from a business's core operations, recorded under the matching principle — in the same period as the revenue it helped generate, not necessarily when cash was paid. That completes the five account types: Assets, Liabilities, Equity, Revenue, and Expenses.
