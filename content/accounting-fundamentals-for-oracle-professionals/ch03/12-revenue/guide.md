# Revenue

Revenue is where a business's story usually starts: the money it makes from doing what it does. This lesson defines revenue precisely, because the exact moment revenue should be recorded is subtler than most people expect — subtle enough that it gets its own deeper lesson later in Chapter 5.

## What you'll learn

- The formal definition of revenue
- Why revenue is not the same thing as cash received
- Common revenue accounts across different kinds of businesses
- A first, light preview of why "when to record revenue" is a bigger topic than it sounds

## What counts as revenue

**Revenue** is the increase in equity that results from a business's ordinary, core operating activities — typically, selling goods or providing services. It's recorded when it's **earned**, which is a specific accounting idea we'll define precisely in lesson 20. For now, hold onto this: earning revenue means the business has substantially done what it promised to do (delivered the goods, performed the service), regardless of whether cash has changed hands yet.

## Revenue is not the same as cash received

This is the single most important thing to internalize in this lesson, and it directly connects to lesson 7's "billing a customer on credit" example. If a company performs $2,500 of consulting work for a client who will pay in 30 days, the company has earned $2,500 of revenue **today** — the moment the work was substantially completed — even though it won't see a dollar of cash for a month. The revenue is recorded now; the cash collection is a separate, later event (which simply reduces Accounts Receivable and increases Cash, with no new revenue).

Conversely, cash received does not automatically mean revenue was earned. If a customer pays $12,000 upfront for a full year of a subscription service, the business has *received* $12,000 of cash, but it hasn't yet *earned* all of it — it still owes eleven more months of service. That unearned portion is recorded as a liability (Unearned Revenue / Deferred Revenue), not revenue, until it's actually earned. We'll return to this exact scenario in lesson 22.

## Common revenue accounts

Different businesses label their main revenue account to match what they actually sell:

- A retailer: **Sales Revenue**
- A consulting firm: **Service Revenue** or **Consulting Revenue**
- A landlord: **Rental Revenue**
- A bank: **Interest Revenue**
- Any business with occasional side income: **Other Revenue** (e.g., selling scrap equipment it no longer needs)

Despite different names, they all behave identically for accounting purposes: revenue is credit-normal, and it increases equity.

## Worked example

A fictional design studio, **Palisade Creative Group**, completes a $9,000 branding project for a client on March 28. The client pays on April 15.

```
March 28 (work completed - revenue earned):
  Debit  Accounts Receivable   $9,000
  Credit Service Revenue                $9,000

April 15 (cash collected - no new revenue):
  Debit  Cash                  $9,000
  Credit Accounts Receivable            $9,000
```

Notice the second entry creates zero revenue — it simply converts one asset (a receivable) into another (cash).

## Recap

Revenue is the increase in equity from a business's core operations, recorded when earned — not necessarily when cash is received. Earning revenue and collecting cash are two separate events that often happen at different times. Next up, lesson 13: expenses, the mirror image of revenue.
