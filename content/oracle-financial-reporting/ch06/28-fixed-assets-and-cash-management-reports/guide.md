# Fixed Assets and Cash Management Reports

Two more modules, two more sets of reports a consultant is expected to know: Fixed Assets, which tracks what a company owns and how its value declines over time, and Cash Management, which tracks what's actually in the bank and whether the system's record of it agrees with the bank's own record.

## What you'll learn

- The Fixed Assets Register and what it documents
- Depreciation reporting and why it matters for both the Balance Sheet and Income Statement
- Cash Management's bank statement reconciliation reports
- Why these two modules' reports both ultimately feed the Balance Sheet

## The Fixed Assets Register

The **Asset Register** is Fixed Assets' core report, documenting the organization's tangible assets — buildings, equipment, vehicles, computers — along with details like acquisition cost, acquisition date, asset category, and location. It functions as the authoritative, detailed listing behind the Fixed Assets line on the Balance Sheet, the same relationship a Trial Balance has to the GL overall: one formal statement line, backed by a detailed register anyone can drill into when a question comes up about what's actually behind that number.

## Depreciation reporting

Beyond the register itself, Fixed Assets reporting covers **depreciation** — the systematic reduction of an asset's recorded value over its useful life, reflecting wear, obsolescence, or usage. Depreciation reports typically show, for a period or a range of periods, the depreciation expense recognized per asset (or asset category), which matters on two statements at once: it reduces the asset's net book value on the Balance Sheet, and it shows up as a depreciation expense on the Income Statement, reducing Net Income for the period. A consultant reviewing Fixed Assets reports during close is typically confirming that depreciation actually ran for the period, ran for the correct assets, and posted the expected amounts to both statements.

## Cash Management: reconciling the system's record to the bank's record

Cash Management's reporting job is different in character: rather than tracking ownership or value decline, it's about confirming that Oracle Fusion's recorded cash position actually matches what the bank itself reports. The core activity is **bank statement reconciliation** — comparing the transactions Oracle Fusion has recorded against a bank's own statement, checking off matching items, and surfacing anything that doesn't match (a check that hasn't cleared yet, a bank fee the system doesn't know about, a timing difference between when something was recorded internally and when the bank processed it).

Cash Management reports typically show the reconciliation status: which transactions have cleared and matched, which are still outstanding, and the resulting reconciled cash balance — the number that should tie to the Cash line on the Balance Sheet once reconciliation is complete.

## Why both modules' reports ultimately feed the Balance Sheet

Fixed Assets reports support the Fixed Assets line (net of accumulated depreciation); Cash Management reports support the Cash line. Both are examples of the same pattern seen throughout this chapter: a summarized line on a formal financial statement is only as trustworthy as the detailed, module-specific reporting that supports it, and a consultant's real job during close is often less about producing the formal statement and more about confirming each supporting module's reports tie out cleanly first.

## Recap

Fixed Assets reporting centers on the Asset Register and depreciation reports, which support the Balance Sheet's asset line and the Income Statement's depreciation expense; Cash Management reporting centers on bank statement reconciliation, which supports the Balance Sheet's cash line.
