# Cash Positioning

Everything so far has been about getting the past right — matching what already happened against the bank's record of it. Cash Positioning looks at today and the very near future: how much cash does the company actually have, account by account, right now?

## What you'll learn

- What a cash position actually shows
- What inputs build it
- What decisions a cash position supports
- How it differs from Cash Forecasting (previewed ahead of Lesson 18)

## What a cash position shows

A **cash position** is a bank account-by-account view of beginning balance, inflows, outflows, and a resulting projected balance, typically for today or the next several days. It answers a narrow but critical question: as of right now, how much usable cash sits in each account, and how is that about to change in the very near term? Unlike a forecast, which can run months out, a cash position is deliberately short-horizon and built from real, mostly-already-known activity rather than long-range projection.

## What builds a cash position

The position is built from:

- **The bank's own reported balance** — the opening and closing balances from the most recent bank statement.
- **Reconciled transactions** — activity already confirmed against the bank.
- **Transactions still open but expected imminently** — a payment run scheduled for today, a receipt expected to clear tomorrow.

Because it leans on real statement balances plus known near-term activity, a cash position is only as reliable as the reconciliation work from Chapter 3 behind it — an account with a pile of stale unreconciled items will show a cash position that doesn't actually reflect reality.

## What decisions it supports

A treasury team uses the cash position to make same-day or same-week decisions: does this account have a surplus worth sweeping into an interest-bearing investment? Does that account need a transfer in before Thursday's payroll run clears? Is the company, in aggregate across all its accounts, actually cash-positive or cash-short this week? These are exactly the decisions a bank account transfer (Lesson 16) is often used to execute.

## Cash Positioning versus Cash Forecasting, briefly

Cash Positioning is short-horizon and transaction-grounded — today, this week, mostly known activity. Cash Forecasting (Lesson 18) looks further out and leans more on projection — open orders, historical trends, multiple future periods. Think of positioning as "what do I actually have," and forecasting as "what should I expect."

## A worked example

Harborview Metals Inc.'s Treasury team reviews Monday morning's cash position: the operating account shows a $220,000 opening balance, $340,000 in expected inflows (reconciled receipts plus Tuesday's known lockbox deposit), and $410,000 in expected outflows (a supplier payment run scheduled for Wednesday), projecting to $150,000 by end of week — comfortably above the $100,000 minimum balance the company likes to maintain, so no transfer is needed this week.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cash position | A short-horizon, account-by-account view of balance plus near-term in/outflows |
| Minimum balance | A target floor a company wants an account to stay above |

## Recap

Cash Positioning turns reconciled, real, near-term activity into a clear account-by-account picture of available cash, supporting same-day and same-week treasury decisions like transfers and investments. Next up, lesson 18: Cash Forecasting, which takes a longer view.
