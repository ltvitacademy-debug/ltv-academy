# Lesson 20 — Knowledge Article Feedback & Retirement

## The real feedback mechanism: Helpful / Not Helpful

Every Published article a user reads carries a simple feedback control: **Helpful** or **Not
Helpful**. Each response is tracked against the article, building up a real, visible signal —
not a guess, not an anecdote from one annoyed caller, but an aggregated rating a Knowledge manager
can actually see. An article with a strong Helpful ratio is doing its job. An article trending
toward Not Helpful is telling the Knowledge manager something specific: this content, as written,
is not solving the problem it claims to solve.

## Turning feedback into a revision queue

Feedback is only useful if someone acts on it. A Knowledge manager implementing this well treats
a falling Helpful ratio as a trigger to pull that article back into **Draft** for revision — the
same Draft state Lesson 19 covered, re-entered deliberately rather than through a rejection. The
article then goes back through Review and the approval gate before it's Published again. Feedback
is the input that tells a Knowledge manager *which* articles deserve that attention first, out of
what might be hundreds sitting in a Knowledge Base.

## Why retiring, not deleting, matters

When an article is genuinely outdated — the process it describes no longer exists, the tool it
references has been replaced — the correct implementation move is **Retired**, a distinct
Workflow state, not deletion. Retiring removes the article from active search results, so end
users don't stumble onto stale guidance. Deleting the record would do that too, but at a real
cost: it destroys the article's entire history — who wrote it, when it was reviewed, what feedback
it accumulated, what it actually said at any point in its life. That history matters for audit,
for compliance, and simply for answering "what did we used to tell people about this?" months
after the fact. Retired keeps the record intact and out of the way; deletion keeps nothing.

## A periodic review cadence

A well-implemented Knowledge Base doesn't wait for Not Helpful votes to pile up before anyone
looks at an article again. A real cadence — commonly a scheduled job checking each article's last
updated date — flags any article that has gone **12 months or more without a review** for a
Knowledge manager to look at, whether or not it has collected negative feedback yet. Content that
nobody has touched in a year is content nobody has verified is still accurate.

## The tie back to Known Error articles

Problem Management's Known Error Database, covered in Chapter 2, generates its own articles —
published guidance describing a known problem and its workaround, stored as `kb_knowledge` records
like any other. Those articles follow this exact same lifecycle: Draft, Review, Published, and
eventually Retired once the underlying problem is permanently fixed and the workaround is no
longer needed. A Known Error article that outlives its usefulness and never gets retired is exactly
the kind of stale content a 12-month review cadence is built to catch.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Helpful / Not Helpful | The real feedback control on a Published article, aggregated into a visible rating |
| Retired | A distinct Workflow state that removes an article from active search while preserving its history |
| Audit trail | The article's preserved record history — author, review, and feedback — lost on deletion but kept on retirement |
| Review cadence | A scheduled check (e.g. 12+ months since last update) that flags stale articles for review |
| Known Error article | A `kb_knowledge` record generated by Problem Management, following this same lifecycle |
