## Nine pages that changed everything

On October 31, 2008, someone writing under the name **Satoshi Nakamoto**
published a whitepaper titled *"Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash
System."* Spanning just nine pages, it laid out a real, working blueprint
for transferring value digitally — covering cryptographic hashing, block
architecture, and the timing mechanisms that would sustain the whole
system.

![A real online article discussing the whitepaper's proof-of-work innovation, quoting the actual mechanism directly.](/courses/blockchain/ch03/04-bitcoin-whitepaper-genesis-block/shot_1.png)
*"Once the CPU effort has been expended to make it satisfy the proof-of-work, the block cannot be changed without redoing the work."*

## What was actually new here

Proof-of-work itself wasn't Satoshi's invention — it built on **Adam
Back's Hashcash**, which already existed. What was genuinely novel was
**applying it to solve the double-spending problem in a fully decentralized
currency**, ensuring every transaction gets verified through network-wide
consensus without needing a central authority at all. That's the real leap
from Lesson 3's precursors to something that actually worked at scale.

## The genesis block: block zero

On January 9, 2009, Nakamoto released version 0.1 of the Bitcoin software
and defined the **genesis block — block number zero** — with a reward of
**50 bitcoins** embedded directly in its Coinbase transaction (Chapter 3,
Lesson 1 covered exactly what a Coinbase transaction is).

Embedded in that very first block's text: a headline from The Times,
dated January 3, 2009 — *"Chancellor on brink of second bailout for
banks."* This is genuinely interpreted two ways at once: as a timestamp
proving the block wasn't created earlier, and as a pointed comment on the
instability of traditional, fractional-reserve banking — the exact system
Bitcoin was built to offer an alternative to.

## The mystery that's never been solved

Nakamoto was active in Bitcoin's development until December 2010, then
disappeared. Real, documented details fuel the ongoing mystery:

- Nakamoto is estimated to hold between 750,000 and 1.1 million Bitcoin —
  worth roughly $73 billion at Bitcoin's late-2021 peak.
- Forum-posting timestamps showed almost no activity between roughly
  midnight and 6 AM Eastern time — an unusual pattern for someone claiming
  to live in Japan.
- Writing style (British spellings like "grey" and "colour," phrases like
  "bloody hard") suggested possible Commonwealth origins.
- Multiple developers who reviewed the original code suspected it was too
  well-designed to be the work of one person alone.

Nobody has definitively proven who Nakamoto actually is — and the identity
remains genuinely unresolved to this day.
