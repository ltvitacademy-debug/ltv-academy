## The real problem every precursor was chasing

In the digital world, any file, data, or code can be copied as easily as
copy-and-paste. That's fine for a term paper — it's a real problem for
money. This is the **double-spending problem**: what actually stops someone
from spending the same digital currency twice? Every precursor covered in
this lesson was a genuine attempt to solve it before Bitcoin existed.

## Bit Gold: the direct precursor

It was **Nick Szabo** who tackled making digital data as unique and finite
as gold — hence the name, **Bit Gold**. In Bit Gold, solved computational
puzzles were sent to a public registry and assigned to the solver's public
key. Each solution became part of the *next* challenge, creating a growing
chain of new property — a real mechanism for the network to verify and
timestamp new coins.

Bit Gold is genuinely referred to as a **direct precursor to the Bitcoin
architecture** — you can already see the shape of "chain" and "public key
ownership" in it, years before Bitcoin's own whitepaper.

## e-gold: real success, real failure

**e-gold**, created by Douglas Jackson, was arguably the most successful
alternative currency before Bitcoin. Users sent in physical gold and
received e-gold credit in return — genuinely popular, especially with gold
enthusiasts and early online traders needing to move money across borders.

But e-gold had one real, fatal weakness: **it wasn't decentralized.**
Because a single company controlled it, the U.S. government was able to
shut the whole thing down. That single fact — centralization as a real
point of failure — is precisely the problem Bitcoin's actual design was
built to avoid.

## The cypherpunk movement

The **cypherpunk movement** — the name combining "cipher" (code,
encryption) and "punk" (anti-establishment, do-it-yourself) — advocated for
widespread use of strong cryptography and privacy technology to protect
people's rights and push toward a more open, decentralized society. Born in
the 1980s-90s out of real concerns about government surveillance and
censorship, cypherpunks pushed for tools like public key cryptography and
VPNs specifically to preserve anonymous, secret communication.

This movement's fingerprints are directly on blockchain and
cryptocurrency's development — and on projects like the Electronic Frontier
Foundation, the Tor Project, and **PGP itself** (Lesson 2). Satoshi
Nakamoto is widely considered a cypherpunk, or at minimum someone closely
aligned with the movement's values.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Double-spending problem | The risk that digital currency, being easily copied, could be spent more than once |
| Bit Gold | Nick Szabo's proposal, considered a direct architectural precursor to Bitcoin |
| e-gold | An early, popular, but centralized digital gold currency, shut down by the U.S. government |
| Cypherpunk | A movement advocating strong cryptography and privacy to preserve individual freedom |
