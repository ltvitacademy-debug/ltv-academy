## 2013: the idea

Ethereum began as an idea from Russian-born Canadian programmer **Vitalik
Buterin**: use blockchain technology to build decentralized applications,
not just move money the way Bitcoin does. His introductory whitepaper was
published in late 2013.

![The real CryptoPotato article, "Ethereum's History: From Whitepaper to Hardforks and the ETH Merge," open on screen during class — the actual source the instructor reads from.](/courses/blockchain/ch04/03-history-of-ethereum/shot_history_article.png)
*The real article referenced live in class, tracing Ethereum's history from its whitepaper through its hard forks.*

Three months after the whitepaper, co-founder **Gavin Wood** published the
**yellow paper** — a detailed technical specification of the EVM, miner
fee rewards, and smart contracts.

## Two terms worth knowing before going further

The instructor paused here to define two terms that come up constantly in
software:

- **API (Application Programming Interface)**: a set of rules that let
  different software talk to each other. Think of it like a restaurant
  menu — you don't need to know how the kitchen works, just what you can
  order and what you'll get back.
- **GUI (Graphical User Interface)**: the visual layer of an app — buttons,
  windows, icons, menus — that lets you interact with software using a
  mouse or touchscreen instead of typing raw commands.

## 2014: crowdfunded, not venture-funded

Unlike many blockchain projects funded by traditional investors, Ethereum
raised its capital through a real **initial coin offering (ICO)** that ran
for 42 days, from July 20 to September 2, 2014. The nonprofit **Ethereum
Foundation** (Swiss-based) created 60 million ETH for public sale, and by
the end of the campaign the project had raised over **31,531 BTC — worth
more than $18 million** at the time, making it the fifth most successful
ICO in crypto history up to that point.

## 2015: Ethereum goes live

After a test phase called **Olympic**, Ethereum's mainnet officially
launched on **July 30, 2015** as **Frontier** — its first public release,
aimed at developers. The very first block, the **Genesis block**,
contained 8,893 Ether transactions and paid a block reward of 5 ETH. At
launch, Ethereum ran on **proof of work**, the same consensus mechanism as
Bitcoin.

Shortly after, the **Ice Age** upgrade (September 2015) introduced a
built-in "difficulty bomb" — designed to make mining progressively harder
over time, which would eventually force the network toward a proof-of-stake
transition.

## EIPs: how Ethereum actually changes

An **Ethereum Improvement Proposal (EIP)** is a real, public document
proposing a change to the network. Anyone can write one, and it moves
through a defined process before it's ever adopted:

![The real eips.ethereum.org page, shown live in class, walking through the actual status terms an EIP moves through.](/courses/blockchain/ch04/03-history-of-ethereum/shot_eip_process.png)
*The real EIP process, straight from eips.ethereum.org: Idea → Draft → Review → Last Call → Final.*

| Stage | What it means |
|---|---|
| Idea | Not yet formally tracked |
| Draft | The first tracked stage of development |
| Review | Ready for wider review |
| Last Call | A final review window before finalization |
| Final | Adopted into the Ethereum protocol |

The **Homestead** upgrade (March 2016) was itself an EIP-enabled milestone
— it gave the platform a graphical user interface for the first time,
making Ethereum usable by non-technical people, and formally enabled the
network to run future EIP-based upgrades.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Whitepaper / yellow paper | Ethereum's original concept document (2013) and its technical specification (2014) |
| ICO | Initial coin offering — how Ethereum's own development was actually funded |
| Genesis block | Ethereum's first block, mined at Frontier's July 2015 launch |
| EIP | Ethereum Improvement Proposal — the real process for changing the protocol |
