# Lesson 15 — Case Study Portfolio Review

**Chapter 2 · Working the Cases · Lesson 15 of 16**

## What you'll learn

- How to select which of this course's eight cases actually belongs in a portfolio built for a specific audience
- A checklist for whether a written-up case study is actually portfolio-ready or just a draft

## A portfolio proves a different thing than a résumé does

A résumé states that you've worked on application architecture; a portfolio shows your actual reasoning on a specific problem, which is a fundamentally different kind of evidence. An interviewer or a hiring committee reading "designed CRM architecture for enterprise clients" on a résumé has to take the claim on faith. The same person reading a well-written case study — Harrow's record-type-versus-separate-object decision, reasoned out with the actual tradeoffs named — gets to evaluate the thinking directly, which is exactly why a portfolio carries more weight in a technical hiring process than another line of résumé text ever could.

## Not every case belongs in every portfolio

This course produced eight worked case studies, and a portfolio built for a specific purpose shouldn't necessarily include all eight. Selecting cases is itself a design decision, and it should follow the same discipline Lesson 9 taught for extracting requirements — in this instance, extracting the requirement of "what does this specific audience need to see." A portfolio aimed at a Service Cloud-focused role should foreground the Corvell case management case and the Castellan field service case, not the Veltrix billing case, even though all three are competently reasoned — not because the other cases are weaker, but because they're not what this specific reader needs evidence of. Padding a portfolio with every case produced, regardless of relevance, dilutes the strength of the cases that actually matter for the audience in front of you.

## A portfolio-readiness checklist

A written-up case is portfolio-ready only if it can answer yes to each of these, not just "it's written down somewhere":

- **Is the problem stated in the reader's terms, not just internal jargon?** A reader unfamiliar with Harrow shouldn't need background knowledge to understand what Harrow's three segments actually needed.
- **Does it show at least one real alternative, per Lesson 10, rather than presenting the chosen design as the only option that was ever considered?** A case with no visible alternative reads as a decision with no reasoning behind it.
- **Does it name at least one risk or assumption, per Lesson 11, rather than presenting the design as risk-free?** A portfolio case that claims a design has no downsides reads as less credible, not more, to an experienced reviewer who knows every real design has tradeoffs.
- **Is the outcome or resolution stated, even for a fictional case?** "Here's what we designed and why" is weaker than "here's what we designed, why, and what we learned once it was in use" — even a fictional case can state a plausible follow-up outcome if it's framed honestly as part of the exercise, not fabricated as a real result.

## Presenting a fictional practice case honestly

These eight case studies are explicitly fictional practice scenarios, built to teach the reasoning, not real client engagements — and a portfolio built from this course has to say so plainly. Presenting a practice case as if it were real client work is a credibility risk that, if discovered, costs far more trust than the case study would have earned in the first place; the honest framing ("a practice case study reasoning through X, built to demonstrate Y specific skill") is not a weaker pitch than claiming it's real — reviewers who've done real architecture work can usually tell a thoughtfully-reasoned practice case from a padded résumé line anyway, and the honest framing is what actually earns the credibility a portfolio is supposed to build.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Portfolio | A collection of worked examples demonstrating an architect's actual reasoning, as distinct from résumé claims |
| Audience-driven selection | Choosing which cases to include in a portfolio based on what a specific reader needs to see, not including everything produced |
| Portfolio-readiness checklist | A set of concrete criteria (reader-facing problem statement, visible alternative, named risk/assumption, stated outcome) a written case must meet before inclusion |
| Honest framing | Presenting a fictional practice case explicitly as practice, not implying it was real client work |
