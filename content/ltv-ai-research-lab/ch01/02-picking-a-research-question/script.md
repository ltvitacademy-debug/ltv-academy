# Script — Picking a Research Question

## Segment 1 (title)

Every assignment you've done so far came with the question already written for you. A research project starts one step earlier: you pick the question yourself, before anyone can check your work against an answer key, because there isn't one. This lesson covers what separates a good research question from a vague one.

## Segment 2 (steps)

A course assignment tells you what to build and what correct looks like. A research question asks something you don't already know the answer to, and commits you to finding out with evidence instead of intuition. That changes how you work — you're designing an experiment whose result could genuinely go either way, not following steps to a known destination, and you own the decision about what counts as proof.

## Segment 3 (steps)

A good research question passes three tests. It's specific — "is RL good for databases" is a mood, not a question. It's falsifiable — some possible result has to be able to prove it wrong. And it's scoped to days, not months — small enough to answer this week, big enough to actually teach you something.

## Segment 4 (steps)

The lab's three projects were picked to pass all three tests. Project 1 asks whether an RL agent beats the optimizer's own plan on one query template. Project 2 asks whether a small reward model ranks cleaned records the way a human rater would. Project 3 asks whether RLHF measurably improves execution correctness over SFT alone, without introducing new hallucinations.

## Segment 5 (outro)

Specific, falsifiable, scoped to days — that's the test every question in this lab was built to pass, and it's worth running your own questions through later in your career too. Up next, lesson three: turning a scoped question into an actual time-boxed experiment plan.
