# Script — Supplier Addresses and Business Classifications

## Segment 1 (title)

Last lesson mentioned addresses and business classifications in passing. Both deserve more than that: addresses are easy to misread because of how the "name" field works, and classifications drive real compliance obligations, not just record-keeping.

## Segment 2 (steps)

When you create an address, the Address Name is a label you choose, not the physical address itself - that's entered separately as street, city, state, and postal code. A supplier address also carries purpose flags: valid for ordering, valid for RFQ, or a remit-to address for payments. One address can serve several purposes, or a supplier might split them across addresses entirely, especially when finance sits in a different city than operations.

## Segment 3 (steps)

A business classification records a certified attribute relevant to procurement compliance - small business, woman owned, veteran owned, minority owned with several subtypes, HUBZone, and more. Each one carries a certificate number, a certifying agency that issued it, and an expiration date. This isn't a self-reported checkbox, it's meant to reflect a real, verifiable certificate.

## Segment 4 (steps)

That expiration date matters operationally. The system flags a classification that's already expired or coming up on expiration, typically within a 30-day window. For a company reporting supplier diversity spend, or meeting a government contracting goal, an expired certificate can mean that spend silently stops counting, even though the actual purchasing never changed.

## Segment 5 (outro)

Brightfield's fictional supplier, Maple and Co. Printing, uses one address for both ordering and remittance, and carries a Woman Owned classification with eleven months left before it needs attention. Up next, lesson nine: supplier bank accounts, where payments actually get sent.
