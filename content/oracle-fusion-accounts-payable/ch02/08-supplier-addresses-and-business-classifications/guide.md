# Supplier Addresses and Business Classifications

Lesson 7 introduced addresses and business classifications in passing, as two of the tabs that round out a supplier profile. Both deserve more than a passing mention: addresses are easy to misunderstand because of how "address name" is labeled, and business classifications matter for reasons beyond simple record-keeping — they drive real compliance and reporting obligations.

## What you'll learn

- Why an address's "name" is a label, not the physical address
- How one address can serve several purposes (ordering, remittance, RFQ) for the same supplier
- What a business classification is, and why it needs a certifying agency and expiration date
- What happens when a classification certificate is about to expire

## The address name is a label, not the location

When you create a supplier address, the **Address Name** field is an internal identifier you choose — something like "Columbus Distribution" or "Remit-To Georgia" — not the street address itself, which is entered separately (Address Line 1, City, State, Postal Code, Country). This matters because the same physical address can be given a name that describes its *purpose* rather than its location, which is especially useful when a supplier has one office handling several different functions.

## One address, multiple purposes

A supplier address typically carries purpose flags indicating what it's used for — for example, whether it's valid for ordering (a buyer sends POs there), for RFQ purposes (sourcing sends solicitations there), or as a remit-to address (where payments or remittance advice should go). A single address can serve more than one purpose, or a supplier might deliberately use separate addresses for ordering versus remittance — common when a supplier's finance office is in a different city than its operations.

## Business classifications: compliance, not decoration

A **business classification** records a certified attribute of the supplier relevant to procurement compliance and supplier diversity programs — common classifications include Small Business, Woman Owned, Veteran Owned, Service-Disabled Veteran Owned, Minority Owned (with subtypes like African American, Hispanic, Asian, or American Indian owned), HUBZone, LGBTQ+ Owned, and Person with Disability Owned.

Each classification carries its own **certification details**: a certificate number, the **certifying agency** that issued it (a third-party or government body Brightfield's procurement team has set up as an authorized certifier), and an **expiration date**. This is not a self-reported checkbox — it's meant to reflect a real, verifiable certificate.

## Why expiration dates matter operationally

Because classifications expire, the supplier record surfaces an alert when a certificate has already expired or is coming up on expiration (commonly flagged inside a 30-day window on either side). For a company that reports on supplier diversity spend, or participates in government contracting where a percentage of spend must go to certified small or diverse businesses, an expired certificate quietly sitting on a supplier record can mean that spend no longer counts toward the goal it was supposed to support — even though nothing about the actual purchasing changed.

## A worked example

Brightfield's fictional supplier **Maple & Co. Printing** has one address, "Maple HQ," flagged for both ordering and remittance purposes. Its profile also carries a Woman Owned business classification, certified by a (fictional) regional certifying body, with a certificate number on file and an expiration date eleven months out — well outside any expiration warning window for now.

## Recap

An address's name is a chosen label, separate from the physical address it points to, and one address can serve multiple purposes like ordering or remittance. Business classifications are verifiable certifications — not self-reported claims — each with a certifying agency and an expiration date that the system actively monitors.
