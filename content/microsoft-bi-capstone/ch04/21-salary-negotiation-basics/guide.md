# Lesson 21 — Salary Negotiation Basics

**Chapter 4 · Job Preparation · Lesson 21 of 25**

## What you'll learn

- Why you research a realistic salary **range** before you ever get to
  an offer, not after
- Why you avoid giving a number first whenever you can, and how to
  answer "what are your salary expectations" without doing that
- The basic, respectful mechanics of countering an offer for a first
  BI developer role — no aggressive tactics, no ultimatums
- Why the number on the offer letter isn't the whole picture

## Research a range before the offer stage

Salary negotiation starts weeks before anyone extends an offer. Before
you're deep into any interview process, find a realistic range for
"BI Developer" (or "SQL Developer," "Reporting Analyst," "Power BI
Developer" — titles vary) in your actual metro area, using at least
two independent sources:

| Source type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Aggregated salary sites | Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, Payscale, LinkedIn Salary |
| Government labor data | Bureau of Labor Statistics occupational wage data |
| Direct market signal | Live job postings in your area that actually list a range |

You're not looking for one exact number — you're building a range,
because that's what real offers fall into. A first BI developer role
in a mid-size market might realistically land in the mid-to-high
$50,000s through the low $70,000s; a large metro or a company with a
mature data team can run meaningfully higher. Adjust for your specific
market rather than trusting a single national average.

## Never give a number first if you can avoid it

Whoever names a number first gives away information. If you say a
number that's lower than what the company had budgeted, you've just
capped your own offer — they were never going to volunteer to pay you
more than you asked for. If you say a number that's too high before
they've seen your resume, you risk getting screened out before you get
a chance to make your case. The safer move, in almost every case, is
to let the employer name a number first, or to answer with a range
instead of a figure.

## Answering "what are your salary expectations"

This question shows up early, often on a first screening call, before
you have much leverage. A few ways to answer it without boxing
yourself in:

- **Redirect first, if it feels natural:** "I'd love to learn more
  about the role and the budgeted range before I give a number — what
  range did you have in mind?"
- **If pushed, answer with your researched range, not a single
  number:** "Based on my research for this role in this market, I'm
  targeting something in the $X to $Y range."
- **Anchor to your research, not a guess.** The range you answer with
  should be the one you built in the research step above, not a
  number you're inventing on the spot.

Both of these keep you from committing to a number that turns out to
be lower than what they'd actually have paid.

## Countering an offer respectfully

Once an offer arrives, you have real leverage you didn't have during
the interview — they've decided they want you. A respectful counter
has a few consistent parts: thank them for the offer, state your
researched range (not a demand), give a specific reason grounded in
market data or the value of the role, and stay collaborative rather
than issuing an ultimatum. You are not threatening to walk; you're
asking whether there's room to move.

```
Thank you for the offer — I'm excited about this role.

Based on my research, similar BI Developer roles here
run closer to $68,000-$72,000 base.

Is there room to move the base toward that range,
or adjust the sign-on bonus to bridge the gap?

I'm ready to move forward once we're aligned.
```

Notice what this script doesn't do: it doesn't threaten another offer
you don't have, it doesn't demand an exact figure, and it doesn't
apologize for asking. It's a request for room to move, made once, in
writing or on a call, and it leaves the door open either way.

## The offer is more than the base number

A base salary number is the headline, but it isn't the whole offer.
Before you accept or counter, look at the full package: signing bonus,
annual bonus target, PTO, remote or hybrid flexibility, the timeline
to your first performance review (and raise), and any tuition or
certification reimbursement. A slightly lower base with strong PTO and
a fast review cycle can be worth more than a slightly higher base
without either — evaluate the whole offer, not just the first number
on the page.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Range (salary) | A realistic low-to-high band for a role in a specific market, built from research — the target of a negotiation, not a single number |
| Anchoring | The effect where the first number named in a negotiation pulls the rest of the conversation toward it — a reason to avoid naming a number first |
| Total compensation | The full value of an offer, including base salary, bonuses, and benefits — not just the base salary figure |
| Counter-offer | A respectful, specific request to adjust an offer, grounded in research rather than a demand |
