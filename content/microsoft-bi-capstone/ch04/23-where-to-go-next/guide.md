## You finished a real, complete thing

Before looking ahead, it's worth being clear about what you just did.
This program took you from writing your first `SELECT` statement
through designing a dimensional warehouse, building an SSIS ETL
package, publishing an SSRS paginated report, and shipping a Power BI
dashboard — all against one real business scenario, manufacturing
work orders, built the way an actual BI developer job would ask for
it. That's not a tutorial you followed. It's a finished, defensible
piece of work, and it's the foundation for everything in this lesson.

Nothing here is required. Plenty of BI developers spend years doing
exactly what this program prepared you for, without ever needing a
"next track." This lesson is for when you're curious what further
growth can look like — not a signal that you're not done yet.

## Three honest directions

Most BI developers who keep growing pick one of three general
directions, and they're not mutually exclusive:

| Direction | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Go deeper** | Become the person on your team who really knows Power BI, SSRS, or SQL Server — advanced DAX, complex report design, query performance tuning, at a depth a generalist doesn't reach |
| **Branch out** | Move toward an adjacent data role that uses a lot of what you already know, plus new tools — data engineering, data analytics, or database administration |
| **Certify** | Pursue a vendor certification that puts a credential behind skills you already have, which can matter for resume screening even when the material itself isn't new to you |

Which one fits depends less on what's "better" and more on what you
notice yourself gravitating toward once you're actually doing the
work day to day — which is exactly why this decision doesn't need to
be made before you have a job.

## Adjacent data roles

If branching out is the direction that appeals to you, here's what
each adjacent role generally builds on top of what you already have:

| Role | Builds on | New territory |
|---|---|---|
| **Data engineer** | Your ETL experience from SSIS, your comfort with SQL Server | Cloud-based pipeline tools, larger-scale data processing, often Python or Spark |
| **Data analyst** | Your reporting and dashboarding experience from SSRS and Power BI | Deeper statistics, forecasting, and business-facing analysis rather than pipeline-building |
| **Database administrator** | Your SQL Server fundamentals | Backups, performance tuning, security, and uptime — the operational side of the database rather than the reporting side |

Each of these is a reasonable path from where you're standing. None of
them requires starting over — they extend specific pieces of what you
already built in this capstone rather than replacing them.

## Realistic certification paths

If a credential is the direction that appeals to you, a few categories
are worth knowing about, without committing to a specific one yet:

- **Microsoft certifications tied to the tools you now use** — Microsoft
  publishes role-based certifications for Power BI and for data
  platform work on Azure, and having hands-on project experience
  first (which you now have) makes studying for one of these far more
  concrete than studying cold.
- **Vendor-neutral data certifications** — some certifications focus on
  SQL and data fundamentals generally, rather than one vendor's stack,
  which can be useful if you're not yet sure which direction you're
  headed.
- **Cloud platform certifications** — if branching into data
  engineering appeals to you, a foundational certification from a
  major cloud provider is often the first concrete step in that
  direction, once you've started working with cloud-based tools.

Certifications are worth pursuing when they support a direction you're
already leaning toward, not as a substitute for the hands-on project
work you've already done in this program.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Specialization | Deepening skill in one tool or area you already work in, rather than adding a new one |
| Adjacent role | A role that shares a meaningful amount of skill overlap with your current one, but adds new territory — data engineer and data analyst relative to BI developer, for example |
| Certification path | A sequence of vendor or vendor-neutral credentials that support a specific direction, built on top of real hands-on experience rather than in place of it |
