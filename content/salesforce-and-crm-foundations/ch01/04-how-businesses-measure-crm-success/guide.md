# Lesson 4 — How Businesses Measure CRM Success

**Chapter 1 · CRM Fundamentals · Lesson 4 of 20**

## What you'll learn

- Why "we have a CRM now" isn't the same thing as "CRM is working"
- The core metrics that come out of the data from Lesson 3
- How marketing, sales, and service each measure success differently
- Why bad data quietly breaks every one of these numbers

## Having the software isn't the goal

A business can buy CRM licenses for every employee and still get
nothing out of it, if the data inside is thin, stale, or wrong. The
real measure of success isn't "did we adopt a CRM" — it's whether the
numbers that come out of it are ones leadership can actually trust and
act on. Those numbers map directly onto the lifecycle from Lesson 2.

## Marketing's numbers

Marketing is judged on how well it fills the top of the funnel with
leads that are actually worth sales' time:

- **Lead volume** — how many leads came in, by source
- **Conversion rate** — what share of leads get qualified and converted (Lesson 3)
- **Cost per lead** — what it took in ad spend or campaign cost to generate one

A high lead volume with a terrible conversion rate usually means
marketing is attracting the wrong people, not that sales is failing.

## Sales' numbers

Sales is judged on turning qualified interest into revenue:

- **Win rate** — what share of opportunities close as won, not lost
- **Average deal size** — the typical dollar value of a closed-won opportunity
- **Sales cycle length** — how long an opportunity takes, from created to closed
- **Pipeline** — the total value of open opportunities, weighted by how likely they are to close

Pipeline is the number leadership watches most closely, because it's
the forward-looking answer to "how much revenue is coming" — and it
only exists because every opportunity was tracked consistently in one
system, not guessed at in a meeting.

## Service's numbers

Service is judged on whether existing customers stay satisfied and
stay customers:

- **Case volume** — how many support cases come in, and about what
- **Resolution time** — how long a case stays open before it's closed
- **Customer satisfaction (CSAT)** — direct feedback after a case closes
- **Retention / churn rate** — what share of customers renew versus leave

A business can win plenty of new deals and still shrink, if churn
quietly outpaces new sales — which is exactly why service metrics
matter as much as sales ones, not less.

## The number that undermines all the others: data quality

Every metric above is only as good as the data behind it. Duplicate
accounts split a single customer's history into two incomplete
pictures. Opportunities left sitting in a stale stage make win rate
and pipeline both meaningless. Leads nobody bothered to log don't
exist for the purposes of a conversion-rate calculation, even though
they were real. This is why CRM adoption — reps and agents actually
entering and updating data — is treated as its own success metric, not
an assumed baseline.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Conversion rate | The share of leads that get qualified and converted into real opportunities |
| Win rate | The share of opportunities that close as won rather than lost |
| Pipeline | The total value of open opportunities, often weighted by likelihood to close |
| Churn rate | The share of customers who leave or don't renew |
| CSAT | Customer satisfaction, typically measured right after a service interaction |
