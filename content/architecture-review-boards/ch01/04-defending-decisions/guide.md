# Lesson 4 — Defending Decisions

**Chapter 1 · The Review Board · Lesson 4 of 14**

## What you'll learn

- The difference between defending a decision and defending your ego
- Why tying every decision back to a stated requirement is the single strongest defense you can build in advance
- How to explain "the road not taken" without sounding defensive
- What a genuinely good defense sounds like versus a rehearsed-sounding one
- Why conceding a point well is itself a defensive skill, not a failure

## Defending the decision, not yourself

"Defending" a decision under board questioning doesn't mean treating every question as an attack to repel. The moment a presenter starts defending their own competence instead of the design's logic, the quality of the actual conversation drops — reviewers can feel the difference between someone explaining their reasoning and someone protecting their pride, and the second reads as weaker, not stronger, even when the underlying design is fine. The useful reframe: a question about your design is a request for the reasoning behind it, not a verdict on you. Answer the reasoning.

## Tie every decision back to a requirement

The single most effective thing you can do, almost entirely in advance rather than live, is make sure every significant decision in your design traces back to a specific requirement or constraint you stated earlier. This matters because most hostile-sounding questions in a review are really asking one of two things: "why this and not the obvious alternative," or "what requirement actually demanded this." If you've already connected the decision to a requirement during your presentation (Lesson 3's deep-dive section exists partly for this reason), the defense of that decision under questioning is just restating a connection the board already heard — not building a new argument live, under pressure, for the first time. Decisions made because they were "best practice" in the abstract, with no specific requirement behind them, are the hardest ones to defend, because there's nothing concrete to point back to.

## Explaining the road not taken

A reviewer will often ask about an alternative you didn't choose — not necessarily because they think you're wrong, but because they want to hear you reason about tradeoffs rather than just defend the one path you picked. The strongest answer names the alternative accurately (showing you actually considered it, not that you're inventing a strawman now), states the specific reason it didn't fit this scenario's constraints, and avoids implying the alternative was a bad idea in general — it just wasn't the right fit here. "We considered X; it would have been the better choice if [condition], but given [this project's actual constraint], Y served the requirement better" reads as architectural judgment. Dismissing the alternative outright ("X is just worse") reads as someone who didn't seriously evaluate it.

## What a good defense actually sounds like

A defense that holds up under real scrutiny shares a few traits: it's specific (naming the exact requirement, number, or constraint, not a vague appeal to "best practice"), it's calm (answering the question asked, not a longer speech that drifts past it), and it acknowledges scope honestly (if a question exposes a genuine gap, saying so is stronger than inventing a justification on the spot). A rehearsed-sounding defense — reciting the same phrase regardless of how the question is actually asked — tends to unravel the moment a reviewer rephrases the question or asks a follow-up that the memorized answer doesn't cover, because it was never built from the reasoning, only from the words.

## Conceding well is a defensive skill

Not every question has a defense, and treating every challenge as something to win is itself a mistake a sharp board will notice. If a reviewer surfaces a genuine gap — a scenario your design doesn't handle, a tradeoff you hadn't fully considered — the strongest response names it honestly, states how significant you think it actually is, and, where possible, sketches how you'd adjust the design to address it. This is covered in depth in Lesson 9 (Recovering From a Mistake), but the core idea belongs here too: a candidate or architect who can say "that's a real gap, here's how I'd close it" under live questioning demonstrates exactly the judgment a board is trying to evaluate — which is often worth more than a design that happened to have no gaps at all.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Requirement traceability (in defense) | Connecting a specific design decision back to the stated requirement that justified it, so a challenge has a concrete anchor to answer against |
| Road not taken | An alternative design approach the presenter considered and didn't choose, which a reviewer may probe to test the depth of the presenter's reasoning |
| Rehearsed-sounding defense | An answer built from memorized phrasing rather than underlying reasoning, which tends to break down under a rephrased or follow-up question |
| Conceding well | Honestly acknowledging a genuine gap a reviewer surfaces, rather than manufacturing a justification to avoid admitting it |
