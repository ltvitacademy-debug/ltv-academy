# Lesson 11 — Review Board Simulation

**Chapter 3 · Practice · Lesson 11 of 14**

## What you'll learn

- How to run a complete, self-contained mock review board end to end
- A sample business scenario sized to actually practice the full skill set from Chapters 1 and 2
- How to structure a solo or small-group simulation when you don't have access to a real panel
- What to capture during the simulation so it's actually useful afterward, not just a one-off exercise
- How this lesson's simulation sets up the specialized-focus simulations in Lessons 12 and 13

## Why a full simulation, now

Chapters 1 and 2 broke the review-board skill set into individual pieces: presenting, structuring, defending, handling ambiguity, whiteboarding, managing time, recovering from mistakes, reading the room. Each of those is easier to practice in isolation than all at once, which is exactly why they were taught separately — but a real review board doesn't present them one at a time. It demands all of them simultaneously, under a single clock, in front of people who weren't told which skill you're currently working on. This lesson is the first point in the course where you put the pieces together.

## A sample scenario sized for full practice

Use this scenario, or adapt your own to a similar scope: *A regional healthcare provider is consolidating patient-intake and billing, currently handled by three disconnected systems (a legacy intake database, a separate billing platform, and a spreadsheet-based insurance-verification process run manually by staff), into a single new platform. The new system must integrate with an external insurance-eligibility verification API, must comply with healthcare data-privacy requirements for any patient information it stores or transmits, must support roughly 200 intake staff across 12 locations, and must be deliverable in two phases over nine months given a fixed budget.* This scenario is deliberately sized to force real decisions across data model, integration, security, and phased delivery — the same breadth a real board will probe.

## Running the simulation solo or in a small group

If you have a study partner or small group, assign roles explicitly: one presenter, one or more "panelists" who each take a specific evaluation lens (one plays a security-focused reviewer, one plays an integration-focused reviewer, one plays a generalist pushing on business value), and set a real clock. If you're practicing solo, the structure still works: design the solution under a real time limit, record yourself presenting it out loud (even to an empty room), and separately write out the five hardest questions you can imagine a panel asking about your own design — then answer them out loud, on camera or recording, as if they'd just been asked live. Talking to yourself about your own design feels artificial at first; it is still measurably more useful than only ever rehearsing silently in your head, because it forces the actual verbal and sequencing skills from Chapter 2, not just the thinking.

## What to capture during the run

A simulation that isn't reviewed afterward is just a rehearsal, not a practice cycle. During or immediately after the run, capture: how your actual time budget compared to your plan (Lesson 8), which assumptions you stated out loud versus realized afterward you'd left unstated (Lesson 6), any moment you had to recover from a mistake and how it went (Lesson 9), and the one or two questions that were hardest to answer well — those are your highest-value material for the next simulation, not a sign the exercise failed.

## Setting up the specialized simulations ahead

This lesson's scenario and process are general-purpose, covering a representative spread of domains without concentrating on any one of them. Lessons 12 and 13 reuse this same simulation structure but deliberately load the questioning toward one specific domain each — security in Lesson 12, integration in Lesson 13 — because real boards sometimes do exactly that when a panelist's specialty is especially relevant to the scenario, or when an earlier answer reveals a specific area worth probing harder. Running the general version first gives you a baseline to compare against once the questioning gets deliberately lopsided.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mock review board | A self-run or small-group simulation of a full review-board session, used to practice the complete skill set under realistic pressure |
| Evaluation lens | A specific domain focus (security, integration, business value) one simulated panelist is assigned to push on |
| Practice cycle | Running a simulation, capturing what was hard or revealing, and feeding that back into the next simulation, as distinct from a one-off rehearsal |

## Lab

Run this lesson's healthcare-consolidation scenario as a full simulation, solo or with a partner, under a real time limit of your choosing (30-45 minutes is reasonable for a first attempt: design time, a presentation, and a Q&A segment). Afterward, write a short debrief covering: your time-budget accuracy, one assumption you realize you left unstated, one moment (real or anticipated) where you'd need to recover from a mistake, and the single hardest question from the exercise.
