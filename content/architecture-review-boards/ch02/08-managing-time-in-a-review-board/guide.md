# Lesson 8 — Managing Time in a Review Board

**Chapter 2 · Performing Under Pressure · Lesson 8 of 14**

## What you'll learn

- Why time mismanagement is one of the most avoidable, and most common, ways a sound design still underperforms in review
- How to split your attention between content and the clock without either one suffering
- A concrete triage method for when you realize, partway through, that you're running over
- Why rushing your strongest material is worse than cutting your weakest material
- How Salesforce's own CTA review board structure treats saved presentation time as a direct asset for the Q&A phase

## Time mismanagement is avoidable, and common

Of every way a presentation underperforms relative to the actual quality of the design behind it, running out of time badly is one of the most avoidable — because unlike design quality, it's almost entirely a matter of planning and rehearsal, not technical skill. A candidate who spends eighteen of twenty minutes on context and high-level architecture, then has two minutes left for every load-bearing decision, hasn't failed at architecture. They've failed at pacing, and a board evaluating the finished product won't always separate the two cleanly in the impression it leaves.

## Splitting attention between content and the clock

The goal isn't staring at a clock throughout your presentation — that pulls focus from the content and reads as anxious. The goal is building enough awareness of your time budget (from Lesson 3) that you have a rough sense of where you should be at a few natural checkpoints: roughly a third of the way through, you should be transitioning out of context into the architecture itself; roughly two-thirds through, you should be finishing your load-bearing deep dives and turning to tradeoffs. Checking the clock at these few deliberate points, rather than continuously, keeps you aware without making the awareness itself a distraction.

## Triage when you're running over

Realizing partway through that you're behind schedule is common, even with good preparation — a question or a tangent can eat more time than planned. The useful triage question at that point isn't "how do I speed up," it's "what's the highest-value content for the time I actually have left." Skipping a planned example while keeping the point it illustrates, compressing a tradeoff discussion to its headline instead of its full reasoning, or cutting a planned section entirely rather than rushing through all of them equally, are all better choices than trying to deliver every planned section at double speed. A board can tell the difference between a presenter who made a deliberate choice to cut something and one who just ran out of time mid-sentence.

## Don't rush your strongest material to save your weakest

A common and backwards instinct under time pressure is to speed through everything proportionally, which means your most important, most load-bearing content gets the same compressed treatment as your least important supporting detail. The better instinct is the opposite: protect the time budget for your two or three load-bearing decisions (the ones Lesson 3 said deserve real depth) even if that means cutting a less important section entirely. A board that sees you make that call deliberately — "I'm going to skip the rollout-phasing detail to make sure I have time to properly walk through the data model decision" — reads it as good judgment under pressure, which is itself part of what's being evaluated.

## The CTA board: saved time becomes Q&A time

Training sources on Salesforce's CTA review board describe the presentation phase as having a fixed window, with any time saved during the presentation rolling over and being added to the Q&A phase rather than simply being lost. This creates a specific incentive structure worth understanding even outside the CTA context: an efficient, well-paced presentation doesn't just avoid running over — it can actively buy you more time for the part of the session (sustained, direct questioning) where a well-prepared candidate often performs strongest relative to the clock. Treating your presentation time as something to spend efficiently, not just avoid exceeding, is a subtly different and more useful framing.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Time checkpoint | A planned point partway through a presentation where you check progress against your time budget without continuous clock-watching |
| Triage (under time pressure) | Deciding what to cut or compress when running behind, prioritized by value rather than cutting everything proportionally |
| Deliberate cut | Explicitly naming a section you're skipping to protect time for higher-value content, as opposed to silently running out of time |
