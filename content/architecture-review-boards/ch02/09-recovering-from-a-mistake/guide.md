# Lesson 9 — Recovering From a Mistake

## Mistakes happen; recovery is a separate, evaluated skill

Every candidate and architect misstates something under live pressure eventually — says a number that contradicts an earlier slide, draws a connection on the whiteboard that doesn't match what they said five minutes ago, or realizes mid-answer that an assumption they're relying on is actually wrong. This is normal, expected, and not in itself disqualifying. What a board is actually watching, separately from the mistake, is how you handle discovering it. A calm, clean correction often costs you less credibility than the original mistake did — while a mistake left to compound, or badly covered up, costs far more than the mistake itself ever would have.

## Three different kinds of mistakes, three different recoveries

Not every mistake calls for the same response:

- **A factual error** (a wrong number, a misremembered detail) calls for a short, direct correction: state the correct version, briefly, and move on. Dwelling on it or over-apologizing draws more attention to it than the original error did.
- **A design inconsistency** (something you said in the deep dive contradicts something you said in the high-level overview) calls for actually resolving the contradiction, not just acknowledging it exists: say which version is correct and briefly why, so the board isn't left holding two conflicting claims about your own design.
- **A misstatement under pressure** (saying something in the heat of a rapid Q&A exchange that doesn't actually reflect your real position) calls for a clean retraction: "Let me correct that — what I actually meant was..." stated plainly, without an extended justification for why the misstatement happened in the first place.

## A short, calm correction pattern

Across all three cases, the pattern that reads best is short: name the correction, state the accurate version, and continue — without an extended apology, without re-explaining the entire context that led to the error, and without visibly dwelling on it. "Correction — that connection should go through the integration layer, not direct to the data store; the rest of the flow I described still holds" is a clean fix. A long, anxious apology followed by re-litigating how the mistake happened draws far more attention to it, and eats time you don't have, for no benefit the board actually values.

## Don't bet on it going unnoticed

A natural impulse when you notice your own mistake mid-presentation is to hope no one else caught it and quietly move on without correcting it. This is almost always the wrong bet with an experienced panel: reviewers are listening carefully precisely because that's the job, and an uncorrected inconsistency that a reviewer catches later in Q&A ("earlier you said X, but just now you said Y — which is it?") reads far worse than if you'd caught and fixed it yourself the moment it happened. Self-correcting signals that you're tracking your own presentation carefully; being caught in an inconsistency you could have fixed yourself signals the opposite.

## The CTA board's explicit allowance for revision

Lesson 5 covered the CTA board's expectation that candidates defend and, where warranted, revise their solution under objection. The same principle extends to self-caught mistakes: training material on the CTA format describes the review board as expecting candidates to be able to adjust a solution live when they realize something doesn't hold up, not just when a judge points it out. A candidate who catches their own inconsistency and cleanly revises it mid-session is demonstrating the same real-world judgment the board rewards when a judge catches it instead — the self-caught version is simply the stronger showing of that same skill.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Factual error | A wrong number or detail, best handled with a short, direct correction |
| Design inconsistency | A contradiction between two things said about the same design, requiring an actual resolution of which version is correct |
| Misstatement under pressure | Something said in the heat of rapid questioning that doesn't reflect your real position, best handled with a clean, brief retraction |
| Self-correction | Catching and fixing your own mistake before a reviewer catches it for you |
