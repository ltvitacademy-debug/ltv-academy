# Your First 90 Days as an Oracle Financials Consultant

**Chapter 2 · Resume, Capstone Story and Certification · Lesson 14 of 15**

Getting the role is the goal of everything so far in this course. This lesson is about what actually happens after — a realistic 30/60/90-day framework for your first real engagement, and the mistakes new consultants make most often in exactly this window.

## What you'll learn

- A realistic 30/60/90-day framework for a first Oracle Financials consulting role
- The most common mistakes new consultants make in each phase
- Why your capstone experience doesn't replace a real engagement's learning curve — and how to say so honestly

## Days 1-30: learn the real environment

Your capstone taught you Oracle Fusion Financials generally. A real client's instance is never a blank version of what you practiced on — it has its own configuration history, its own quirks, its own undocumented workarounds someone built three years ago for a reason nobody remembers. The first 30 days are about learning *this specific* environment: shadow the close process, read the client's actual configuration and documentation, and ask more questions than you answer. The most common mistake here is assuming your training generalizes perfectly and jumping in to "fix" something before you understand why it's set up that way.

## Days 31-60: start contributing on small, well-bounded work

By day 60, you should be taking on real tasks — configuration changes, testing support, specific tickets — but still within a scope someone more senior reviews before it goes live. This is where your hands-on practice pays off directly: you've already configured matching rules, resolved holds, built reconciliations. The mistake to avoid here is taking on ambiguous work without checking the actual requirement first, the same scenario-question discipline from lesson 4 — restate it, ask a clarifying question if it matters, then act.

## Days 61-90: own a small workstream, and build real trust

By day 90, a realistic goal is owning a defined piece of work end to end — a specific reconciliation, a specific report, a specific subledger's period-close checklist — with real accountability, not just following instructions. Trust at this stage comes less from technical correctness and more from communication: flagging a problem early, giving a realistic timeline instead of an optimistic one, and documenting what you did clearly enough that someone else could pick it up. The mistake to avoid: going quiet when something's wrong instead of raising it, which is exactly the instinct lesson 2 and lesson 11 both trained.

## What your capstone experience actually is, and isn't, in this window

Be honest with yourself here, the same way lesson 8 asked you to be honest on your resume: the capstone gave you real reps at diagnosing and fixing problems, but it didn't give you the experience of a real client's politics, a real production environment's scale, or a real team's existing conventions. Say that plainly if asked — "I've done this kind of diagnostic work in a training environment; I expect the real version to have more moving parts, and I plan to ask more than I assume in the first month" is a credible, mature answer that actually builds confidence rather than undermining it.
