# Behavioral Interview Preparation

**Chapter 2 · Resume, Capstone Story and Certification · Lesson 11 of 15**

Every Oracle Financials interview eventually turns away from the software and asks about you — how you handle pressure, ambiguity, and disagreement. These behavioral questions are not a formality. For a consulting role especially, they're often weighted as heavily as the technical questions, because a technically correct consultant who can't communicate under pressure is a real liability on a client engagement.

## What you'll learn

- The STAR format applied to behavioral questions generally, building on lesson 9's use of it for the capstone
- Four behavioral questions specific to consulting work, with an approach for each
- Why your capstone experience gives you real material here, not just for technical questions

## STAR, as a general-purpose tool

Situation, Task, Action, Result works for any behavioral question, not just the capstone story. The discipline is the same: set up the situation briefly, name what you were responsible for, describe the specific actions you took, and close with a concrete result — ideally one you can quantify or verify, not just assert.

## Four consulting-specific behavioral questions

**"Tell me about a time you had to deliver difficult news to a stakeholder."**
Your capstone gives you a real structure for this even in a training context: when Elena Marsh called with six symptoms and a two-day deadline, the honest answer wasn't "everything's fine" — it was naming exactly what was broken, giving a realistic plan, and following through. Use that structure: name the hard truth plainly, pair it with a credible plan, and follow through visibly.

**"Describe a situation where requirements were ambiguous or kept changing."**
Consulting work is full of this. A strong answer doesn't complain about the ambiguity — it describes how you narrowed it: asking a specific clarifying question (the move from lesson 4's scenario questions), documenting what was agreed so it doesn't drift again, and flagging scope changes early rather than absorbing them silently.

**"Tell me about a time you made a mistake. What happened, and what did you do?"**
Pick something real and specific, own it without over-explaining or deflecting, and emphasize what you changed afterward, not just that you apologized. A believable answer admits the mistake plainly in one sentence before moving to what you did about it.

**"How do you handle working with someone whose communication style is very different from yours?"**
Name the actual friction honestly (not generically), describe the specific adjustment you made — asking for things in writing, over-communicating status, adjusting your own pace — and close with how the working relationship actually improved. Avoid framing this as "the other person was the problem."

## Why the capstone matters here too

Lesson 9 covered the capstone as a technical story. It's also your best behavioral material: a tight deadline, six real interconnected problems, and a need to communicate findings clearly to a stakeholder under pressure. Don't limit the capstone to technical questions — it's genuinely useful for "tell me about a time you worked under pressure" as well.
