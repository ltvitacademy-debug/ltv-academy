# Expense Accounting

Every expense report that gets approved eventually needs a proper accounting entry — a debit to an expense account and a credit to a liability account, following the same double-entry logic you learned in the Accounting Fundamentals course. This lesson shows how Oracle Fusion Expenses builds that entry through Subledger Accounting, the same engine you already used for Payables and Receivables.

## What you'll learn

- The basic accounting entry behind a reimbursed expense report
- Why Expenses uses Subledger Accounting rather than posting directly to the GL
- How itemization and distributions affect the number of accounting lines
- How accounting differs between cash expenses and corporate card expenses

## The basic entry

At its simplest, an approved and accounted expense item produces:

- A **debit** to an expense account — determined by the expense type's default distribution from the template (lesson 3), such as Travel & Lodging Expense or Business Meals Expense.
- A **credit** to a liability account — representing what the company now owes, either to the employee (Employee Expense Payable) or to the corporate card issuer (Corporate Card Payable), depending on how the item was captured.

```
Journal entry - Hotel expense, cash-paid (illustrative)
  Debit:  Travel & Lodging Expense        $275.00
  Credit: Employee Expense Payable                   $275.00

Journal entry - Hotel expense, company-liability card (illustrative)
  Debit:  Travel & Lodging Expense        $275.00
  Credit: Corporate Card Payable                      $275.00
```

The liability stays on the books until Payables actually pays it, at which point a separate entry (generated by the Payables payment process, not Expenses) clears the liability and credits cash.

## Why Subledger Accounting, not a direct GL post

Expenses does not write directly to the General Ledger. Instead, it generates accounting events that Subledger Accounting (SLA) interprets using accounting rules, the same architecture used by Payables invoices and Receivables transactions. This buys Castellan the same benefits you saw in those earlier courses: the ability to see a complete audit trail from the original expense item through to the GL journal, the ability to run "what-if" accounting before actually posting, and consistent handling of things like multi-currency conversion and intercompany balancing without Expenses needing its own separate logic for any of it.

## Itemization multiplies accounting lines, not complexity

Recall Priya's itemized hotel folio from lesson 6. Three expense items from one receipt means three separate accounting events, each hitting its own expense account:

```
Priya's itemized folio -> three accounting events (illustrative)
  Hotel (room+tax)      $924.00  -> debit Travel & Lodging Expense
  Business Meal         $18.47   -> debit Business Meals Expense
  Entertainment-Personal $21.00  -> non-reimbursable, no company liability credit
```

Notice the non-reimbursable item: it still needs to be visible in the system for policy tracking, but because the company isn't paying it, there is no corresponding liability credit — Expenses either posts it to a tracking account with no cash impact or excludes it from the liability calculation entirely, depending on how system options were configured.

## Cash versus corporate card accounting, side by side

The expense account debited is identical either way — a Hotel expense posts to the same Travel & Lodging account regardless of payment method. What changes is only the liability account credited, because that determines who gets paid when Payables runs: the employee, or the card issuer.

## Recap

An approved expense report generates a debit to an expense account and a credit to a liability account (employee or card issuer), built through Subledger Accounting rather than a direct GL post, exactly like Payables and Receivables. Itemization produces one accounting event per itemized piece, and non-reimbursable items are tracked without creating a liability.
