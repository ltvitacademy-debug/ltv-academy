# Revenue Recognition Basics in Receivables

If you took the Accounting Fundamentals course in this catalog, you've already met the idea that revenue isn't always recognized the moment cash or an invoice shows up — it's recognized when it's earned. This lesson closes Chapter 3 by looking at how that principle shows up specifically inside Receivables transactions.

## What you'll learn

- The difference between immediate and deferred revenue recognition on a transaction line
- What a revenue scheduling rule actually controls
- Why this matters even though the invoice and the cash timing don't change

## Immediate vs. deferred recognition

Every Receivables transaction line can recognize its revenue one of two ways:

- **Immediate** – the full revenue amount is recognized in the same accounting period the line is accounted in. This is the right choice for most straightforward product sales: the goods ship, the sale is complete, revenue is earned now.
- **Deferred** – revenue is spread across multiple accounting periods according to a **revenue scheduling rule**, because the obligation being billed for is actually delivered over time: a 12-month software subscription, a maintenance contract, a service retainer.

Note that this is purely an accounting-recognition decision — it doesn't change when the invoice is sent, when it's due, or when cash is collected. A one-year maintenance contract can be invoiced in full on day one, due per its payment terms, while its revenue is recognized one-twelfth per month over the year.

## Revenue scheduling rules

A revenue scheduling rule defines how a deferred amount gets spread:

- **Fixed schedule** – revenue is recognized in a defined number of periods, in equal (or specified) amounts per period, regardless of the exact number of days in each period.
- **Daily revenue rate** – revenue is recognized based on the actual number of days in each accounting period relative to the total days in the recognition window, which handles partial first/last periods more precisely than a simple fixed split.

Until revenue is recognized, the unrecognized portion sits in an **unearned revenue** account (a liability, since it represents an obligation still owed to the customer) rather than a revenue account; as each period's recognition runs, Receivables moves the appropriate amount from unearned revenue to recognized revenue.

## A worked example

Northwind Fixtures Co. sells Harborline Retail Group a 12-month equipment maintenance contract for $12,000, invoiced in full upfront on January 1 with Net 30 terms. Because the contract line uses a deferred revenue scheduling rule (daily rate, 12 equal monthly periods), Receivables doesn't recognize $12,000 of revenue in January. Instead, it books the invoice as a debit to Accounts Receivable and a credit to Unearned Revenue for the full $12,000 at completion, then recognizes $1,000 of revenue each month for twelve months, moving it from Unearned Revenue to Revenue as each period's recognition process runs — even though Harborline paid the whole invoice back in January.

## Recap

A Receivables transaction line can recognize revenue immediately or defer it across periods using a revenue scheduling rule (fixed schedule or daily rate), independent of invoice and payment timing. Deferred amounts sit in unearned revenue until each period's recognition moves the appropriate share into revenue. This closes Chapter 3.
