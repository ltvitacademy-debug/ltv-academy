# Lesson 29 — Communicating Governance to the Business

**Chapter 5 · Getting Started · Lesson 29 of 30**

## What you'll learn

- Why the word "governance" itself can work against you with a
  business audience
- How to explain governance in one plain sentence, with no acronyms
- Why framing it as an enabler, not a constraint, changes how people
  react to it
- Which channels actually carry the message, and what role the
  executive sponsor plays in each

## The word problem

Lesson 17 and Lesson 18 covered RACI and executive sponsorship — the
structural side of governance communication, who's accountable to
whom. This lesson is about something that trips up even well-staffed
programs: the actual words you use with people who don't live in this
material every day.

"Data governance" itself is part of the problem. To a business
audience, "data" sounds like a tactical IT concern and "governance"
sounds like bureaucracy — rules layered on top of work that was going
fine without them. None of Chapters 1 through 4's real substance
survives if the first sentence someone hears makes them tune out.

## One plain-language definition

Before anything else, have one sentence ready that avoids acronyms and
jargon entirely: *data governance is the set of rules, roles, and
responsibilities for how data gets created, stored, accessed, and
used — so that the numbers people rely on are accurate, consistent,
and available when they're needed.* No "metadata," no "lineage," no
framework names. If a term from Chapters 1-4 is genuinely necessary in
a specific conversation, translate it into what it means for that
person's job before using the term itself.

## Frame it as an enabler, not a constraint

The single biggest shift in how governance lands is framing. The same
program described as "rules you now have to follow" and "the reason
you'll trust the number in next week's report" is the same program —
but only one of those gets a willing audience. Concretely:

- Instead of "you can't access this data anymore," say "here's the
  faster, approved way to get this data, with less waiting."
- Instead of "this policy restricts what you can do," say "this policy
  is what makes the self-service dashboard safe to roll out."
- Instead of leading with the rule, lead with the business problem the
  rule solves — Lesson 27's pilot result is exactly this kind of
  evidence, if you ran one.

## Choosing the channel

Different audiences need the message delivered differently, and the
executive sponsor (Lesson 18) has a distinct job in each:

| Channel | Audience | Sponsor's role |
|---|---|---|
| All-staff email or town hall | Whole organization | Signs it, or speaks at it — visible ownership |
| Manager-only briefing | People leaders | Equips managers to answer their own teams' questions |
| Office hours / embedded stewards | Day-to-day data users | Mostly steward-led (Chapter 3), not sponsor-led |
| One-on-one with a skeptical stakeholder | A specific blocker | Sponsor spends political capital directly |

Repetition across more than one channel matters more than any single
polished announcement — a message heard once in an email and never
again fades; the same message in a town hall, a manager briefing, and
a steward's office hours in the same month actually sticks.

## Tie it back to real evidence

Communication that leans on abstractions ("better data culture")
competes poorly with communication grounded in something that
actually happened. If Lesson 27's pilot produced a real result —
reconciliation time cut, a specific error caught before it reached a
report — that's the single most persuasive thing in any of these
channels, more persuasive than a framework slide or a maturity-model
diagram. Plan to use it.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Framing | How a message is positioned — as a constraint vs. an enabler — which shapes how an audience reacts to otherwise-identical content |
| Enabler framing | Describing governance in terms of what it makes possible (faster, trusted access) rather than what it restricts |
| Channel | A specific communication vehicle (town hall, briefing, office hours, one-on-one) matched to a specific audience |

## Lab

Write the one-sentence, jargon-free definition of data governance
from this lesson in your own words (keep it under 40 words, no
acronyms). Then take one specific policy or rule from your
organization (or invent a plausible one) and rewrite it twice: once
as a constraint ("you can't...") and once as an enabler ("here's the
faster way to..."). Notice which version you'd actually want to read
if you received it in an email.

## Check yourself

You're ready for Lesson 30 when you can state the plain-language
definition of data governance without jargon, explain why framing as
an enabler changes reception, and match at least two audiences to
the right communication channel.
