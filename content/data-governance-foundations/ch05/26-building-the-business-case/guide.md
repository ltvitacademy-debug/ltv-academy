## Governance has to compete for the budget

Lesson 4 covered the cost of poor data in the abstract — rework,
bad decisions, compliance exposure. Lesson 25 turned that into a
specific, organization-level gap list. This lesson is about the step
between having that list and actually getting a program funded:
writing the case that competes against every other initiative asking
for the same budget and the same people's time.

Nobody gets governance funded by asserting it's important. Governance
work gets funded the same way any other business initiative does —
with a defined problem, a proposed solution, a resource ask, and a way
to measure whether it worked.

## Four components of a credible business case

1. **The problem, in business terms.** Not "our metadata isn't
   managed" — "the reporting team spent three weeks reconciling two
   conflicting revenue numbers before last quarter's board meeting."
   Lesson 25's gap list supplies the raw material; this step
   translates it into a story a non-technical sponsor already
   recognizes.
2. **The proposed solution and scope.** What you're actually asking
   for — typically a scoped pilot (Lesson 27), not a company-wide
   program on day one. Naming the scope up front prevents the case
   from reading as an open-ended ask.
3. **Required resources.** People (even part-time), a modest tooling
   budget if one is needed, and — critically — executive sponsorship
   itself as a resource (Lesson 18). Underselling the people cost is
   the single most common way business cases fail to deliver later.
4. **Anticipated value and how you'll measure it.** Tie value to
   things the audience already tracks: time spent reconciling numbers,
   number of compliance findings, time to approve a new report. Vague
   value ("better data culture") doesn't survive a budget review;
   measurable value does.

## Framing for different audiences

The same program, framed three different ways, because each
stakeholder is actually weighing a different risk:

| Audience | What they're actually weighing | How to frame it |
|---|---|---|
| CFO | Cost avoidance, audit exposure | Rework hours saved, fines or findings avoided |
| CIO | Operational risk, technical debt | Fewer duplicate/conflicting data pipelines, faster onboarding of new data sources |
| Business unit leader | Speed and trust | Faster, more confident answers to the questions their team already asks |

Industry write-ups on this topic report meaningful numbers worth
knowing as context — one analysis put the average annual cost of data
non-compliance in the eight figures for large organizations, and
another noted that analysts can lose a third or more of their time
hunting for a trusted version of data when no inventory exists. Treat
figures like these as *illustrative industry context*, not your own
organization's numbers — the credible business case uses your own
readiness-assessment findings as the real evidence, and only reaches
for outside statistics to frame why the category matters at all.

## Why the gap list does most of the work

A business case written without Lesson 25's gap list tends to read as
generic — every governance pitch ever written says roughly the same
things about "trust" and "quality." A gap list makes the case
specific to *this* organization: it names the actual dimension that's
weakest, the actual pain that's already visible, and the actual gap
between where things stand and where a first program needs them to
be. That specificity is what separates a business case that gets
funded from a slide deck that gets politely filed away.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Business case | The document that justifies an initiative by naming the problem, solution, resources, and anticipated value |
| Cost avoidance | Value framed as fines, rework, or risk *not* incurred — the framing a CFO typically weighs most heavily |
| Scoped ask | A business case's request limited to a defined pilot or phase, rather than an open-ended program |

## Lab

Using the gap list you wrote in Lesson 25's lab, draft a one-page
business case for your own organization (or the team/department you
used before) with four short sections: the problem in business terms
(one real or representative anecdote), the proposed scope (name one
pilot-sized ask, not a company-wide rollout), the resources required
(people and time, named specifically), and how you'd measure anticipated
value (one or two concrete, trackable metrics). Keep each section to
2-3 sentences — the discipline of being specific in a short space is
the actual exercise.
