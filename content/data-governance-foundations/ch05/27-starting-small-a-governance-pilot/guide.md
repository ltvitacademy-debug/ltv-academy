## Why pilot instead of rolling out everywhere

Lesson 26's business case got you a yes. The temptation at that point
is to go big immediately — stand up the full operating model from
Chapter 2, staff every role from Chapter 3, publish a complete policy
library from Chapter 4, across the whole organization, all at once.

That temptation is worth resisting. Scaling governance too quickly
scales whatever mistakes are baked into your first attempt right along
with it — a confusing RACI, an unrealistic policy, a tool nobody
adopted. A pilot is a controlled environment to find those mistakes
while the cost of being wrong is still small: one domain, one
business unit, a fixed window of time.

Pilots also do something a roadmap slide never can: they produce a
true story. "We cut reconciliation time on customer revenue data from
three weeks to four days" is more persuasive to a skeptical executive
than any amount of framework diagramming, and it's the exact kind of
evidence Lesson 29 needs for communicating governance to the rest of
the business.

## Choosing where to pilot

Not every domain makes an equally good pilot. Three criteria, used
together:

- **High value** — a domain the business actually cares about (revenue
  data, customer data, a regulated dataset) so a win is visible and
  worth talking about
- **High pain** — a domain where people already feel the problem, so
  stakeholders show up and engage instead of needing to be convinced
  the problem exists
- **High feasibility** — a domain small enough, with few enough
  systems and stakeholders, that a small team can actually finish
  something in a defined window

The best pilot domain scores well on all three. A domain that's
high-value and high-pain but spans twelve systems and four business
units is a program, not a pilot — that's a feasibility failure, and
it's the single most common way "starting small" quietly turns into
"starting the same big thing, just called a pilot."

## The five pieces a pilot needs

1. **A defined objective** — one sentence, stated before the pilot
   starts: what should be true at the end that isn't true now.
2. **A baseline measurement** — the current state of whatever the
   objective is about, captured *before* anything changes, so the
   "after" number means something.
3. **A named owner** — someone from Chapter 3's cast (a data owner or
   steward), not "the governance team" as an abstraction.
4. **A fixed time window** — 60 to 90 days is typical. A pilot without
   an end date is just an ongoing project wearing a pilot's name.
5. **A feedback and scale decision point** — a planned moment, at the
   end of the window, to look at the baseline-vs-result numbers and
   explicitly decide: scale it, adjust it, or stop it.

## Common pilot pitfalls

- **Too ambitious a scope** — picking a domain that fails the
  feasibility criterion above, so the pilot never finishes within its
  window.
- **No executive visibility** — running the pilot quietly, so even a
  genuine win has no audience and dies for lack of a champion.
- **No baseline, no metrics** — "it feels better now" doesn't survive
  a scale-or-stop decision, no matter how true it is.
- **No defined end** — letting the pilot drift into an unplanned,
  permanent half-state instead of reaching an explicit decision point.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pilot | A scoped, time-boxed governance effort in one domain, used to prove the model before scaling |
| Baseline | The measured current state, captured before the pilot starts, that the end-of-pilot result is compared against |
| Scale-or-stop decision | The planned moment at the end of a pilot's window where the team decides to expand it, adjust it, or end it |
