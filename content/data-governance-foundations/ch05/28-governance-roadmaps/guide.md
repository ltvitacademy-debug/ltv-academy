# Lesson 28 — Governance Roadmaps

**Chapter 5 · Getting Started · Lesson 28 of 30**

## What you'll learn

- How a successful pilot turns into a multi-phase roadmap instead of
  just repeating itself in a second domain
- A realistic phase structure — roughly 90 days, 3-6 months, and
  6-12 months — and what belongs in each
- Why every phase needs its own named owner and measurable milestone,
  not just a date on a timeline
- How to keep a roadmap honest instead of turning it into an
  aspirational wall poster

## From one pilot to a program

Lesson 27's pilot proved something in one domain, in one fixed
window. A roadmap is what turns that single proof point into an
organization-wide program — not by repeating the pilot in every
domain at once, but by sequencing the work in phases, each one
building on what the last phase actually learned.

Industry write-ups on governance implementation converge on roughly
the same shape, even though the exact day counts vary by
organization: an initial discovery-and-pilot phase measured in weeks,
a foundation-building phase measured in months, and a scale phase
measured in the better part of a year. The specific numbers below are
a reasonable starting template — adjust them to your own
organization's size and pace, but keep the *shape*.

## A realistic phase structure

1. **Days 0-30 — Discovery.** Confirm goals and stakeholders, identify
   the critical data domains, and capture current pain points — this
   overlaps heavily with Lesson 25's readiness assessment and often
   runs alongside it rather than strictly before it.
2. **Days 31-60 — Foundation.** Assign the first real data owners and
   stewards (Chapter 3), document the first policies (Chapter 4), and
   catalog the priority domain's assets and glossary terms.
3. **Days 61-90 — Pilot.** This is Lesson 27's pilot, running inside
   the roadmap rather than as a separate, disconnected exercise:
   launch the governance workflow for the one chosen domain, certify
   its priority datasets, and start monitoring quality.
4. **Months 3-6 — Expand.** Take what worked in the pilot — the
   workflow, the templates, the lessons about what didn't work — and
   apply it to two or three more domains, not all of them at once.
5. **Months 6-12 — Scale.** Governance processes become
   business-as-usual: new data sources get owners and classifications
   by default, the operating model (Chapter 2) handles steady-state
   decisions, and the program stops needing to justify its own
   existence every quarter.

## What belongs in every phase

A roadmap that's just a row of colored boxes on a timeline is a
decoration, not a plan. Each phase needs three things attached to it:

- **A named owner** for that phase's work — not the program in the
  abstract
- **A defined output** — what artifact or capability exists at the end
  that didn't exist at the start (a policy document, a certified
  dataset, a staffed council)
- **A measurable milestone** — a number or a yes/no state that lets
  anyone, including a skeptical executive, check whether the phase
  actually happened

This is the same discipline Lesson 25's gap list and Lesson 27's
pilot already used — defined, named, measurable — applied to the
roadmap as a whole instead of to one dimension or one pilot.

## Keeping the roadmap honest

Roadmaps fail quietly in two directions. The first is treating the
dates as sacred and marching forward even when a phase's milestone
wasn't actually hit — that just moves the honesty problem to the next
phase instead of solving it. The second is treating the roadmap as
aspirational marketing — a wall poster nobody actually checks against
reality. Revisit the roadmap at the end of every phase, compare it
against what was actually delivered, and adjust the next phase's
scope based on that evidence, the same way Lesson 27's scale-or-stop
decision used pilot results instead of a guess.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Roadmap | A phased sequence that turns a single pilot's proof point into an organization-wide program |
| Phase | One segment of the roadmap (e.g., discovery, foundation, pilot, expand, scale) with its own owner, output, and milestone |
| Business-as-usual | The end state where governance decisions happen through the standing operating model rather than a special program |

## Lab

Sketch a five-phase roadmap for your own organization (or the
team/department you've used throughout this chapter), reusing the
pilot you scoped in Lesson 27's lab as the "Pilot" phase. For each of
the five phases — discovery, foundation, pilot, expand, scale — write
one line naming its owner, one line naming its output, and one line
naming its milestone. Fifteen short lines total; the goal is a
roadmap you could defend phase-by-phase if an executive asked "how do
we know this phase actually happened."
