## Governance programs rarely fail loudly

A governance program almost never fails with a dramatic, visible collapse. It fails quietly: budget keeps getting renewed, meetings keep happening, and the underlying problems — conflicting numbers, duplicated effort, untraceable data — never actually go away. Recognizing the specific failure pattern early is the difference between a correctable course and a program that limps along for years producing nothing.

## Five common failure patterns

1. **No executive sponsorship.** Governance initiated and run entirely by a mid-level data team, with no senior leader who can actually mandate compliance across departments. Early warning sign: policies exist on paper but no one outside the data team follows them, and there's no consequence when they don't. (Lesson 18 covers why sponsorship specifically matters.)

2. **Governance as a one-time project, not an ongoing program.** A council is formed, policies are written, a kickoff happens — and then the initiative quietly stops, because it was scoped and budgeted like a project with an end date instead of an operating discipline. Early warning sign: there's a "governance launch" but no cadence of recurring reviews, metrics, or renewed decisions after it.

3. **Tool-first thinking.** An organization buys a data catalog, MDM platform, or quality tool and treats the purchase itself as "doing governance" — skipping the harder work of actually defining ownership, policy, and decision rights the tool is supposed to support. Early warning sign: the tool is live, dashboards exist, but nobody can say who's accountable when the dashboard shows a problem.

4. **No clear decision rights.** Everyone agrees data quality matters, but when two departments disagree about a definition, there's no defined process or authority to resolve it — so the disagreement just persists, unresolved, indefinitely. Early warning sign: the same definitional argument resurfaces in meeting after meeting with no record of ever being settled.

5. **Governance disconnected from business value.** The program measures itself by internal activity — policies written, meetings held, catalog entries created — rather than by any business outcome anyone outside the data team cares about. Early warning sign: when budget season comes, no one outside the data organization can articulate what the governance program actually did for them.

## Why these are organizational failures, not technical ones

None of these five patterns involve a technology gap. They're failures of sponsorship, structure, decision rights, and measurement — which is exactly why the rest of this course spends so little time on specific tools and so much on frameworks (Chapter 2), roles (Chapter 3), policy (Chapter 4), and how to actually start a program well (Chapter 5). Fixing a governance program that's failing almost never starts with buying new software.

## How the rest of this course addresses each one

Sponsorship gaps are addressed directly in Chapter 3 (roles, including Lesson 18's focus on executive sponsorship). Project-vs-program thinking and tool-first mistakes are addressed by choosing the right operating model in Chapter 2. Decision-rights gaps are addressed by RACI structures in Chapter 3. And disconnection from value is addressed by building the business case properly in Chapter 5.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Executive sponsorship | Senior leadership authority that gives a governance program the power to mandate compliance |
| Tool-first thinking | Mistaking the purchase of a data tool for the completion of governance work |
| Decision rights | A defined process and authority for resolving disagreements about data |
