## What data governance actually is

Data governance is the system of decision rights, accountability, and rules that determines who can create, change, access, and retire an organization's data — and what "good" data is supposed to look like once it exists. It is not a tool. It is not a single team. It is the set of agreements an organization makes about its own information so that two departments looking at "revenue" or "active customer" are looking at the same thing.

Most working definitions (DAMA International's among them, which Lesson 7 covers in depth) boil down to the same core idea: governance is about **exercising authority and control over data assets**, the same way a finance function exercises authority and control over money. No company would let every employee independently decide what counts as "revenue." Most companies still let that happen with data.

## Why this is unavoidable now, not optional

Three forces have converged to make governance a board-level topic rather than an IT backwater:

1. **Volume and sprawl.** Data now lives in SaaS tools, cloud warehouses, spreadsheets, and vendor systems that no single person inventories. Nobody governs what they can't see.
2. **Regulation.** GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, HIPAA, and sector-specific rules now carry real financial penalties for mishandled personal data — turning governance from a "nice to have" into a legal obligation. Lesson 5 goes deeper on this driver specifically.
3. **AI and automated decisions.** Machine learning models trained on ungoverned data inherit every inconsistency, bias, and error in that data, then make those errors at scale and speed. Garbage in is no longer just an annoying report — it's a decision made automatically, thousands of times, before anyone notices.

## What an organization without governance actually looks like

It is rarely dramatic. It looks like:

- Three departments presenting three different "total customer count" numbers in the same meeting, each one technically correct by its own undocumented definition
- A spreadsheet of "the real numbers" that one analyst maintains by hand, which the rest of the company quietly relies on
- A data breach investigation that takes weeks because nobody can say with confidence where a particular customer's data actually lives
- A new analytics hire spending their first month just trying to figure out which of four "customer" tables is the right one

None of these are failures of technology. They are failures of agreement — exactly what governance exists to fix.

## Where this course goes from here

This chapter stays conceptual: what governance is, what it costs to skip, and what's driving it. Chapter 2 covers named frameworks (DAMA-DMBOK, DCAM, and others) and operating models. Chapter 3 moves into roles — owners, stewards, councils. Chapter 4 covers policies and standards. Chapter 5 is about actually starting a program at a real organization. By the end, you'll be able to walk into a governance conversation and know what's actually being asked of you.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Data governance | The system of decision rights, accountability, and rules over how data is created, accessed, and used |
| Data asset | Data treated and managed with the same rigor as a financial or physical asset |
| Data sprawl | Data spread across many disconnected systems with no central inventory or ownership |

## Lab

Don't solve it. Just describe exactly what disagreed and why you think it happened. This is the raw material governance exists to prevent.
