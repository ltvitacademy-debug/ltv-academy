## An important caveat, stated plainly

This lesson is an orientation to the *shape* of a few widely-referenced data regulations —
not legal advice, and not a comprehensive survey of global privacy law. Regulatory
requirements are detailed, jurisdiction-specific, and change over time. Any organization
with real compliance obligations needs actual legal counsel, not a foundations course. What
this lesson gives you is enough vocabulary to recognize why Chapter 4's policies and
standards so often trace back to a specific regulatory requirement, not an abstract idea of
"privacy is good."

## GDPR — the EU's general framework

The General Data Protection Regulation applies globally to any organization processing the
personal data of EU residents, regardless of where that organization is based. It generally
requires **opt-in consent** before processing someone's personal data, and it designates
certain categories — racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, genetic
and biometric data, health information — as "special category data" requiring an explicit
legal basis, often explicit consent, beyond ordinary processing. GDPR also grants data
subjects broad rights, including data portability across different controllers.

## CCPA/CPRA — California's consumer-focused model

The California Consumer Privacy Act (as amended and expanded by the CPRA) applies to
California residents' personal data and takes a structurally different approach than GDPR:
it generally uses an **opt-out** model for the sale or sharing of personal information,
rather than GDPR's opt-in requirement. In practice, that means a business can generally
collect and process personal information by default under CCPA, and it's the consumer's
responsibility to actively exercise their right to opt out of having it sold or shared — the
opposite default from GDPR.

## HIPAA — US health information specifically

The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act protects Protected Health
Information (PHI) specifically within US healthcare contexts, applying to "covered entities"
and their "business associates" who handle PHI — not to personal data generally the way
GDPR and CCPA do. HIPAA grants individuals rights to access and request amendment of their
own records within a "designated record set." HIPAA compliance has continued to evolve —
for example, more recent guidance has emphasized stronger authentication requirements and
tighter rules around website tracking technologies.

## The one distinction that trips people up most

If you remember one structural fact from this lesson, make it this: **GDPR defaults to
opt-in consent; CCPA defaults to opt-out.** An organization that assumes "we're
GDPR-compliant, so we're covered everywhere" can still be out of compliance with CCPA's
different consent model, and vice versa — the two regulations solve a similar underlying
concern (control over personal data) with genuinely different mechanics, not just different
paperwork.

## Why this matters for everything else in this chapter

Regulatory requirements are one of the main reasons a policy (Lesson 19) exists in the first
place, and one of the main triggers that sends a policy back through its lifecycle for
revision (Lesson 22). A policy like "customer financial information is confidential" often
isn't an arbitrary business choice — it's the organization's way of satisfying an underlying
legal obligation, translated into language the business can actually act on day to day.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| GDPR | EU regulation on personal data; generally opt-in consent model |
| CCPA/CPRA | California regulation on consumer data; generally opt-out model for sale/sharing |
| HIPAA | US regulation protecting health information (PHI) in healthcare contexts |
| Special category data | GDPR's term for sensitive categories requiring extra legal basis |
