# Lesson 22 — Policy Lifecycle

**Chapter 4 · Policies and Standards · Lesson 22 of 30**

## What you'll learn

- The six stages a policy moves through, from first draft to retirement
- What happens at each stage, and who's typically involved (tying back to Chapter 3's roles)
- Why "retirement" is a stage that gets skipped more than any other — and why that's a
  problem
- What triggers a policy to move back through the cycle instead of staying static

## Why a policy needs a lifecycle at all

A policy isn't a document you write once and leave alone — the business, the regulatory
landscape (Lesson 24), and the systems a policy governs all keep changing. Policy lifecycle
management is the structured, end-to-end governance of a policy from its initial draft
through revisions, approvals, publication, and eventual retirement. Skipping stages, or
treating the cycle as a one-time event instead of an ongoing process, is how organizations
end up with policies nobody follows because nobody remembers they exist, or policies that
actively contradict how the business now actually works.

## The six stages

1. **Drafting** — the policy owner identifies the business or regulatory need, assigns an
   author (typically a steward, per Lesson 19), defines scope, and produces a first
   controlled version naming its owner, status, intended audience, and a proposed review date
2. **Review** — specialists — legal, compliance, IT, the governance committee — assess the
   draft for accuracy, legality, practicality, and whether it conflicts with any existing
   policy
3. **Approval** — the authorized body (typically the governance committee, sometimes with
   sponsor sign-off for anything organization-wide) approves, rejects, or sends the draft back
   for changes, with every decision recorded against the specific version
4. **Publication** — the approved policy reaches the people actually expected to follow it,
   with access controlled by role — publishing a policy nobody can find is barely different
   from not publishing it at all
5. **Review and revision** — policies get checked on a set schedule (the proposed review
   date from stage 1) or triggered early by a regulatory change, a business change, or a
   steward flagging that the policy no longer matches reality
6. **Retirement** — outdated policies are formally retired and removed from active
   circulation — not just forgotten in a folder where someone might still find and follow a
   rule that no longer applies

## The stage that gets skipped

Retirement is, in practice, the stage organizations handle worst. It's easy to approve and
publish a new policy; it's much easier to simply stop thinking about an old one than to
formally mark it retired. The result is an environment where outdated policies sit alongside
current ones with no clear signal which is which — and an employee who finds the old one
first has no way to know it's obsolete. A policy's lifecycle isn't complete until retirement
is handled with the same formality as approval.

## What triggers a policy back into the cycle

- A scheduled review date arrives (set at drafting time)
- A regulatory change makes part of the policy outdated or newly non-compliant (Lesson 24)
- A business change — a new product line, a merger, a new system — makes the policy's scope
  wrong
- A steward or committee flags that the policy, as written, no longer matches how work
  actually gets done

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Policy lifecycle | The structured path a policy follows from draft to retirement |
| Review date | A scheduled trigger to re-examine a policy before it goes stale |
| Retirement | Formally removing an outdated policy from active circulation |
