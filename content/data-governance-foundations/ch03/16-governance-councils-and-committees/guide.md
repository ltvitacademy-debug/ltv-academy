# Lesson 16 — Governance Councils and Committees

**Chapter 3 · Roles and Structure · Lesson 16 of 30**

## What you'll learn

- Why individual roles (owner, steward, custodian) aren't enough on their own
- The typical layered structure: council, steering committee, governance committee, working groups
- What each layer actually does, and what it escalates upward
- How to right-size this structure for a smaller organization

## Why roles alone aren't enough

Lessons 13–15 gave you four roles tied to individual people and domains. But data rarely
stays inside one domain's boundaries — a dispute between the "customer" owner and the
"orders" owner about what counts as an active account needs somewhere to go that isn't
either person's individual authority. That's what a governance **council** or **committee**
structure exists for: a standing, cross-functional body that sets policy, resolves disputes
that cross domains, and gives the whole collection of individual roles a shared forum.

## The typical layered structure

Most mature governance programs separate strategy from operations across a few layers,
even if a smaller organization compresses several of them into one group:

1. **Governance council (or steering committee)** — senior leaders who set overall strategy,
   sponsor the program, and make the highest-level trade-off calls (budget, priorities,
   which domains get attention first)
2. **Governance committee** — the operational layer: sits below the council, above the
   stewards, and decides day-to-day how data is defined, owned, and used across domains.
   This is usually where cross-domain disputes that owners can't resolve alone land.
3. **Subcommittees / working groups** — focused on a specific domain or initiative (e.g., a
   "customer data" working group), doing the detailed work of defining standards and
   resolving issues at the subject-matter level, escalating to the main committee only when
   they can't resolve something themselves

## Who sits on the committee

A governance committee typically draws from business, IT, security, legal, and compliance —
the committee needs enough functional range to actually resolve cross-cutting issues without
constantly having to pull in outside specialists. IT and architecture representatives weigh
in on technical feasibility; legal and compliance interpret regulatory requirements; business
representatives bring the domain context an owner or steward alone might not have for a
dispute between domains.

## What the committee is actually for

- **Creating and approving policy** — the committee is usually where Chapter 4's policies get
  drafted, reviewed, and formally adopted
- **Resolving cross-domain disputes** — conflicts no single owner can settle alone
- **Formally empowering owners and stewards** — naming who holds which role, and making
  that assignment visible and legitimate across the organization
- **Prioritizing issues** — when stewards surface more data quality problems than can be
  fixed at once, the committee decides what gets attention first
- **Acting as final authority** — for anything that can't be resolved at a lower level

## Right-sizing this for a smaller organization

Not every organization needs four distinct layers. A smaller company might run with just two:
an executive sponsor (Lesson 18) plus a single cross-functional committee that both sets
policy and resolves disputes — compressing council and committee into one group that meets
monthly. What matters isn't the number of layers; it's that there's *some* standing,
cross-functional forum above the level of individual data owners, so cross-domain issues have
somewhere real to go instead of stalling indefinitely.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Governance council | Senior body setting strategy, sponsoring the program, making top-level trade-offs |
| Governance committee | Operational body handling policy, cross-domain disputes, and prioritization |
| Working group | Domain- or initiative-specific group doing detailed standards work |
