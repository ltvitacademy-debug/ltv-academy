## Why there's more than one framework

DMBOK (Lesson 7) is the field's reference encyclopedia, but it isn't the only named framework organizations actually adopt. Three others come up constantly in real governance work, each solving a different piece of the puzzle: **DCAM**, built for maturity benchmarking; **COBIT**, built for IT governance with data governance as part of its scope; and **ISO/IEC 38505**, built for board-level accountability. Knowing what each one is actually *for* prevents the common mistake of picking a framework because it's well-known rather than because it fits the problem.

## DCAM — Data Management Capability Assessment Model

Developed by the **EDM Council**, DCAM is built around structured maturity scoring: it assesses an organization's current data management capability across a defined set of components and produces a maturity profile, which then becomes the basis for a roadmap. DCAM's strength is benchmarking — it gives a defensible, structured way to say "here's where we are today" and "here's the gap to where we need to be." Its tradeoff is that it's assessment-heavy and less prescriptive about exactly how to execute the fixes it identifies.

## COBIT — IT governance with data governance inside it

**COBIT**, maintained by **ISACA**, is fundamentally an IT governance framework — it covers the full scope of how an enterprise governs and manages IT, with data governance as one component inside that larger scope rather than the sole focus. Organizations that already run COBIT for broader IT governance often extend it to cover data governance specifically, rather than adopting a separate, dedicated framework. It's a strong fit where data governance needs to plug into an existing, mature IT governance structure rather than stand alone.

## ISO/IEC 38505 — governance as a board-level responsibility

**ISO/IEC 38505** is an international standard that frames the governance of data explicitly as part of organizational governance — a responsibility of the governing body and senior executives, not an operational, IT-level concern. It operates at a noticeably higher altitude than DCAM or COBIT: it doesn't tell you how to run a stewardship program, it establishes that data governance is something the board is accountable for. In practice, organizations that need to demonstrate governance alignment to international regulators or auditors often use ISO 38505 as the high-level mandate sitting above a more operational framework.

## Combining frameworks in practice

These frameworks aren't mutually exclusive, and most real governance programs borrow from more than one. A common, practical combination: ISO 38505 establishes the board-level mandate and accountability; DCAM or DMBOK supplies the operational structure and maturity roadmap underneath it; COBIT comes into play specifically where data governance needs to integrate with a broader, already-established IT governance program.

## A simple way to choose

Ask three questions: Do we need a maturity benchmark to show leadership where we stand? (DCAM.) Do we already run IT governance and need data governance to plug into it? (COBIT.) Do we need to establish board-level accountability for data as a formal governance matter? (ISO 38505.) None of these replace DMBOK's role as the shared reference vocabulary — they answer "which structure do we actually operate under," while DMBOK answers "what does good data management even look like."
