# Lesson 11 — Centralized, Decentralized and Federated Governance · Voiceover script

Segments map 1:1 to slides. Target: ~2-3 minutes total.

## S1 · TITLE

Three operating model types, in detail — and why one of them has become the common default for larger organizations.

## S2 · STEPS — Centralized

One central team owns every governance decision. Strength: maximum consistency, one enforced definition everywhere. Weakness: it doesn't scale — a central team becomes a bottleneck and lacks the local context business units actually have.

## S3 · STEPS — Decentralized

Each business unit governs its own data independently, no central authority. Strength: speed and deep local context. Weakness: this is exactly how conflicting definitions happen — nothing forces "active customer" to mean the same thing twice. It tends to emerge by default, not deliberate design.

## S4 · STEPS — Federated

A central body sets shared standards and definitions for what genuinely needs consistency; business units keep authority over their own local decisions. Strength: real consistency where it matters, speed everywhere else. Weakness: hardest to design and run — the line between central and local has to be actively maintained.

## S5 · STEPS — Why federated is the common default

For mid-size and large organizations with more than one distinct business unit, federated is the most commonly recommended starting point — not because it's easy, but because pure centralized doesn't scale, and pure decentralized recreates the exact problem governance exists to solve.
