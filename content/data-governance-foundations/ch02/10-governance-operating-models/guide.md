## An operating model is the "how," not the "what"

Lessons 7 and 8 covered *frameworks* — the body of knowledge and the named standards that define what good governance covers. An **operating model** is a different thing entirely: it's how governance actually runs day to day inside a specific organization — who does the work, where decisions get made, and how authority flows. Two organizations can both claim to "follow DAMA-DMBOK" and run completely different operating models, because DMBOK defines *what* needs governing, not *who* governs it or *where* that authority sits.

## What every operating model has to define

Regardless of which specific model (Lesson 11 covers the three major types), every real operating model answers the same core questions:

1. **Where do decisions get made?** At the center, close to the business units that own the data, or somewhere in between?
2. **Who holds the authority?** A dedicated governance team, business-unit data owners (Lesson 13), a council (Lesson 16), or some combination?
3. **How does policy get enforced across the organization?** Through a central mandate, through voluntary adoption with incentives, or through a formal escalation path when disagreements happen?
4. **How does the model scale as the organization grows?** Does adding a new business unit or acquiring a company require rebuilding the model, or does it extend naturally?

## Why the same framework runs differently in practice

A large, decentralized conglomerate and a small, single-product company can both genuinely be practicing DAMA-DMBOK's eleven knowledge areas — but the conglomerate likely needs decisions pushed out to each business unit (because no central team could realistically own data quality for a dozen unrelated product lines), while the small company can centralize almost everything in one small governance team. Same framework, same knowledge areas, completely different operating model. This is precisely why Lesson 11 doesn't present "the one correct operating model" — it presents three distinct types, because the right choice depends on organizational structure, not framework choice.

## The stakes of getting this wrong

An operating model mismatched to the organization is one of the quiet failure patterns from Lesson 6: a centralized model forced onto a decentralized, multi-business-unit company creates a bottleneck — every decision waits on a small central team that can't realistically understand every business unit's data. A decentralized model with no central coordination at a company that genuinely needs consistency (say, for a single regulatory reporting requirement across all units) creates exactly the conflicting-definitions problem governance exists to prevent. The operating model isn't a secondary detail — it's often the single biggest factor in whether a governance program actually works.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Operating model | How governance actually runs day to day — who decides, where authority sits, how it's enforced |
| Framework | What needs to be governed (DMBOK's knowledge areas, for example) — distinct from how it's run |
| Decision rights | Who has the authority to make and enforce a governance decision |
