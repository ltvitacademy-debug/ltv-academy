## What a maturity model actually measures

A data governance maturity model is a structured way to answer one question: how capable is this organization, right now, at governing its data — not in terms of good intentions, but in terms of defined, repeatable, measured practice? Maturity models score an organization against defined levels, from ad hoc and reactive at the bottom to fully optimized and continuously improving at the top. The value isn't the score itself — it's that a maturity model forces a structured, honest assessment instead of a gut-feel claim like "we're pretty good at this."

## Three real, named models

**Gartner's data governance maturity framework** (now retired as an active Gartner offering, but its five-level structure remains widely referenced) assessed organizations across five progressive levels — commonly described as Aware, Reactive, Proactive, Managed, and Optimized — along seven building blocks including vision, strategy, metrics, and organizational roles.

**IBM's Data Governance Maturity Model** assesses eleven governance disciplines (including data risk management, data quality management, and information lifecycle management) across four stages: Aware, Controlled (sometimes called Reactive), Managed, and Optimized — with detailed criteria defined for each discipline at each stage.

**CMMI's Data Management Maturity (DMM) model**, from the CMMI Institute, applies the same five-level process-maturity structure CMMI uses elsewhere in software and systems engineering — Initial, Managed, Defined, Quantitatively Managed, and Optimizing — to data management specifically, assessed across six key themes including data governance, data quality, and platform/architecture.

These models differ in detail, but they share the same underlying shape: a small number of ordered levels, moving from ad hoc/reactive practice toward defined, measured, and continuously improved practice.

## Where most organizations actually land

Across these models, the honest, widely reported finding is the same: most organizations cluster in the lower-middle levels — aware that governance matters and reacting to problems as they arise, but without the proactive, measured, organization-wide discipline the top levels describe. This isn't a reason for discouragement; it's the actual, documented starting point most governance programs begin from, which is exactly why Chapter 5 of this course treats "assessing readiness" and "starting small" as real, necessary steps rather than something to skip past.

## Using a maturity assessment honestly

The failure mode to avoid: treating a maturity score as a badge to report upward rather than a diagnostic to act on. A maturity assessment is useful only if it identifies *specific* gaps — which of the building blocks or disciplines are weakest — and feeds directly into a roadmap (Lesson 28 covers governance roadmaps specifically). A maturity model that just produces a number with no attached action plan is governance theater, not governance work.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Maturity model | A structured framework for assessing how capable an organization is at a given discipline, across ordered levels |
| Maturity level | One stage in a maturity model's progression, from ad hoc to fully optimized |
| Maturity assessment | The actual exercise of scoring an organization against a maturity model's levels |
