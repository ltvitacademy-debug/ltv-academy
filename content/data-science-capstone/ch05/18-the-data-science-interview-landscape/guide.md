# The Data Science Interview Landscape

Data science interviews have a reputation for being unpredictable, and there is some truth to it: there is no single standard process, and companies mix and match rounds. But the same building blocks appear again and again. Knowing them lets you prepare for categories instead of trying to guess questions. Everything in this lesson describes common patterns, not a promise about any particular employer, so read each job posting and ask your recruiter what to expect.

## What you'll learn

- The rounds a data science hiring process commonly includes
- How the process differs between analytics-flavored and machine-learning-flavored roles
- What to say in a recruiter screen
- A simple, realistic preparation plan for the rest of this chapter

## The common rounds

A typical process, in rough order, looks like this. Not every company uses every round, and the order varies.

1. **Recruiter screen (about 20-30 minutes).** Fit, logistics, interest, salary expectations, work authorization. Not technical, but you can be filtered out here.
2. **Hiring manager or team conversation.** Your background, your projects, and how you think about the problem the team works on.
3. **SQL and/or coding screen.** Live or timed problems in SQL and often Python or pandas. Lesson 20 practices this.
4. **Statistics and machine learning questions.** Concepts, judgment and trade-offs: bias and variance, evaluation metrics, A/B tests, leakage. Lesson 19 practices this.
5. **Case study or take-home.** An open-ended business problem, either discussed live or done on your own time. Lesson 21 covers strategy.
6. **Presentation or onsite loop.** Several conversations in a row, sometimes including a presentation of a past project. Your capstone presentation practice applies directly here.
7. **Behavioral and values conversations.** Often woven into every round, sometimes a dedicated one. Lesson 22 covers this.
8. **Offer and negotiation.** Lesson 23.

## Role flavors change the mix

Job titles are inconsistent, so read the description, not just the title.

| Flavor | Common emphasis |
|---|---|
| Analytics or product-focused data scientist | SQL, statistics, experiment design, business cases, communication |
| Machine-learning-focused data scientist | Modeling, evaluation, coding, sometimes deployment and MLOps |
| Applied or research-flavored | Deeper math and statistics, papers, more open-ended problems |

Junior roles frequently lean toward SQL, statistics fundamentals and communication, since employers expect to teach the rest. Whatever the flavor, SQL is commonly tested, so it deserves steady practice.

## The recruiter screen

Prepare a 60-second answer to "Tell me about yourself" in three beats: where you come from, what you built (the Harvest Table churn project in one sentence, with its result and that the data was fictional), and what you are looking for. Have two or three questions ready about the team, the kind of data they use, and how models or analyses reach decisions. Be ready with a salary range, but a common approach is to let the employer share their range first if you can.

## What interviewers are usually evaluating

Across rounds, the underlying questions tend to be the same:

- **Can you get the data?** SQL, joins, aggregation.
- **Can you reason about it?** Statistics, experimental thinking, skepticism.
- **Can you build something sound?** Model choice, validation, avoiding leakage.
- **Can you connect it to a decision?** Business framing and trade-offs.
- **Can you communicate and collaborate?** Clear explanations, honest limits.

## A four-week preparation plan

Adapt this to your timeline:

- **Week 1:** Resume, LinkedIn, GitHub polish. Draft your 60-second pitch and three project stories.
- **Week 2:** SQL and pandas practice, a few problems per day, said aloud.
- **Week 3:** Statistics and machine learning question review; rehearse explanations without jargon. Do one practice take-home.
- **Week 4:** Mock interviews with a friend or mentor, behavioral stories, research target companies.

Keep applying throughout; do not wait until you feel perfectly ready. Rejections are common and are not a verdict on you, especially early in a career.

## Recap

Expect a sequence: recruiter screen, team conversation, SQL and coding, stats and machine learning, case or take-home, a final loop and behavioral questions, then an offer. The mix depends on the role. Prepare by category, keep a short pitch ready, and practice out loud. Next, we work through statistics and machine learning questions.
