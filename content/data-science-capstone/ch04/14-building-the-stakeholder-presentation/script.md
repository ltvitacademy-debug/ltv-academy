Technical audiences reward the order in which you did the work. Business audiences want the reverse: the answer first, then the evidence, then the risks, then the ask. So our deck opens with the recommendation. Each cycle, contact the three hundred sixty-nine active customers the model ranks as highest risk. Then the evidence, then the value with its assumptions, then the risks, stated specifically.

A note on which numbers to use. The test set gave point four oh five precision in the top ten percent, but that was only seventy-four customers. For the deck we use cross-validated predictions for all thirty-six hundred ninety customers, so the top ten percent is the full three hundred sixty-nine. Those customers cancel at forty percent, with an interval from thirty-five to forty-five, against fifteen point four percent for the average customer. That is a lift of two point six times, comfortably above the twenty point eight percent break-even.

One chart carries it: a bar per risk decile, with the average and the break-even as reference lines. The two tallest bars are the customers worth contacting.

Now stress-test the value. Under the illustrative assumptions, the program returns about fifty-one hundred per cycle, about thirty-seven fifty at the pessimistic end of the interval. But the sensitivity table shows the real risk: if the offer saves fewer than about sixteen percent of would-be churners, the program loses money. The model predicts who might leave, not who will respond, and the thirty percent save rate is an assumption. So the recommendation includes a pilot with a randomized holdout group.

Finally, say the limitations out loud. One snapshot, no test on a later period. Assumed costs. Modest signal: six in ten customers on the list would not have left. Explanations describe the model, not causes. A specific limitations slide reads as competence. A vague one reads as evasion. Next, we rehearse the questions you will be asked.
