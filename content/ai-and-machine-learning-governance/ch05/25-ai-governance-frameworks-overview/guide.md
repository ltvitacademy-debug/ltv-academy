## An important note before we start

This lesson names real, publicly known frameworks so you recognize them when they come up in a job, a vendor conversation, or a news article. **It is an orientation, not legal advice.** Frameworks get updated, superseded, and reinterpreted. If your organization needs to determine which framework applies to it, and exactly how, that is a conversation for legal and compliance professionals with current access to the primary source documents — not a single lesson in an IT training course.

## The NIST AI Risk Management Framework

Published by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, the NIST AI RMF is a voluntary framework organized around a small set of core functions for managing AI risk throughout a system's lifecycle — commonly described as Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. It doesn't prescribe specific technical controls; it gives organizations a shared vocabulary and structure for building their own AI risk program, which is why it shows up so often as the starting reference point in US-based AI governance work.

## ISO/IEC 42001

ISO/IEC 42001 is an international standard for an **AI management system** — modeled on the same "management system" pattern as ISO's well-known quality (ISO 9001) and information security (ISO 27001) standards. Rather than dictating specific AI rules, it specifies what an organization's *process* for managing AI responsibly should look like: policies, roles, risk assessment, and continual improvement, structured so an organization can seek formal certification against it.

## The EU AI Act's risk-tiered approach

The European Union's AI Act is a piece of binding law (not voluntary, unlike the two frameworks above) that classifies AI systems into risk tiers — broadly, from unacceptable-risk systems that are prohibited, through high-risk systems that carry the heaviest compliance obligations, down to limited- and minimal-risk systems with lighter or no specific obligations. The exact scope, deadlines, and obligations are detailed and have been subject to phased implementation — which is precisely the kind of specific, time-sensitive detail this lesson will not attempt to state definitively. The structural idea worth taking away is the one this entire chapter has been building toward: **not every AI system deserves the same level of scrutiny, and risk tier should drive governance effort.**

## Other frameworks you'll encounter

You will also run into sector-specific guidance (for example, financial regulators publishing model-risk-management guidance long before "AI governance" was a common phrase), individual countries' own national AI strategies, and large technology vendors' own published responsible-AI frameworks. None of these are covered here in detail — the goal of this lesson is recognition, not mastery of any one of them.

## How to actually use this lesson

Treat this as a map, not a manual. When your organization tells you which framework it has adopted or which regulation applies to it, go to that framework's own primary source document and, ideally, involve someone with compliance or legal expertise. What this lesson gives you is the vocabulary to not be lost in that conversation.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| NIST AI RMF | A voluntary U.S. framework organizing AI risk management around Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage |
| ISO/IEC 42001 | An international standard specifying what an organization's AI management system and processes should look like |
| EU AI Act | Binding EU law that classifies AI systems into risk tiers with different compliance obligations |
