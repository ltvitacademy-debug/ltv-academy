# Lesson 7 — Data Provenance and Consent

**Chapter 2 · Data for AI · Lesson 7 of 30**

## What you'll learn

- What provenance means for training data, and why it has to be tracked before training, not after
- What consent means in this context, and why it's a separate question from legality
- The common sources of training data and the different provenance questions each one raises
- Why "we found it on the internet" is not a provenance answer

## Provenance: knowing where data actually came from

Provenance is the documented history of a piece of data — where it originated, how it was collected, what happened to it before it reached the training set. For training data specifically, provenance has to answer more than "which database did we pull this from." It has to answer where that database's data originally came from: Was it collected directly from the people it describes? Purchased from a broker? Scraped from public web pages? Generated by another AI system? Each answer carries a different risk profile, and "we're not sure" is itself a governance finding, not a neutral answer.

## Consent: a separate question from legality

Consent asks whether the people whose data is being used actually agreed to this specific use — not just whether using the data happens to be legal. A company's terms of service might technically permit using customer data for "service improvement," but a customer who agreed to that language years ago, for a different purpose, may reasonably not have anticipated their data training a model that makes decisions about other people. Consent, done well, is specific about what the data will be used for, not a blanket permission buried in boilerplate. This becomes especially important when models are trained on content people created — writing, images, code, conversations — rather than data collected through a transaction.

## Common sources and what each one raises

- **Internally collected data** (your own customers, your own systems) has the clearest provenance, but still needs a consent check against what customers were actually told.
- **Licensed third-party data** shifts the provenance question to the license terms: what was the vendor allowed to collect, and does their license to you extend to training a model, specifically?
- **Public or scraped data** has the murkiest provenance of the four. "Publicly visible" is not the same as "available for any use," and the people described in scraped data were never asked anything.
- **Data from another AI system's output** raises its own provenance question: does that system's license or terms permit using its output to train a separate model?

## Why "we found it on the internet" isn't an answer

Provenance governance exists specifically because that phrase used to be treated as sufficient and increasingly isn't. A governed organization documents the actual source, the collection method, the applicable license or consent basis, and who approved the use — before training starts, so the question doesn't have to be answered retroactively after a model is already in production.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Provenance | The documented history of where a piece of data originated and how it was collected |
| Consent | Whether the people whose data is used actually agreed to this specific use, distinct from legality |
| Scraped data | Data collected automatically from publicly visible web pages, often without direct consent |
