Almost every analysis starts from an uncomfortable fact: you rarely see everything. You care about all your customers, but you only have some of them. This lesson covers the vocabulary of populations and samples, how far a sample can wander, and how a badly collected sample can be wrong in ways more data never fixes.

The population is the complete group you care about. The sample is the part you actually observe. A number describing the population, like its true average spend, is a parameter: fixed, but usually unknown. A number computed from your sample is a statistic. We use statistics to estimate parameters, and statistics change from sample to sample.

We simulated a hundred thousand customers so we can see the truth: average spend is one seventy point two two. Now draw five random samples of two hundred. The means run from one fifty-nine to one eighty. None is wrong; that spread is ordinary random sampling variability. Random error has no lean, it averages out, and the next lessons will measure it.

Bias is different. It is a systematic tilt caused by how you collect the data. Suppose a survey is answered only by customers with six or more orders a year. Even with two thousand respondents, the average comes out at two forty-one, far above the true one seventy. Collecting twenty thousand such respondents would just make you more precisely wrong.

Bias sneaks in several ways. Selection bias means some people simply cannot be reached. Voluntary response means those who opt in are unusual; when we let the chance of responding rise with spend, the estimate hit two fifty. Survivorship bias means you only see the ones who stayed. Always ask who is missing from your data and why.

Better designs help. Simple random sampling is the baseline. Stratified sampling splits the population into groups and samples within each, so every region appears in proportion, and here it gives one seventy-four. Convenience sampling, using whoever is easy to reach, is common and risky. Fix bias with design, not with volume.
