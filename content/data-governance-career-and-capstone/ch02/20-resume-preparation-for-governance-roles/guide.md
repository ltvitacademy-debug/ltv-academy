## The action + artifact + framework pattern

A strong governance bullet answers three questions in one line: what did you do, what did you actually produce (something a reviewer could ask to see), and what standard, tool, or framework did you use. Vague verbs like "worked on" or "helped with" tell a reviewer nothing they can follow up on.

**Before:** "Worked on data quality for the customer domain."

**After:** "Wrote and ran 12 SQL data quality checks (NULL rate, uniqueness, referential integrity) against a 6-table customer domain, combined into one UNION ALL failures report."

**Before:** "Did some documentation for the project."

**After:** "Built a 40-term business glossary and data dictionary for the Customer and Order domains, with a named owner and an approval status on every term."

**Before:** "Helped trace where some data came from."

**After:** "Documented table-to-report lineage for an executive revenue dashboard across four hops, then used it to trace a reporting discrepancy back to its source system."

**Before:** "Worked on compliance and access stuff."

**After:** "Classified 18 tables by sensitivity level and designed a role-based access model applying least privilege across the customer and HR domains."

Notice what each "after" version does: it names a real number (12 checks, 40 terms, four hops, 18 tables), a real artifact (a failures report, a glossary, a lineage diagram, an access model), and a real concept from this path (referential integrity, approval status, root-cause tracing, least privilege). That's the pattern — repeat it for every bullet.

## Structure of a one-page governance resume

At this career stage, one page is the right length. A common order:

1. **Header.** Name, city, email, LinkedIn URL.
2. **Summary (optional).** Two lines naming your target role and strongest governance skills.
3. **Capstone / projects.** Three to four deliverables, each with two to three bullets written in the action + artifact + framework pattern.
4. **Skills, grouped.** Governance frameworks (DAMA-DMBOK, RACI), SQL and profiling, documentation and catalog tools.
5. **Experience.** Earlier roles, with bullets that show data, process, or documentation work where honestly possible.
6. **Education and certifications.**

Draw your project bullets from across the whole capstone: the business glossary and critical data elements, the data quality rules and SQL checks, the lineage documentation, the access and retention policies, the governance operating model, and the executive strategy document. Pick the ones closest to the job posting in front of you, and only list a skill you could discuss out loud for five minutes.

## Tailoring and keywords

Read the job posting and note the terms it repeats — "data steward," "metadata," "lineage," "access governance," "DAMA-DMBOK." Where they truthfully describe your work, reuse that exact wording; applicant tracking systems and human reviewers both scan for it. Never paste in a term you can't back up in conversation.

## Common mistakes

- **Vague verbs instead of named deliverables.** "Assisted with governance" says nothing; "built," "wrote," "documented," "classified" each point at a real artifact.
- **Listing frameworks or tools you can't discuss for five minutes.** If a bullet mentions DAMA-DMBOK or a specific platform, be ready for a follow-up question about it.
- **Blurring who decided versus who implemented.** This path's owner/steward distinction (Data Governance Foundations, Lesson 13) applies to how you describe your own capstone role too — be precise about whether you proposed a policy or executed one that was already approved.
- **Inconsistent numbers across documents.** If your resume says "12 checks" and your portfolio says "15," that gap is the first thing a careful reviewer will notice.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Action + artifact + framework | A resume-bullet pattern naming what you did, what you produced, and what standard or tool you used |
| Applicant tracking system (ATS) | Software that scans resumes for keywords before a human reviews them |
| Tailoring | Matching a resume's wording to a specific job posting's actual language, truthfully |
