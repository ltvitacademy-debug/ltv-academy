# Lesson 23 — Explaining Your Capstone in Interviews

**Chapter 2 · Career Preparation · Lesson 23 of 35**

## A structure for the short version

Interviewers rarely want a chronological tour of all 18 lessons. They want four things, in order:

1. **Situation.** The problem, stated in one sentence: a fictional global organization with no shared definitions, no quality checks, and no documented data lineage, across multiple systems.
2. **Task.** What the engagement needed to deliver: a working governance program — not just a document, an actual operating model with real artifacts behind it.
3. **Action.** What you actually built: critical data elements and a business glossary, data owners and stewards, SQL data quality rules, documented lineage, access and retention policies, governance KPIs, and an operating model.
4. **Result.** What the finished program demonstrates — framed as the capability it proves, not as an invented business outcome. This was a training capstone, so the honest claim is "a complete governance program a real organization could adopt," not a fabricated dollar figure or percentage improvement nothing in the project actually measured.

## The 90-second version, for LTV Global specifically

"I led the data governance workstream for a fictional multi-system global organization, LTV Global, across an eighteen-lesson capstone. I identified the critical data elements and assigned named owners and stewards, built a business glossary that resolved conflicting definitions across departments, wrote SQL data quality rules against the organization's data, documented lineage so issues could be traced to their source, and designed the access, retention, and governance operating model that ties it together. I packaged the whole thing into an enterprise governance strategy and presented it as an executive deck."

Notice this names real deliverables and real lesson topics, not invented statistics. That's deliberate: a confident, specific answer about what you actually built is stronger than a vague answer propped up by a number you can't defend under a follow-up question.

## Fielding common follow-ups

- **"Walk me through one specific artifact."** Pick the one you know best — often the SQL data quality rules or the glossary — and go deep rather than skimming across all of them.
- **"What would you do differently?"** Name one real, honest limitation. A reasonable answer: the operating model chose a single governance structure (Data Governance Foundations, Lesson 11 covers the centralized/decentralized/federated choice); in a real rollout you'd expect to revisit that choice as the organization's needs became clearer.
- **"What was the hardest decision?"** A good, honest answer names a real trade-off from the capstone — for example, choosing which operating model fits a multi-system global organization, or deciding how to prioritize which data elements counted as "critical" first.

## When you're asked about a part you didn't personally lead

If this capstone was completed solo, this is less of an issue — but if any part of your real experience involved a team, be straightforward: "That part — the governance architecture design — was [teammate]'s focus; my own strongest contribution was the data quality rules and the glossary." Claiming credit for work you didn't do is the fastest way to lose credibility the moment a follow-up question goes one level deeper than you can answer.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Situation / Task / Action / Result | A four-part structure for compressing a long project into a short, interview-ready answer |
| Scope honesty | Accurately representing which parts of a project you personally did versus which you didn't |
| Capability claim | Stating what a project demonstrates you can do, instead of an invented business outcome it never actually measured |
