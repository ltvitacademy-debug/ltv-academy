# Lesson 18 — Final Review and Retrospective

**Chapter 1 · Capstone: LTV Global Data Governance Program · Lesson 18 of 35**

## What you'll learn

- A full retrospective on everything the LTV Global program built,
  Lesson 1 through Lesson 17
- Three realistic caveats about what a program like this actually
  takes in the real world — time, politics, and iteration
- Why "done" is the wrong word for a governance program, even a
  successful one
- How to carry this capstone forward as you move from Chapter 1 into

**Reminder:** LTV Global, its program, and everything built for it
across this chapter are fictional and illustrative — invented for this
capstone, never a real case study.

## What the program achieved

In eighteen lessons, LTV Global went from a six-week DSAR that reached
the board to a governed program with:

- A landscape inventory and a scored, owned set of critical data
  elements — five, once `DateOfBirth` was caught (Lessons 2-4, 6)
- A business glossary, a sensitivity classification, and real T-SQL
  quality checks against Atlas (Lessons 5-7)
- Documented lineage and formally declared authoritative sources,
  including a golden Customer record that answers the Lesson 1 problem
  directly (Lessons 8-9)
- Access and retention policy, governance KPIs, and a four-stage issue
  workflow (Lessons 10-11)
- A federated hybrid operating model with a permanent home for all of
  the above (Lesson 12)
- An AI governance strategy applied to two real pilots, and a target
  architecture for Summit (Lessons 13-14)
- An assembled deliverables package, a written strategy document, and
  a board-ready presentation (Lessons 15-17)

Every one of those pieces depended on the one before it — nothing in
this list could have been built out of order.

## Three realistic caveats

This capstone compressed what a real program like this would take
into eighteen lessons. A real rollout would differ in three specific
ways:

- **Time.** Lessons 1 through 17 read as a continuous eighteen-step
  sequence. A real version of this program plays out over twelve to
  twenty-four months, across multiple budget cycles, not one
  continuous sprint — and Dana Whitfield would be defending the budget
  for it more than once along the way.
- **Politics.** This capstone's authoritative-source decisions
  (Lesson 9) and classification calls (Lesson 6) landed cleanly in one
  meeting each. In practice, a domain owner whose system "loses" an
  authoritative-source decision often pushes back, and a federated
  hybrid model (Lesson 12) only holds together with real executive
  backing when that happens — not because the org chart says it
  should.
- **Iteration.** `DateOfBirth` surfacing mid-program (Lesson 6) and
  the drift the Lesson 15 consistency check caught weren't
  edge cases — they're what every real governance program looks like.
  A program that never finds a gap after its first pass isn't more
  mature, it's not looking hard enough.

## Why "done" is the wrong word

Lesson 12's operating model made this explicit already: nothing built
in this chapter is a one-time document. The glossary, the CDE
register, the KPIs, the architecture — all of it is maintained going
forward by the structure this program built, not frozen the moment
this capstone ends. A governance program's actual deliverable isn't
the eighteen artifacts; it's the operating model that keeps producing
and correcting them after the capstone is over.

## From capstone to career

Everything built in this chapter is evidence of a real, demonstrable
skill set: landscape assessment, CDE scoring, ownership design,
glossary and classification work, SQL-based quality checks, lineage
and authoritative-source documentation, policy design, KPI design, AI
governance, architecture, and executive communication. That's not a
list of topics you studied — it's a portfolio of work a hiring manager
can ask you to walk through, which is exactly what the next part of
this course prepares you to do.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Retrospective | A structured look back at what a program achieved and what would be done differently |
| Program maturity | How well a governance program detects and responds to new gaps, not whether it has zero gaps |
| Portfolio evidence | Concrete, specific work a candidate can describe and defend, as opposed to general topic familiarity |
