# Lesson 17 — Building the Executive Presentation

**Chapter 1 · Capstone: LTV Global Data Governance Program · Lesson 17 of 35**

## What you'll learn

  Whitfield and the board
- What executives actually listen for, versus what a governance
  practitioner is tempted to lead with
- How to frame the Lesson 1 incident in terms of time and dollar risk,
  not governance jargon
- What "the ask" looks like at the end of a capstone presentation like

**Reminder:** LTV Global and every figure below are fictional and
illustrative, invented for this capstone. The UK GDPR penalty

only to frame how a real board would hear this kind of risk.

## Fourteen deliverables, one story

Lesson 16 wrote a thorough document. Nobody presents a thorough
document to a board. This lesson compresses it into a narrative Dana
Whitfield can deliver in minutes, not a reading of Lesson 16 section
by section.

## What executives actually listen for

- **Time compressed to seconds, not slides.** A board doesn't need
  the six-week DSAR timeline explained — it needs the number six
  weeks, once, and what changes it to ten days.
- **Risk stated in dollars, not jargon.** "Confidential classification
  violation" means nothing to a board. "Exposure under UK GDPR's
  penalty structure — up to 4% of global annual turnover or £17.5
  million, whichever is greater" means something, even stated once as
  context rather than a prediction.
- **A roadmap, not a wishlist.** Four phases already completed reads
  as momentum; a list of things governance "should" do reads as an
  ask with nothing behind it.
- **One specific ask, not vague support.** "Please value data
  governance" gets nodded at and forgotten. A named budget, headcount,
  or mandate renewal gets a yes or no.

## The five-beat arc

1. **The hook.** Open exactly where Lesson 1 opened: a UK customer's
   GDPR request took six weeks and four departments, and Beacon and
   Comet couldn't agree on that customer's own order history. That
   reached this board once already.
2. **The cost of inaction.** State the exposure plainly: the staff
   hours already spent on the first DSAR, and the regulatory exposure
   of a repeat incident under UK GDPR's real penalty structure — not
   a prediction, just the ceiling a comparable finding could reach.
3. **What we built.** Not fourteen lesson titles — four outcomes: named
   owners for the data that matters, one reconciled Customer record,
   governed rather than unmonitored AI pilots, and a real architecture
   for Summit instead of an empty name.
4. **The roadmap.** Foundation, Control, Operationalize, Extend —
   stated as momentum already proven, with what's next named
   specifically rather than left open-ended.
5. **The ask.** Funding to build the Lesson 14 architecture in
   production, headcount for Marcus Ibe's governance office as it
   takes on permanent operational work, and Dana Whitfield's mandate
   renewed for the next phase.

## Why the GDPR incident opens it, every time

Lesson 1's incident isn't just a teaching device for this course — in
the presentation itself, it's the one story every person in the room
already half-remembers, because it reached the board once already.
Opening there means the ask at the end lands as "finish fixing the
thing you already know about," not "fund something new and abstract."

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Narrative arc | The ordered sequence of beats a presentation moves through to build toward its ask |
| The ask | The single, specific, decidable request a presentation ends on |
| Risk framing | Translating a technical or compliance issue into terms — usually time or money — that a non-specialist audience weighs decisions by |
