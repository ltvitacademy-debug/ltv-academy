# Lesson 19 — Portfolio of Solutions

**Chapter 3 · Presenting and Defending · Lesson 19 of 21**

## What you'll learn

- Why a CTA candidate benefits from a standing portfolio, not just scenario notes written and discarded per exercise
- What belongs in each case study's portfolio entry, and what doesn't
- How to organize a portfolio so it's actually usable under interview or exam time pressure, not just complete
- How to build your own first portfolio entry from work you've already done in this course

## A portfolio is a tool you'll reach for again, not a record of what you did once

Every case study in this course so far has produced real artifacts — requirements breakdowns, stated assumptions, blueprint diagrams, rejected-alternatives tables, objection-and-answer pairs. If those artifacts live only in whatever scratch document you used while working through a given lesson, you've done the thinking once and thrown away the asset. A portfolio collects that work deliberately, organized so you can actually find and reuse it later — before a real interview, before an actual review board, or simply to refresh your own thinking on a domain you haven't touched in months.

## What belongs in a portfolio entry

A useful portfolio entry per case study is short enough to review in a few minutes, not a transcript of everything you ever wrote about it: the one-paragraph scenario summary, the two or three hard facts and hidden constraints that most shaped the design (Lesson 9's categories), the key stated assumptions and their consequences (Lesson 10), the one or two highest-priority risks and how the design answers them (Lesson 11), a compact version of the four blueprint artifacts — diagrams if you have them, otherwise a few bullet points per artifact (Lesson 12) — and your best one or two rejected-alternatives entries (Lesson 13). This is deliberately a summary of the *reasoning*, not a re-creation of the full original scenario text or every quiz question you've answered about it.

## What doesn't belong

A portfolio entry gets less useful, not more, if it tries to capture everything — the full objection-and-answer transcript from every mock session, every minor assumption that turned out not to matter, every version of a diagram you iterated through before landing on the final one. The test is the same removal test from Lesson 9: if cutting a piece of content wouldn't change how useful the entry is the next time you open it under time pressure, cut it. A thirty-page portfolio entry per case study is not more impressive than a two-page one; it's slower to actually use.

## Organizing for time pressure, not for completeness

The organizing principle that matters most is retrieval speed: when you need to recall how you handled Alder Trust Bank's segregation-of-duties requirement, or why Fenwick State's design treats a student-employee dual-persona case the way it does, you should be able to find that specific reasoning in under a minute, not by re-reading an entire case study from the top. Organizing entries by scenario (as this course's seven case studies naturally suggest), with a short tag or heading per domain touched (data, security, integration, identity, delivery), makes a specific decision findable by the domain a new question is actually about, not just by which company the scenario happened to be about.

## Building your first entry from work you've already done

You don't need a new scenario to start a portfolio — you already have seven case studies' worth of requirements, assumptions, risks, blueprints, and rejected alternatives scattered across this course's earlier labs. The fastest way to build a first real portfolio entry is to go back to whichever case study you know best from your own lab work so far (likely the one you used in Lesson 8's or Lesson 18's mock session, since you've already reasoned through it twice) and compress what you already produced down to the format described above.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Portfolio entry | A compact, reusable summary of a case study's reasoning, built for quick retrieval under time pressure |
| Removal test (reapplied) | Cutting any portfolio content that wouldn't change how useful the entry is the next time it's opened |
| Retrieval speed | The organizing priority that a specific past decision can be found quickly, not just that everything is recorded |
| Domain tagging | Labeling a portfolio entry by the architecture domains it touches, so a new question can find relevant past reasoning fast |

## Lab

Pick the case study you know best from your own work in this course so far. Compress everything you've already produced about it into a single portfolio entry following this lesson's format: scenario summary, hard facts/constraints, assumptions with consequences, top risks and their answers, compact blueprint bullets, and your best rejected-alternatives entry. Apply the removal test to cut anything that wouldn't speed up your own future retrieval.
