# Lesson 20 — Peer and Instructor Feedback

**Chapter 3 · Presenting and Defending · Lesson 20 of 21**

## What you'll learn

- A rubric with four concrete categories for giving and receiving structured feedback on a mock session
- Why vague feedback ("that was good," "needs work") doesn't actually help a candidate improve
- How to give feedback as a peer that's honest without being unkind, and useful without being exhaustive
- How to receive critical feedback on your own mock session without getting defensive about it

## Feedback only helps if it's specific enough to act on

A mock review board (Lessons 8 and 18) is only as useful as the feedback that follows it. "That was pretty good" and "you seemed nervous" are both real reactions a peer or instructor might have, and neither one tells the candidate what to actually change before the next attempt. Useful feedback names a specific moment, ties it to a specific category, and — ideally — suggests what a stronger version would have looked like, the same way this course's model answers in Lessons 15 through 17 don't just say "defend your choice," they show the actual words a strong defense uses.

## A four-category rubric

**Breadth** asks whether the presentation covered the architecture domains the scenario actually touches, or quietly skipped one — did a presentation on Carrow Equipment's dealer network address sharing architecture, integration, identity, and application architecture (B2B Commerce), or did it spend all its time on one domain and leave the others as an afterthought. **Defensibility** asks whether each major decision had a stated reason behind it, including a rejected alternative where relevant — not whether the panel happened to agree with every choice, but whether the candidate could explain *why* under direct questioning. **Communication** asks whether the opening framed the problem clearly, whether the walkthrough told one connected story rather than five disconnected topics (Lesson 8), and whether answers to questions were direct rather than rambling. **Time management** asks whether the walkthrough left genuine room for Q&A, or ran long and crowded it out (Lesson 14). Scoring a mock session against these four categories, even informally, gives feedback a shape instead of a vague overall impression.

## Giving feedback: specific, tied to the rubric, and proportionate

The strongest peer feedback names one or two concrete strengths and one or two concrete things to change next time, each tied to a rubric category and a specific moment in the session — "under Defensibility: when asked why you chose Salesforce Connect over replication for the pricing data, you answered correctly but took almost two minutes to get there; a tighter version would lead with the staleness-risk reason immediately, the way Lesson 16's model answer does." This is more useful than either an empty compliment or an unfocused list of every small thing that could have been better — the goal is a short, actionable list the candidate can actually use before their next attempt, not an exhaustive transcript review.

## Receiving feedback without getting defensive

The instinct to explain or justify a choice the moment feedback names it as a weakness is natural and usually counterproductive — the point of the feedback session is to hear the observation fully before deciding what to do with it, not to win the point in the moment. A candidate who argues down every piece of critical feedback as it's given typically walks away having absorbed less of it than one who takes notes, asks a clarifying question if the point genuinely isn't clear, and reflects afterward on which pieces of feedback to actually act on. Not every piece of feedback will be equally right — reasonable people can disagree about a design trade-off — but dismissing feedback defensively in the moment, before actually understanding it, forecloses that reflection before it happens.

## Peer review works both directions

Giving careful, rubric-based feedback to someone else's mock session sharpens your own eye for the same four categories in your own work — noticing that a peer's walkthrough told five disconnected stories instead of one connected narrative makes you more likely to catch the same pattern the next time you present. Treating peer-feedback sessions as a one-way service you're doing for someone else, rather than a skill-building exercise for yourself too, leaves real value on the table.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Breadth (rubric category) | Whether a presentation covered every architecture domain the scenario actually touches |
| Defensibility (rubric category) | Whether each major decision had a stated, explainable reason behind it |
| Communication (rubric category) | Whether the framing, narrative connectedness, and directness of answers were clear |
| Time management (rubric category) | Whether the walkthrough left genuine room for a full Q&A period |
