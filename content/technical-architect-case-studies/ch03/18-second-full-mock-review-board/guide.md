# Lesson 18 — Second Full Mock Review Board

**Chapter 3 · Presenting and Defending · Lesson 18 of 21**

## What you'll learn

- How to run a second full mock review board at a harder standard than Lesson 8's first attempt
- What's different about applying the full toolkit (Chapters 1–3 so far) versus just the format from Lesson 8
- How to choose a case study you haven't already drilled, and why that choice matters
- How to generate harder, more targeted panel questions than your first mock session

## Why a second mock session, and why now

Lesson 8 taught the review-board format itself — opening, walkthrough, Q&A — early in the course, before you had the full toolkit from Chapters 2 and 3. Running that same format now, after requirements extraction (Lesson 9), stated assumptions (Lesson 10), risk prioritization (Lesson 11), the blueprint artifacts (Lesson 12), rejected alternatives (Lesson 13), presentation pacing (Lesson 14), and three full lessons of objection-handling patterns (Lessons 15–17), is a genuinely different exercise, not a repeat. The first mock session tested whether you understood the format. This one tests whether you can actually use everything the course has given you, under the same time pressure.

## Choosing your case study deliberately

Pick a case study from Chapter 1 that you have *not* already built a full mock presentation for in Lesson 8 — if you used Meridian Outfitters' global retail scenario then, use a different one now (Alder Trust Bank, Brightwell Health, Carrow Equipment, the Public Sector DHHS case, Nimbus Mobile, or Fenwick State University). Drilling the same scenario twice teaches you to perform *that* scenario well, not to apply the method to a scenario you're approaching freshly — and the actual review-board skill is doing this well on a scenario you haven't already rehearsed to death.

## Running the full sequence this time

Unlike Lesson 8's lighter run-through, this session should actually produce the intermediate artifacts, not skip straight to the presentation: a short written pass extracting explicit requirements, implied constraints, and decorative detail (Lesson 9's technique) from your chosen scenario; two or three stated assumptions with consequences (Lesson 10); a short risk list sorted by likelihood and impact, naming which risks should visibly shape the design (Lesson 11); the four blueprint artifacts at blueprint-level detail, not implementation-level (Lesson 12); and at least one rejected alternative with an honest, non-strawmanned justification (Lesson 13). Only once these exist do you build the timed presentation outline (Lesson 14) and rehearse it.

## Generating harder questions than your first mock session

Lesson 8's questions were "four questions you predict the panel would ask based on that scenario's specific edge cases" — a reasonable start. This time, deliberately draft at least one question from each of the three objection-pattern families from Lessons 15–17: a data-or-security objection probing a trade-off or boundary case, an integration-or-scale objection probing whether a number was reasoned or guessed, and a delivery-or-governance objection probing timeline feasibility or long-term maintainability. Writing one question from each family guarantees your mock session doesn't accidentally skip an entire category of challenge just because it felt less natural to write.

## What "harder" actually means here

A harder mock session isn't about picking an artificially more complicated scenario — every Chapter 1 case study already touches multiple domains at once. It means holding yourself to the full standard from every lesson so far at once: did you actually state your assumptions instead of silently guessing, did your rejected-alternatives entry treat the alternative fairly, did your pacing protect real Q&A time, did your answers to your own planted objections apply the design's existing reasoning instead of inventing something new on the spot. Checking yourself against this full list after the session is more valuable than the session's raw runtime.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Full-toolkit mock session | A mock review board that actually produces every intermediate artifact (requirements, assumptions, risks, blueprint, rejected alternatives), not just the final presentation |
| Objection-family coverage | Deliberately drafting at least one predicted question from each objection pattern family, rather than letting questions cluster around one familiar type |
| Fresh scenario | A case study not already rehearsed in an earlier mock session, chosen specifically to test the method rather than memorized content |

## Lab

Run the session timed, then score yourself against the "what harder actually means" checklist in this lesson.
