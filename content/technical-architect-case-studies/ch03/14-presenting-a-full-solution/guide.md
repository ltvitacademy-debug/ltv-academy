# Lesson 14 — Presenting a Full Solution

**Chapter 3 · Presenting and Defending · Lesson 14 of 21**

## What you'll learn

- A realistic timing breakdown for a full review-board-style presentation and why each portion gets the time it does

## From written blueprint to live presentation

Chapter 2 built the thinking tools — extracting requirements, stating assumptions, prioritizing risk, building a blueprint, justifying alternatives. This lesson is where that thinking becomes something you actually say out loud, in order, against a clock. A well-reasoned design that never gets organized into a clear, timed presentation still fails a review board, the same way a correct answer written illegibly still loses credit — the reasoning has to survive the act of presenting it live, not just exist on paper.

## A realistic timing breakdown

A full review-board-style session built around roughly an hour commonly breaks down into a short framing opening (a few minutes, per Lesson 8), a structured walkthrough covering the blueprint artifacts in order (the bulk of the remaining prepared time), and a genuinely open Q&A period that needs real room to breathe — not five minutes tacked on at the end after the walkthrough ran long. If the walkthrough is budgeted at roughly thirty-five minutes and Q&A at twenty, a candidate who finds themselves at minute forty still presenting the integration diagram has already lost the ability to deliver a full Q&A period, regardless of how good the remaining content is. Protecting Q&A time means rehearsing the walkthrough enough times to know, concretely, how long each blueprint artifact actually takes to present — not guessing on presentation day.

## What to draw live, and what to bring pre-drawn

Whiteboarding during a live presentation serves two different purposes, and conflating them wastes time. A pre-drawn or pre-built diagram (the data model, the integration diagram) should already exist before the session starts, because redrawing a complex diagram live, stroke by stroke, burns walkthrough minutes on something the panel could have seen in ten seconds on a prepared slide. Live drawing earns its place specifically when it's responsive — sketching out the answer to a Q&A question in real time, building a small diagram the panel didn't already see, showing your reasoning as you work through something unplanned. Using live whiteboard time to redraw something already fully designed signals that preparation time wasn't used to actually prepare the visual materials, not that the candidate is being thorough.

## Pacing against the blueprint artifacts

Lesson 12's four blueprint artifacts give a natural pacing checkpoint: roughly equal time (adjusted for which domains the specific scenario weights most heavily) across the conceptual data model, the integration diagram, the security/sharing model, and the user/access model, with the security/sharing model often earning a bit more time when the scenario's highest-priority risk (per Lesson 11) lives there, as it did in the Public Sector Case Management scenario. A presentation that spends fifteen of its thirty-five walkthrough minutes on the data model and rushes the other three artifacts into the last five minutes wasn't paced against the scenario's actual risk profile — it was paced against whichever artifact the candidate happened to feel most comfortable drawing.

## Rehearsal is the only way to know your real pacing

Reading through a presentation outline silently and guessing "that'll take about thirty-five minutes" is consistently wrong in both directions — some candidates badly underestimate how long a verbal explanation takes compared to reading the same words, others over-prepare and end up rushing through content they know perfectly well out of nervousness. A stopwatch rehearsal, done out loud exactly as it would be delivered live, is the only way to find out where the real pacing problem is before it costs Q&A time in the actual session.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Walkthrough budget | The time allotted to the domain-by-domain presentation portion, distinct from opening and Q&A |
| Pre-drawn artifact | A diagram prepared before the session, shown rather than redrawn live, to protect walkthrough time |
| Responsive whiteboarding | Live drawing used specifically to answer an unplanned question, not to redisplay prepared material |
| Pacing against risk profile | Allocating more walkthrough time to the blueprint artifact tied to the scenario's highest-priority risk |
