# Lesson 21 — Governance Roadmaps

**Chapter 5 · Sustaining Governance · Lesson 21 of 25**

## What you'll learn

- Why a roadmap for a program that's already running is a different
  document from the one that launched it
- How to re-sequence a multi-year roadmap using evidence instead of a
  fixed calendar
- The "now / next / later" horizon structure programs use once they
  have more good ideas than capacity
- What breaks a roadmap's credibility a year or two in, and how to
  keep it honest

## This isn't the first roadmap anymore

Data Governance Foundations' Chapter 5 covered the roadmap a *brand
new* program needs to get off the ground: a five-phase, roughly
12-month sequence — discovery, foundation, pilot, expand, scale —
that turns one successful pilot into an organization-wide program.
That roadmap has one job: prove the program should exist.

This lesson picks up after that job is done. The program from
Chapter 1 of this course has a charter, a council, stewards, policies,
and KPIs. It's running. The question now isn't "how do we start" —
it's "what do we do in year two, and year three, and how do we decide
without just repeating last year's plan because it's comfortable."

## From a fixed sequence to a living backlog

A first-program roadmap is mostly linear because it has to be —
foundation has to exist before pilot, pilot before expand. A sustaining
roadmap looks different: it's a **backlog** of competing candidate
initiatives — a new domain to bring under governance, a policy that
needs rewriting, a tooling upgrade, a compliance deadline — that all
want the same scarce stewardship time and council attention. The
program no longer asks "what's next in the sequence." It asks "what's
worth doing next, given everything else on the list."

## The now / next / later horizon

Rather than committing firm dates two years out — which Lesson 28 in
Foundations already warned turns a roadmap into a wall poster — mature
programs sort the backlog into three horizons and re-sort it on a
fixed cadence, typically quarterly:

1. **Now (this quarter).** Committed work with a named owner and a
   milestone, exactly as disciplined as any phase in a first-program
   roadmap.
2. **Next (next one to two quarters).** Likely work, scoped enough to
   estimate, but not yet committed — commitment happens when it moves
   into "Now."
3. **Later (beyond that).** Real candidates worth tracking, intentionally
   described loosely, so the roadmap doesn't pretend to know things it
   doesn't know yet.

An item moves from Later to Next to Now as evidence accumulates — a
new regulation lands, a maturity assessment (Lesson 23) flags a weak
dimension, a KPI (Chapter 3) stalls. The roadmap becomes a record of
decisions made with evidence, not a calendar filled in at the start of
the year.

## What forces re-sequencing

A sustaining program's roadmap gets reshuffled by things a first-year
program rarely has to deal with yet:

- **Maturity gaps.** Lesson 23's assessment might show stewardship is
  solid but data quality monitoring is still ad hoc — that moves a
  quality-tooling initiative from Later into Next.
- **New domains or regulations.** An acquisition, a new product line,
  or a new compliance requirement can insert urgent work ahead of
  everything already queued.
- **Funding changes.** Lesson 22 covers this directly — a budget cut
  forces the backlog to be re-ranked by value, not just by what's
  already in motion.
- **Stakeholder turnover.** A sponsor or steward leaving (Chapter 4)
  can stall an initiative that depended entirely on one person's
  momentum.

## Keeping a multi-year roadmap honest

The same two failure modes from Foundations' Lesson 28 apply here, just
at a longer time scale and with higher stakes, because by year two or
three people have seen enough roadmaps to be skeptical of a new one.
Revisit every horizon at the same cadence you use for KPI reporting
(Chapter 3), retire items that evidence no longer supports, and show
the council what actually moved from Now to done last quarter — not
just what's planned next. A roadmap that only ever adds items and
never closes any is a wish list, not a plan.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Program backlog | The full list of candidate initiatives competing for a sustaining program's limited stewardship and council capacity |
| Now / next / later | A three-horizon structure that commits firmly to the near term while leaving further-out work intentionally loose |
| Re-sequencing | Reordering the backlog based on new evidence — a maturity gap, a regulation, a funding change — rather than an original fixed calendar |

## Lab

Take the five-phase roadmap you sketched in Foundations' Lesson 28 (or
imagine one for a program one year old). Sort its *next* set of
