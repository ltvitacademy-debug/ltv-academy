# Lesson 23 — Program Maturity Assessment

**Chapter 5 · Sustaining Governance · Lesson 23 of 25**

## What you'll learn

- Why "is governance working" needs a more structured answer than a
  KPI dashboard alone can give
- A generic five-level maturity scale you can apply to any governance
  dimension, and why it's deliberately not tied to one vendor's model
- The handful of dimensions worth scoring separately rather than
  rating the whole program with one number
- How a maturity assessment feeds directly back into the roadmap and
  funding conversations from the last two lessons

## A different question than the dashboard answers

Chapter 3 of this course covered KPIs, dashboards, and reporting to
executives — the ongoing, operational "is governance healthy this
month" view. A maturity assessment asks a different, slower-moving
question: not "is quality trending up," but "how capable is this
program, structurally, compared to where it started — and compared to
where it still needs to go." Foundations' readiness assessment
(Chapter 5) asked something similar before a program existed at all;
this is the same instinct, turned on a program that's already running
for a year or more.

## A generic five-level maturity scale

Maturity models show up across many disciplines in roughly the same
shape: a small number of ordered stages describing how deliberate and
repeatable a capability has become. There's no single official,
universally agreed "data governance maturity model" this course will
claim as authoritative — different consultancies and vendors publish
their own branded versions. What's useful, and worth learning
regardless of whose exact labels get used, is the general shape:

1. **Ad hoc.** Governance activity exists, but inconsistently — some
   teams document ownership, most don't, and nothing repeats
   reliably.
2. **Developing.** Core structures exist (a charter, a council,
   defined roles from Chapter 1) but execution is inconsistent across
   domains.
3. **Defined.** Policies, workflows, and decision rights (Chapter 2)
   are documented and generally followed, with real stewards doing
   real work.
4. **Managed.** KPIs and dashboards (Chapter 3) actively drive
   decisions; issues get escalated and resolved on a predictable
   cadence rather than by exception.
5. **Optimized.** The program improves itself — maturity gaps get
   found and closed proactively, and governance is simply how the
   organization operates, not a program people have to be reminded
   about.

Treat the five levels as a shared vocabulary for an honest
conversation, not a certification to chase for its own sake. A program
stuck at Developing that knows exactly why is in better shape than one
that's confused about which level it's even at.

## Score dimensions separately, not the program as one number

A program is rarely at the same level everywhere, and collapsing it
into a single score hides exactly the information a maturity
assessment exists to surface. Score each dimension independently:

| Dimension | What it's assessing |
|---|---|
| Stewardship | How consistently data owners and stewards (Chapter 1) are assigned and actually doing the work |
| Policy and process | How documented and consistently followed policies, workflows, and decision rights (Chapter 2) are |
| Measurement | How much KPIs and dashboards (Chapter 3) actually drive decisions versus sitting unused |
| Adoption and culture | How much governance is followed because people believe in it versus complied with reluctantly (Chapter 4) |
| Tooling | Whether the catalog, workflow, and monitoring tools in use actually support the process, or work around gaps in it |

A program that's Managed on measurement but still Ad hoc on adoption
has a very specific, very actionable gap — one a single blended score
would have hidden entirely.

## Feeding the assessment back into the program

A maturity assessment that doesn't change anything is just an exercise.
The honest use of the result feeds directly into the last two lessons:

- A weak dimension becomes a **Next** or **Now** item on the roadmap
  (Lesson 21) — not a vague aspiration, a scoped initiative with an
  owner.
- A dimension that moved up a level since the last assessment becomes
  evidence in the value realization report (Lesson 22) — proof the
  budget is producing real structural improvement, not just activity.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Maturity level | One stage in an ordered scale (ad hoc through optimized) describing how deliberate and repeatable a capability has become |
| Dimension | One specific aspect of the program — stewardship, policy, measurement, adoption, tooling — scored independently rather than blended |
| Maturity gap | The distance between a dimension's current level and the level the program needs it at, which becomes roadmap input |

## Lab

For a governance program you know (your own organization's, or a
representative one), score the five dimensions above on the one-to-five
scale, one sentence of justification each. Pick the single lowest-scoring
dimension and write what moving it up exactly one level would
concretely require — not "get better," but a specific, nameable change.
