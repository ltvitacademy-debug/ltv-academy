# Lesson 10 — Decision Rights

**Chapter 2 · Policies, Standards and Processes · Lesson 10 of 25**

## What you'll learn

- Why one-off, contested decisions need something beyond the RACI matrix from Lesson 5
- Two widely used decision-rights frameworks: DACI and RAPID
- How to apply decision rights to the governance decisions that actually cause friction
- Why this lesson closes the loop on everything Chapter 2 built

## Why RACI alone isn't quite enough

Lesson 5's RACI matrix is built for recurring *activities* — drafting a policy, fixing a quality
issue — where the same role plays the same part every time. Some governance moments are different:
a one-off or recurring **contested decision** where reasonable people disagree and the organization
needs absolute clarity on who actually has the final say, separate from who did the analysis or
who has to live with the outcome. "Should we grant this policy exception?" and "which domain gets
the steward we can afford to hire this quarter?" are decisions, not activities — and they benefit
from a sharper tool than RACI's Accountable.

## Two real decision-rights frameworks

- **DACI** (Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed) — a lightweight framework, popularized by
  Atlassian, suited to everyday decisions. The **Driver** runs the process and gathers input, the
  **Approver** makes the actual call, **Contributors** give input without veto power, and
  **Informed** parties are told the outcome.
- **RAPID** (Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, Decide) — a heavier framework from Bain & Company,
  suited to bigger, higher-stakes decisions. Someone **Recommends** a course of action, specific
  parties must **Agree** before it proceeds, **Input** providers are consulted, whoever will
  **Perform** the work is identified, and one person holds **Decide** — final authority, even if
  every Agree party pushed back.

Both frameworks share the same core idea RACI's "A" gestures at but doesn't fully isolate: exactly
one person or role has final say on a specific decision, named *before* the disagreement happens,
not fought over in the moment.

## Where governance decisions actually cause friction

- **Policy exception approval** — does this specific request get an exception to a standard, and
  for how long? A named Approver/Decider (often the data owner, sometimes the committee for
  cross-domain stakes) stops this from becoming a drawn-out negotiation every time.
- **Cross-domain disputes** — Foundations Lesson 16 said the committee is where these land; a
  decision-rights framework says, within the committee, who specifically breaks the tie — the
  committee chair, the sponsor, or a vote.
- **Prioritization and resourcing** — when more issues exist than capacity to fix them (Lesson 4's
  stewardship workload problem), someone has to Decide what gets attention first, with input from
  stewards and owners who don't get a veto.

## Why naming decision rights up front reduces conflict, not creates it

It can feel more democratic to leave "who decides" vague until the moment arrives — in practice,
that's when disagreements turn personal, because the fight is now about *who gets to decide*, on
top of the original question. Naming the Decider or Approver before the decision is contested
means the actual disagreement — the content of the decision — gets the committee's or owner's
attention, instead of being sidetracked by a fight over authority that should have been settled in
advance.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Decision rights | Explicit, pre-agreed clarity on who has final authority over a specific contested decision |
| DACI | Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed — a lightweight decision-rights framework |
| RAPID | Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, Decide — a heavier decision-rights framework for higher-stakes calls |
