## Picking up from Foundations

Foundations Lesson 19 defined what a data policy *is*: a high-level, implementation-agnostic
statement of what's required and why, distinct from the standards that carry the technical detail.
This lesson is about the actual document and the process that produces it — the thing Chapter 1's
structure (owners, stewards, committee, sponsor) exists to produce in the first place.

## A reusable policy document template

A real policy document, not just a sentence in a slide deck, typically has these sections:

1. **Title and policy ID** — short, specific, and trackable (not "Data Policy" — "Customer PII
   Access Policy")
2. **Purpose and rationale** — the business reason this policy exists, in plain language
3. **Scope** — exactly who and what this policy applies to, borrowing directly from the charter's
   scope statement (Lesson 1)
4. **The policy statement itself** — the actual rule, written the way Foundations Lesson 19
   described: clear, reasoned, implementation-agnostic, naming who it applies to
5. **Owner and approver** — who is Accountable for this policy per the RACI matrix (Lesson 5), and
   who formally approved it
6. **Effective date and review cycle** — when it took effect, and when it's due for review
7. **Related standards** — links to the standards (Lesson 7) that implement this policy's intent

## The drafting-review-approve-publish lifecycle

1. **Draft** — the steward typically writes the first draft, with the owner providing business
   context (matching the RACI from Lesson 5: owner Accountable, steward Responsible)
2. **Review** — relevant stakeholders from Lesson 2's analysis weigh in — legal and compliance for
   regulatory language, IT for feasibility
3. **Approve** — the governance committee (Lesson 3) formally approves; anything with
   organization-wide weight also needs the sponsor's backing
4. **Publish** — the policy goes into a central, discoverable location — a policy nobody can find
   might as well not exist
5. **Review on cadence** — scheduled re-review, not an assumption it stays correct forever

## Common pitfalls that weaken a policy

- **Too implementation-specific** — naming a specific tool or technique inside the policy itself
  means the policy expires the moment that tool changes; that detail belongs in a standard
- **No named owner** — a policy with no accountable owner has no one to update it, defend it, or
  answer for it when it's challenged
- **No stated scope** — vague about who it applies to is functionally unenforceable (Foundations
  Lesson 19 made this point directly)
- **No review cadence** — a policy that's never scheduled for re-review quietly goes stale as the
  business, regulations, or systems change underneath it

## Why review cadence matters as much as the first draft

A policy approved once and never revisited eventually describes an organization that no longer
exists — systems get replaced, regulations change, domains merge. Building a review date into the
policy itself (annually is common, more often for regulation-heavy domains) is what keeps the
policy a living document the program actually trusts, instead of an artifact from a kickoff meeting
two years ago that nobody's checked since.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Policy lifecycle | The draft-review-approve-publish-review cycle a policy moves through from first idea to living document |
| Policy ID | A short, specific, trackable name/identifier distinguishing one policy document from another |
| Review cadence | The scheduled interval at which an approved policy is re-examined for continued accuracy |
