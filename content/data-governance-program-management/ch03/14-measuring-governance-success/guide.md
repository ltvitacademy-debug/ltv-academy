# Lesson 14 — Measuring Governance Success

**Chapter 3 · Measuring Governance · Lesson 14 of 25**

## What you'll learn

- Why "success" means something different depending on who in the organization you ask
- How to combine the metrics from Lessons 11-13 into one honest program-level verdict
- The difference between measuring compliance and measuring outcomes
- Why a program can hit every metric target and still be failing, and the reverse

## Success means something different to each audience

Ask three people in the same organization whether governance is "succeeding" and you'll get three different answers, because they're measuring three different things:

- A **steward** considers it a success when their domain's data quality is improving and the issue backlog is manageable
- A **business sponsor** considers it a success when governance stopped being a blocker — approvals move fast, nobody's waiting weeks for access
- An **executive** considers it a success when they can trust the numbers in a board deck without someone double-checking them the night before

None of these views is wrong, and none alone is complete. A program that only optimizes for the steward's view of success can still be invisible — or actively resented — at the executive level, and vice versa. Lesson 15 covers tailoring what you report to each of these audiences specifically; this lesson is about defining success in a way that holds up across all of them.

## Combining the metrics into one verdict

Lessons 11-13 gave you KPIs, the quality/adoption split, and a dashboard to display them. None of that is "success" by itself — it's instrumentation. Turning it into a verdict means answering three honest questions on a regular cadence (usually quarterly):

1. **Did the headline metrics move in the right direction** — not just "are they green today," but did the trend actually improve since the program's baseline?
2. **Did adoption increase**, not just quality — a quality-only win without adoption movement means the organization hasn't actually changed its behavior (Lesson 12)
3. **Did issues get resolved faster, or did they just get logged faster** — a growing issue log can mean people finally trust the process enough to report problems, or it can mean the backlog is actually growing. The resolution-time trend from Lesson 11 is what tells you which.

## Compliance vs. outcomes

A subtle trap: it's much easier to measure **compliance** — did people do the thing the policy said to do — than to measure **outcomes** — did doing that thing actually make anything better. A policy acknowledgment rate of 95% is a compliance metric. It tells you people clicked "I agree." It tells you nothing about whether the behavior the policy was meant to produce actually changed.

Outcome measurement is harder because it requires connecting a governance activity to a downstream result — did assigning stewards to the Customer domain actually reduce duplicate customer records six months later? That connection takes longer to show up and is messier to prove, but it's the only kind of evidence that actually answers "is this working."

## Why hitting every target isn't the same as succeeding

A program can hit every metric target on its dashboard and still be failing in a way the dashboard doesn't show — for example, if the targets themselves were set too low to matter, or if teams learned to game a metric (closing issues by marking them "resolved" without actually fixing anything) rather than improve the underlying behavior. The reverse also happens: a program can miss several targets in its first two quarters while still being on a legitimate path to success, because change takes longer than a quarter to show up in lagging metrics. This is why the trend conversation in Lesson 15 — read over multiple quarters, with context, by a human — matters more than any single dashboard snapshot.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Compliance metric | A measure of whether people did what a policy required (e.g., acknowledgment rate) |
| Outcome metric | A measure of whether an activity actually produced the intended downstream result |
| Baseline | The starting measurement a program's progress is tracked against |
| Metric gaming | Teams optimizing the reported number without improving the underlying behavior it's meant to reflect |
