# Lesson 15 — Reporting to Executives

**Chapter 3 · Measuring Governance · Lesson 15 of 25**

## What you'll learn

- Why an executive audience needs a different report than the dashboard from Lesson 13
- The structure that works for a governance update at the steering committee or board level
- How to present bad news about the program without losing the room
- How often to report, and why cadence matters as much as content

## Why executives need a different report than the dashboard

Lesson 13's dashboard is built for someone who wants to explore — click into a tile, drill down to the detail. An executive update is the opposite: a fixed, short artifact that answers "should I still be funding this" without requiring the reader to click anything. Handing an executive a link to the live dashboard and calling it a report is a common mistake — it outsources the job of finding the story in the numbers to someone who doesn't have time to do it.

## A structure that works

A governance update to a steering committee or board typically needs four things, in this order:

1. **One-line verdict** — on track, at risk, or behind, stated plainly in the first sentence, not buried after three paragraphs of context
2. **The two or three numbers that justify that verdict** — pulled from the headline metrics in Lesson 13's dashboard, with trend direction, not just a snapshot
3. **What changed since the last update** — what moved, what didn't, and why, in plain language a non-specialist can follow
4. **What you need from this group** — a decision, a budget approval, an escalation they need to weigh in on, or nothing at all if the answer is genuinely "just keep doing what we're doing"

Everything else — the detailed KPI list, the per-domain breakdown, the issue log — belongs in an appendix or the live dashboard, available if someone asks, not forced into the main narrative.

## Presenting bad news without losing the room

Governance programs regularly have quarters where the honest verdict is "at risk" or "behind." The instinct to soften that into something more palatable is exactly how trust in a program's reporting erodes over time — once an executive catches one optimistic-sounding update that turns out to have been covering a real problem, every future "on track" gets re-read with suspicion. The better approach:

- State the honest verdict first, same as always — don't bury "behind" in paragraph four
- Pair it immediately with **why** (a staffing gap, a dependency on a system migration that slipped, a domain owner who left and hasn't been replaced) rather than letting the number speak for itself
- Bring a specific ask, not just a complaint — "we need one more steward headcount approved" is actionable; "things are hard" is not
- Don't over-promise a recovery date you're not confident in — a missed self-imposed deadline costs more trust than an honest "we don't have a firm date yet, here's what we're doing to get one"

## Cadence matters as much as content

A governance update that arrives irregularly trains its audience to stop expecting it, which makes it easy to deprioritize or cut when budgets tighten. A predictable cadence — monthly for a steering committee, quarterly for a board — keeps governance visible as a standing line item rather than something that only gets attention when there's a crisis. The content within that cadence can flex (a quiet month can be a short update), but the rhythm itself shouldn't.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Executive update | A fixed, short report answering whether a program should keep being funded, as opposed to an explorable dashboard |
| Verdict | The one-line on-track / at-risk / behind judgment that opens an executive update |
| The ask | What the reporting group specifically needs from its audience — a decision, budget, or escalation |
| Reporting cadence | How often updates go out; predictability matters as much as the content itself |
