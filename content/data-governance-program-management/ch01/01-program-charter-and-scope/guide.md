# Lesson 1 — Program Charter and Scope

## Welcome back — and where this course picks up

Data Governance Foundations covered what governance *is*: decision rights, roles like owners and
stewards, councils and committees, policies and standards, at the concept level. This course picks
up exactly where that one left off and asks the practical question Foundations didn't have room
for: given all of that, how do you actually **launch** a governance program at a real organization,
keep it running, and prove it's working? Chapter 1 starts with the single document that makes a
governance effort real instead of aspirational: the program charter.

## What a program charter actually is

A **program charter** is the formal document that authorizes a data governance program to exist.
It is not a project plan and it is not a slide deck for one meeting — it's the reference document
the program points back to whenever someone asks "who said we're allowed to do this?" or "why are
we governing this domain and not that one?" Without a charter, a governance effort is a set of
good intentions that any new executive, budget cycle, or reorg can quietly cancel, because nothing
written down ever said it was supposed to exist in the first place.

## The core elements every charter needs

1. **Purpose and vision** — the problem the program exists to solve, stated in business terms
   ("conflicting revenue numbers across three systems are slowing month-end close"), not governance
   jargon.
2. **Scope** — exactly which data domains, systems, and business units are covered in this phase,
   and just as importantly, what is explicitly *out* of scope for now.
3. **Sponsor and authority** — the named executive sponsor (Foundations Lesson 18 covered this role
   conceptually) whose backing the charter carries, and the governance body — a council or committee
   (Foundations Lesson 16) — that has authority to approve policy under this charter.
4. **Objectives tied to business outcomes** — not "improve data quality" in the abstract, but
   specific, checkable goals: a named report reconciles across systems, a named regulatory
   obligation is met, a named onboarding process shortens.
5. **Success criteria and timeline** — how the program will know, by a stated date, whether it did
   what the charter said it would do.

## Why scope is where most programs actually fail

The single most common governance program failure isn't bad policy writing or weak tooling — it's
**scope that tries to cover everything at once**. A charter that says "govern all enterprise data"
gives the program no way to ever say it succeeded, and no way to prioritize when there's more work
than time. The fix is a deliberately phased scope statement:

- **In scope, phase one** — name the two or three highest-value data domains (customer, product,
  financial transactions — whatever matters most to this organization) and the specific systems
  that hold them.
- **Explicitly out of scope, for now** — name what the program is *not* touching yet, and say so in
  writing. This isn't a weakness; it's what keeps the charter honest and keeps stakeholders from
  assuming the program owns problems it hasn't gotten to.
- **Phase two and beyond** — a stated (even rough) plan for what gets added once phase one proves
  the model works.

A scope statement that can't be pointed to and checked against — "yes, that domain is in scope;
no, that one isn't yet" — isn't really a scope statement.

## Who drafts and approves the charter

The executive sponsor and the program lead typically draft the charter together: the sponsor brings
organizational weight and the authority to name what's in scope, while the program lead brings the
operational detail. The governance council or steering committee then formally approves it — the
same body Foundations Lesson 16 described as the standing cross-functional forum above individual
data owners. A charter nobody with real authority approved carries no more weight than an email.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Program charter | The formal document authorizing a governance program, naming its purpose, scope, sponsor, and success criteria |
| Scope statement | The explicit list of what is, and is not, covered by the program in its current phase |
| Executive sponsor | The senior leader whose authority backs the charter (introduced conceptually in Foundations Lesson 18) |
| Phased scope | Deliberately limiting phase one to a small number of high-value domains, with a stated plan to expand later |

## Lab

Draft a one-page program charter for your own organization (or a hypothetical mid-sized company).
Include: a one-sentence purpose tied to a real business pain point, a phase-one scope naming two or
three specific data domains, an explicit "out of scope for now" list, the named sponsor and
approving body, and one measurable success criterion with a date attached.
