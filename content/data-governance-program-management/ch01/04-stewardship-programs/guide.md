# Lesson 4 — Stewardship Programs

**Chapter 1 · Launching the Program · Lesson 4 of 25**

## What you'll learn

- Why naming a steward role (Foundations Lesson 14) isn't the same as running a stewardship program
- How to recruit and allocate real time for stewards instead of treating it as free extra work
- What a steward community of practice does and why it keeps the program from stalling
- How to track steward workload so the program notices when stewardship is failing

## A named role isn't a running program

Foundations Lesson 14 defined the steward role: the person doing the day-to-day work of defining,
monitoring, and improving data quality within a domain. That's a role definition. A **stewardship
program** is the operational machinery that makes the role actually function across every domain
in scope — recruiting the right people, giving them real time, supporting them with peers facing
the same problems, and noticing when the workload is too much for the time they've been given.
Most governance programs that stall don't fail because nobody understood what a steward was
supposed to do. They fail because stewardship was named as a role and never built as a program.

## Recruiting stewards deliberately

A steward is almost always someone with an existing job who takes this on *in addition* — rarely
a brand-new hire. That means recruitment is really a negotiation with the steward's manager, and
it needs three things to work:

- **The right person** — deep working knowledge of the domain's data, not just willingness to
  volunteer. The best data analyst for a domain is often the best steward candidate for it.
- **Explicit time allocation** — a stated percentage of their week (commonly somewhere in the 10–20%
  range depending on domain size), written into their role description, not left as "whenever you
  have a spare hour."
- **Manager buy-in** — the steward's actual manager has to agree the time allocation is real and
  won't quietly get absorbed by their primary job's deadlines the first time both compete.

## Supporting stewards with a community of practice

Stewards for different domains face strikingly similar problems — unclear ownership of an edge
case, a data quality issue nobody's sure how to classify, a stakeholder pushing back on a new rule.
A **steward community of practice** — a recurring, informal forum where stewards across domains
compare notes — does real work that policy documents can't:

- Shares solutions so each steward isn't solving the same problem alone
- Builds consistency across domains, since stewards who never talk to each other tend to drift
  toward different standards for similar situations
- Gives stewards (who often lack much positional authority on their own) a peer network that
  makes the role feel less isolated

## Tracking steward workload

A stewardship program that never measures how much work its stewards are actually carrying can't
tell the difference between "stewardship is working" and "stewards are quietly falling behind and
nobody's noticed yet." Track, at minimum: how many open data quality issues each steward is
carrying, how long issues sit before resolution, and whether the time allocation negotiated at
recruitment still matches the actual workload. When it doesn't, that's a signal for the program —
and often the executive sponsor — to either add stewards or formally increase allocated time,
not a signal to just expect the steward to absorb it.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stewardship program | The operational machinery — recruiting, time allocation, support, tracking — that makes the steward role function across every in-scope domain |
| Time allocation | The explicit, negotiated percentage of a steward's week set aside for stewardship work |
| Community of practice | A recurring, informal peer forum where stewards across domains compare problems and solutions |
