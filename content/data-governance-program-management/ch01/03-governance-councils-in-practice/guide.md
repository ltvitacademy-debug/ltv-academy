# Lesson 3 — Governance Councils in Practice

**Chapter 1 · Launching the Program · Lesson 3 of 25**

## What you'll learn

- The operational details Foundations' conceptual council/committee structure left out
- How to select council membership deliberately, not just by title
- A workable meeting cadence and agenda structure
- How decisions actually get made, and how they get documented

## From concept to operation

Foundations Lesson 16 explained *why* a layered council and committee structure exists: individual
data owners can't resolve cross-domain disputes alone, so there needs to be a standing,
cross-functional body above them. This lesson is about what that looks like on a Tuesday morning —
who's actually in the room, what's on the agenda, and how a real decision gets made and recorded.

## Selecting membership deliberately

A council assembled by "whoever's available" tends to either rubber-stamp everything or get stuck
in constant disagreement. A working council needs:

- **Enough functional range** to resolve real disputes — business, IT, legal, compliance, security —
  without pulling in outside specialists every time (the same range Foundations Lesson 16
  described for the committee layer)
- **Real decision authority** — members who can commit their function to a decision, not just
  relay it back to someone else who will overrule them later
- **A manageable size** — large enough to cover the needed range, small enough to actually reach a
  decision in a meeting; most working councils land somewhere around six to ten voting members

## A workable meeting cadence

- **Council (strategic layer)** — typically quarterly, focused on budget, priorities, and
  escalations the committee couldn't resolve
- **Committee (operational layer)** — typically monthly or every two weeks, focused on policy
  approval, cross-domain issues, and steward escalations
- **Working groups** — as frequently as the domain's work requires, often weekly during active
  policy drafting

A council that meets too rarely becomes a rubber stamp for decisions already made elsewhere; one
that meets too often turns into a standing meeting nobody prepares for.

## A working agenda structure

A council or committee meeting that runs well tends to follow the same shape every time, so
members know what to expect and come prepared:

1. **Standing items** — a short review of open issues and metrics (Chapter 3 of this course covers
   governance metrics in depth)
2. **Decisions needed** — items with a specific ask: approve this policy, resolve this dispute,
   assign this domain an owner
3. **Escalations** — issues a working group or steward couldn't resolve at their level (Lesson 9
   covers escalation paths directly)
4. **New business** — anything not yet triaged into the above

Items without a clear decision attached shouldn't be on the agenda at all — they belong in a status
update, not a meeting.

## How decisions actually get made and recorded

Some councils vote formally; many operate by consensus with the chair (often the executive sponsor
or program lead) breaking ties. Either way, two things matter regardless of method: the decision
has to be **recorded** — who decided what, and when — and it has to be **traceable** back to the
charter's scope and the policy or standard it affects. A decision nobody wrote down is a decision
that will be re-argued from scratch the next time it comes up.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Council membership | The deliberately chosen set of people with real decision authority sitting on the body |
| Meeting cadence | How often each layer (council, committee, working group) actually convenes |
| Decision log | The record of what was decided, by whom, and when — the artifact that prevents re-litigating settled questions |
