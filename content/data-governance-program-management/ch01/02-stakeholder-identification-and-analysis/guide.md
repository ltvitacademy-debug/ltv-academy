# Lesson 2 — Stakeholder Identification and Analysis

**Chapter 1 · Launching the Program · Lesson 2 of 25**

## What you'll learn

- Why a governance program needs a deliberate stakeholder list, not just "whoever shows up"
- How to identify stakeholders across business, IT, legal, and leadership
- The power/interest grid — a standard technique for analyzing and prioritizing stakeholders
- How to turn the analysis into an engagement strategy for each group

## Why "whoever shows up" isn't a stakeholder list

Lesson 1's charter named a scope — specific data domains and systems. That scope automatically
creates a group of people with something at stake: people who own that data, people who depend on
it, people who must approve how it's governed, and people who could quietly undermine the program
if nobody ever talked to them. A program that identifies stakeholders informally, by who happens to
show up to meetings, reliably misses the people whose objection shows up *after* a policy is
written — which is the most expensive time to discover it.

## Identifying stakeholders across the organization

A thorough stakeholder pass looks in four directions:

1. **Data owners and stewards** — the roles Foundations Chapters 3 covered conceptually. They have
   direct authority and day-to-day responsibility over the domains in scope.
2. **Business units that consume the data** — finance, sales, operations, or any team that reports
   on or acts on the data, even if they don't own it.
3. **IT, security, legal, and compliance** — the functions that must weigh in on feasibility, risk,
   and regulatory obligation before a policy becomes real.
4. **Leadership and external parties** — the executive sponsor (Foundations Lesson 18), the
   governance council (Foundations Lesson 16), and, where relevant, external auditors or
   regulators who will eventually ask the program to prove it works.

The output of this step is a **stakeholder register** — a simple list naming each stakeholder, what
they care about, and how the program's scope affects them.

## Analyzing stakeholders: the power/interest grid

Not every stakeholder needs the same attention. A widely used technique for prioritizing who gets
it is the **power/interest grid**, which plots each stakeholder on two axes:

- **Power** — how much influence or authority this person has over whether the program succeeds
- **Interest** — how much this person cares about, or is affected by, the program's outcome

Plotting stakeholders on this grid produces four groups, each with a different engagement
approach:

| Quadrant | Power | Interest | Engagement approach |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manage closely | High | High | Direct, frequent engagement — these are often your sponsor and council members |
| Keep satisfied | High | Low | Periodic, high-level updates — enough that they stay supportive without being burdened |
| Keep informed | Low | High | Regular detail — stewards and analysts who need to know what's changing |
| Monitor | Low | Low | Light-touch awareness — track them, but don't over-invest attention |

## Turning analysis into an engagement strategy

The grid only has value if it changes what you actually do. For each stakeholder (or group),
decide: how often do they hear from the program, through what channel, and what do you need from
them in return — approval, input, awareness, or nothing yet. A VP in the "manage closely" quadrant
who only gets a generic monthly email is being under-engaged relative to their power to help or
block the program; a frontline analyst in "keep informed" who gets pulled into every steering
committee meeting is being over-engaged relative to what they actually need.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stakeholder register | A documented list of everyone affected by, or able to influence, the governance program |
| Power/interest grid | A technique plotting stakeholders by their influence and their level of interest to prioritize engagement |
| Engagement strategy | The specific plan — frequency, channel, ask — for how the program interacts with a given stakeholder |
