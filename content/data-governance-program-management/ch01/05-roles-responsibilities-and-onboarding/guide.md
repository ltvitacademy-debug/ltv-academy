# Lesson 5 — Roles, Responsibilities and Onboarding

**Chapter 1 · Launching the Program · Lesson 5 of 25**

## What you'll learn

- How to use a RACI matrix to turn Foundations' individual roles into one clear picture
- Why "everyone's a little bit responsible" is the same as nobody being responsible
- A practical onboarding sequence for someone stepping into a governance role
- How this closes the loop on everything Chapter 1 built

## Why you need one picture of all the roles together

Chapters 3 of Foundations introduced owners, stewards, councils, and sponsors one at a time. In a
running program, the real question isn't "what does a steward do" in isolation — it's "when this
specific activity happens, who does what, and who actually has final say." A **RACI matrix** is the
standard tool for answering that question across every role at once: each activity gets exactly one
**R**esponsible (does the work), one **A**ccountable (owns the outcome and signs off), as many
**C**onsulted (give input beforehand) as needed, and as many **I**nformed (told after the fact) as
needed.

## A worked example

| Activity | Data Owner | Steward | Committee | Sponsor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Draft a new data policy | A | R | C | I |
| Resolve a cross-domain dispute | C | C | A/R | I |
| Fix a specific data quality issue | I | R | I | — |
| Approve the program charter | C | — | C | A |

Reading this: for drafting a policy, the steward does the writing (R) but the owner is accountable
for the result (A) — the committee weighs in (C), the sponsor is told once it's done (I). The exact
assignments will differ by organization, but the discipline is the same: **every row has exactly
one A.**

## Why "everyone's a little responsible" fails

The single most common RACI mistake is assigning Accountable to more than one role, or to none at
all, because it feels more collaborative. It isn't — it's the opposite. When a data quality issue
sits unresolved for weeks, "everyone's a little responsible" means nobody is actually responsible,
and the issue has nowhere specific to land. A single named Accountable doesn't mean that person
does all the work alone; it means there is exactly one person whose job it is to make sure the
activity actually gets done, even if the Responsible party does the hands-on work.

## Onboarding someone into a governance role

Defining the role on paper and actually getting someone productive in it are different problems.
A practical onboarding sequence:

1. **Role description in writing** — what this role is Responsible/Accountable for, referencing
   the RACI matrix directly, not a vague mission statement
2. **Context, not just process** — a walkthrough of the program charter's scope and why this
   domain matters, so the new owner or steward understands the "why" behind what they're being
   asked to do
3. **Tools and access** — whatever system(s) the role needs to actually do the work (issue
   tracker, data catalog, policy repository)
4. **A shadow period** — pairing with whoever held the role before, or with a steward in a similar
   domain, before taking on issues solo
5. **A formal, visible announcement** — the same legitimacy point Foundations Lesson 16 made about
   committees formally empowering owners and stewards: a role nobody announced isn't a role anyone
   else will respect under pressure

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| RACI matrix | A tool assigning Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed roles to each governance activity |
| Accountable (the "A") | The single person who owns the outcome and must sign off — exactly one per activity, never zero or more than one |
| Onboarding sequence | The practical steps — role description, context, tools, shadowing, announcement — that get a new person productive in a role |
