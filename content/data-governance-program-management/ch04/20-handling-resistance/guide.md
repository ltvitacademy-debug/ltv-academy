# Lesson 20 — Handling Resistance

**Chapter 4 · Adoption and Change · Lesson 20 of 25**

## What you'll learn

- Why resistance shows up even when communication and training were both done well
- The common root causes behind resistance, and why "they just don't get it" is rarely the real one
- A practical approach to working through resistance instead of overriding it
- When escalation or enforcement is the right call, and when it backfires

## Resistance is normal, not a failure

Even a governance rollout with a well-built stakeholder map (Lesson 17), a thoughtful communication plan (Lesson 18), and solid training (Lesson 19) will still run into resistance. That's not a sign something went wrong upstream — it's a normal part of the adoption arc from Lesson 16. Some portion of any affected group will sit in "aware but resistant" for a while no matter how well the first three lessons were executed. The question isn't how to prevent resistance entirely; it's how to work through it instead of either ignoring it or steamrolling it.

## What's actually behind resistance

"They just don't get it" is the laziest explanation for resistance, and it's rarely true. More common root causes:

- **Extra work with no visible benefit to them** — the new process genuinely adds steps to someone's day, and the payoff (better data quality, faster audits) accrues to the organization or to someone else, not to them directly
- **A past bad experience** — a previous governance effort, or a different top-down mandate entirely, was poorly run, and people are generalizing from that experience
- **Genuine process gaps** — the new workflow really doesn't handle their specific edge case well, and what looks like stubbornness is actually a legitimate design flaw nobody's listening to
- **Loss of informal control** — governance often formalizes a decision that someone used to make informally; resistance can be less about the new process and more about losing that autonomy

Each of these needs a different response. Treating all resistance as the first type (just needs convincing) when it's actually the third type (a real process gap) means the resistance never actually resolves — it just gets suppressed until it resurfaces somewhere else.

## Working through it instead of overriding it

- **Ask before assuming** — a direct conversation ("what specifically makes this hard for you?") surfaces the real root cause far faster than guessing from the outside
- **Separate the legitimate complaint from the delivery** — someone can raise a real process gap in a frustrated or unproductive way; don't let the tone cause you to dismiss a valid point
- **Fix what's actually fixable** — if the resistance traces back to a genuine process gap, route it back through the governance workflow (Lesson 8) as a real change request, not just a complaint to manage
- **Use a visible early adopter**, not a mandate, where possible — peer influence from someone in the same role who's already using the process successfully usually does more than another message from the governance team
- **Be honest about what won't change** — if a concern can't be addressed (the process really does add work, and that trade-off was a deliberate decision), say so plainly rather than implying it might still change

## When escalation or enforcement is the right call

Working through resistance has limits. If a legitimate process gap has been addressed, the communication and training were adequate, and a specific individual or group still refuses to comply for reasons that amount to "I don't want to," that's the point to escalate through the governance council's decision rights (Lesson 10) rather than keep negotiating indefinitely. Escalating too early — before genuinely trying to understand the resistance — burns trust and teaches people that raising concerns gets them labeled as a blocker. Escalating too late, after a pattern of legitimate concerns has been ignored for months, does the same damage in the other direction. The judgment call is distinguishing "won't" from "can't yet," and that distinction only comes from actually listening first.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Root cause (of resistance) | The actual underlying reason someone is resisting, as opposed to the assumed one |
| Early adopter | A peer who has already successfully adopted a change and can influence others by example |
| Legitimate process gap | A real flaw in a new process that resistance is correctly pointing to, not just complaining about |
| Escalation (for resistance) | Moving a stalled case to the governance council's decision rights when good-faith efforts haven't resolved it |
