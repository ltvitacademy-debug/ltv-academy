# Lesson 19 — Training and Data Literacy

## What you'll learn

## Awareness isn't the same as ability

Lesson 18's communication plan can successfully make every affected person aware that a new process exists and why it matters. That still isn't enough to produce adoption, because awareness and ability are different problems. Someone can fully agree that a new data-intake workflow matters and still not know how to actually submit a request through it, what fields are required, or who to contact when something doesn't fit the standard case. **Training** is what closes that specific gap — not convincing people the process matters (that's communication's job), but teaching them exactly how to do it.

## Process training vs. data literacy

These two get conflated, but they're different layers:

- **Process training** is narrow and tactical: how to use this specific tool, how to follow this specific workflow, what this specific field means. It's tied to one governance process and goes stale when that process changes.
- **Data literacy** is broader and more durable: understanding what a data domain is, why definitions matter, how to read a quality scorecard, what a steward actually does. It's foundational knowledge that makes every future process training land faster, because people aren't learning governance vocabulary for the first time every single rollout.

A program that only ever does process training keeps re-teaching the same foundational concepts from scratch with every new initiative. A program that invests in baseline data literacy — even a short, generic session on "what governance means here and why" — makes every subsequent process training shorter and more effective, because the vocabulary and the "why" are already established.

## Designing training people actually retain

A single one-hour training session, delivered once, is one of the least effective ways to produce lasting behavior change — attendees forget most of it within weeks, and a new hire who joins afterward never gets it at all. More durable approaches:

- **Short, task-specific materials available on demand** — a two-minute walkthrough of "how to submit an intake request," findable when someone actually needs to do it, beats a slide they saw once months earlier
- **Hands-on practice, not just a lecture** — having someone actually submit a test request in the new system, with a real person available to help, sticks far better than watching a demo
- **Built into onboarding**, not just the initial rollout — so new hires get the same grounding without a separate catch-up effort
- **Reinforced at the moment of use** — a tooltip, a short reference card, or a help link embedded in the actual tool, right where someone needs it, not buried in a training portal they'd have to remember to go search

## Why data literacy pays off beyond governance compliance

Baseline data literacy — understanding what data quality means, how to read a dashboard, how to think about a metric's definition — is useful to people whether or not they ever interact with the formal governance process directly. An analyst who understands why a consistent definition of "active customer" matters makes better decisions even outside any governed workflow. This is part of why data literacy investment tends to be easier to justify to a skeptical stakeholder than process-specific training: it's a durable organizational capability, not just compliance overhead.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Process training | Narrow, tactical instruction on how to use one specific governance tool or workflow |
| Data literacy | Broader, durable understanding of data concepts that makes every future process training land faster |
| Reinforcement at point of use | Help embedded directly in the tool or moment someone needs it, instead of a training session held once |
| Awareness vs. ability | Knowing a process exists and matters (communication's job) vs. knowing how to actually do it (training's job) |
