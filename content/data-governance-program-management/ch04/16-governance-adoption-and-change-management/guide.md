## Governance is a change problem wearing a technology costume

Chapters 1-3 covered building the program: charters, councils, policies, metrics, dashboards. None of that produces adoption by itself. A governance program can have a perfectly designed operating model, a well-written policy, and an accurate dashboard, and still fail — because adoption isn't a property of the program design. It's a property of whether people actually change what they do every day, and people don't change their daily habits because a policy document told them to.

This is the uncomfortable truth every governance lead eventually runs into: governance is fundamentally a **change-management problem** that happens to involve data. The technical and procedural work from Chapters 1-3 is necessary, but it's the easy half. Getting a few hundred or a few thousand people to actually use a business glossary, actually route new data sources through intake, actually stop keeping a shadow spreadsheet — that's the hard half, and it's what this chapter is about.

## The stages people move through

Whatever specific change model an organization uses (and there are several well-known ones, each with its own vocabulary), most describe some version of the same arc when a new governance process arrives:

1. **Unaware** — people don't know the process exists yet, or don't know it applies to them
2. **Aware but resistant** — they've heard about it and see it as extra work, a blocker, or someone else's problem
3. **Compliant** — they do the minimum required, often grudgingly, because it's now mandatory
4. **Engaged** — they've internalized why it matters and start doing more than the minimum without being asked

Most governance programs get stuck between stages 2 and 3 — people comply because they have to, not because they've bought in. A program that stalls at "compliant" is fragile: the moment enforcement slips, behavior reverts. Lesson 20 covers specifically what to do when a group gets stuck at stage 2.

## Why mandate alone doesn't produce adoption

A governance council can mandate a process. It cannot mandate that people believe in it, and belief is what separates "compliant" from "engaged." Mandate alone tends to produce exactly enough behavior change to avoid consequences and no more — the minimum viable compliance, performed under observation and abandoned the moment attention moves elsewhere. Real adoption requires the things the rest of this chapter covers: understanding who's actually affected (Lesson 17), communicating clearly and repeatedly why it matters to them specifically (Lesson 18), giving people the skills to actually do the new thing (Lesson 19), and having a real plan for the people who push back (Lesson 20).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Change management | The discipline of helping people move from resisting a change to adopting it, as opposed to just mandating the change |
| Adoption stage | Where a given group sits on the unaware → resistant → compliant → engaged arc |
| Minimum viable compliance | Doing exactly enough to avoid consequences, without any real behavior change |
| Mandate | A top-down requirement to follow a process, which by itself produces compliance but not engagement |
