# Lesson 17 — Stakeholder Management

**Chapter 4 · Adoption and Change · Lesson 17 of 25**

## What you'll learn

- Why stakeholder management for adoption is different from the stakeholder identification you did in Chapter 1
- A simple way to map stakeholders by influence and how affected they are by a specific change
- What each quadrant of that map needs from you, differently
- How to keep the map current as a governance program evolves

## Different from Chapter 1's stakeholder work

Lesson 2 covered identifying and analyzing stakeholders when launching the program — a broad, one-time pass to figure out who needs a seat at the table. This lesson is narrower and ongoing: for any *specific* governance change (a new intake workflow, a policy rollout, a new mandatory field), who is actually affected, and what does each of them specifically need from you to move toward adoption? The Chapter 1 stakeholder list tells you who's in the room. This lesson is about what happens to each of them once a specific change lands on their desk.

## Mapping by influence and impact

A simple, durable way to organize this: for a given change, plot each affected group on two axes — how much **influence** they have over whether the change succeeds (can they block it, fund it, or set an example others follow), and how much **impact** the change has on their day-to-day work. That produces four groups:

- **High influence, high impact** — these people can make or break adoption, and the change directly affects them. They need the most direct, personal attention: one-on-one conversations, early involvement in the rollout design, a real chance to shape it before it's final.
- **High influence, low impact** — they can affect whether the change succeeds, but it doesn't touch their own daily work much. They need to be kept informed and occasionally asked to use their influence (an executive sponsor publicly backing the change, for instance), but don't need the same depth of hand-holding.
- **Low influence, high impact** — the change affects their daily work a lot, but they can't individually block or accelerate it. This is usually the largest group — the analysts and individual contributors who actually have to use the new process. They need clear training (Lesson 19) and a visible channel to raise problems, even though no single one of them can stop the rollout.
- **Low influence, low impact** — minimal direct attention needed; a general announcement is usually enough.

## What each quadrant actually needs

The mistake most governance leads make is treating every stakeholder the same way — one all-hands announcement for everyone. That wastes the high-influence/high-impact group's patience with a message too generic to address their specific concerns, while giving the low-influence/high-impact group (who outnumber everyone else) too little practical detail to actually do the new thing. Tailoring effort to the map isn't extra work for its own sake — it's recognizing that a thousand-person rollout and a five-person executive conversation are different problems that happen to share a policy document.

## Keeping the map current

A stakeholder map drawn once at the start of a rollout goes stale. People change roles, a quiet skeptic turns out to have real influence once the change starts affecting their team, a champion you were counting on moves to another department. Revisit the map at the same cadence as the communication plan in Lesson 18 — not constantly, but often enough that the map still reflects who's actually in each quadrant today, not who was there when the project kicked off.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Influence | A stakeholder's ability to help a specific change succeed or block it |
| Impact | How much a specific change affects a stakeholder's own day-to-day work |
| Stakeholder map | A chart plotting affected groups by influence and impact to tailor engagement effort |
| Champion | A stakeholder who actively supports and models a change for others, beyond just complying with it |

## Lab

Pick one specific governance change (real or from an earlier lab — a new intake workflow, a mandatory glossary field). List four real or realistic stakeholder groups, one per quadrant of the influence/impact map, and write one sentence per group on what that group specifically needs from you to move toward adoption.

Can you draw the influence/impact map from memory and name what each quadrant needs? Can you explain why treating every stakeholder group the same way with one generic announcement usually fails both the highest-influence and highest-impact groups?
