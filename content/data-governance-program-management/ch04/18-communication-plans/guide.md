# Lesson 18 — Communication Plans

**Chapter 4 · Adoption and Change · Lesson 18 of 25**

## What you'll learn

- Why a single announcement email is not a communication plan
- The elements a real communication plan needs for a governance change
- How to tailor message and channel to the stakeholder map from Lesson 17
- How to know if the plan is actually working, not just that it was sent

## One email is not a plan

The most common failure mode in governance rollouts is treating communication as a single event: one announcement email, sent once, to everyone, the week the new process goes live. By the time most people read it (if they read it at all), they've forgotten it within a week, and the first time the new process actually affects their work — weeks or months later — they have no memory of why it exists or what they're supposed to do. A **communication plan** is not an announcement. It's a repeated, varied set of touches over time, built around the stakeholder map, not just the launch date.

## What a real plan needs

A communication plan built for adoption, not just notification, needs:

- **Multiple touches over time**, not one — a heads-up before launch, a reminder at launch, a follow-up a few weeks in once people have actually tried the new process and have real questions
- **A message tailored to each audience**, drawing on Lesson 17's map — a high-influence executive needs the business case and risk framing; a low-influence, high-impact analyst needs the practical "here's exactly what changes in your daily workflow" version
- **More than one channel** — email, a team meeting, a short recorded walkthrough, office hours for questions — because any single channel reaches only a fraction of people reliably
- **A two-way path**, not just broadcast — a place for people to ask questions or flag confusion, which also doubles as an early-warning system for the resistance Lesson 20 covers
- **A visible owner** — a name people can actually reach, not a shared inbox that feels like shouting into the void

## Tailoring to the stakeholder map

Lesson 17's four quadrants need genuinely different communication, not just a different subject line on the same email:

| Quadrant | What the message should emphasize |
|---|---|
| High influence, high impact | The business case, their role in shaping the rollout, early access to ask questions privately |
| High influence, low impact | A concise heads-up and what you need from them (a public endorsement, approval) |
| Low influence, high impact | Exactly what changes in their daily workflow, with concrete before/after examples |
| Low influence, low impact | A short, informational note — no action expected |

Sending the executive-framing message to the analyst audience (or vice versa) wastes both groups' attention — one gets buried in business-case language they don't need, the other never gets the practical detail they actually need to act on.

## How to know the plan is working

A communication plan that was "sent" isn't the same as one that "worked." Signals worth tracking: are people actually asking questions through the two-way channel (silence usually means confusion or disengagement, not understanding), is the adoption-stage placement from Lesson 16 actually moving for the groups you targeted, and are the same basic questions still coming up weeks after the "launch" touch (a sign the message didn't land the first time and needs repeating, not that people are being slow).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Communication plan | A repeated, multi-channel, audience-tailored set of touches over time, as opposed to a single announcement |
| Touch | One instance of reaching an audience — an email, a meeting, a recorded walkthrough |
| Two-way channel | A path for the audience to ask questions or raise concerns back, not just receive a broadcast |
| Message tailoring | Adjusting what's emphasized in a communication based on the audience's position on the stakeholder map |
