Most audiences at work are not statisticians. They are managers, executives, and operations leads, and they care about one question: what should we do? This lesson is about answering it.

Start with the decision. Before you open slides, write down what decision this audience makes, what they already know, and how much time you have. Ten minutes is about three or four ideas, not fifteen charts. Anything that does not serve the decision goes in an appendix.

Then use a four-part flow. The answer: customers with three or more support tickets churn about four times as often. The evidence: one or two charts titled with findings. The caveat: this is an association in one dataset, not proof of cause. And the ask: a two-month pilot with proactive outreach and a comparison group. The answer comes first, so nobody waits through methodology to learn why they are there.

Now translate the numbers. Percentages are precise but slippery. Frequencies are easier to picture. Our helper turns a rate into about one in N. For our illustrative churn data, that gives about one in nine for customers with no tickets, versus about one in two for those with three or more. Pair the relative comparison with the absolute gap: thirty-three point four percentage points higher.

Translate jargon too. A correlation of point two seven becomes: customers with more tickets tend to churn more, though tickets alone are far from the whole story. Statistically significant becomes: unlikely to be just chance. A confidence interval becomes: a plausible range, not a promise.

Finally, prepare for hard questions. If someone asks whether tickets cause churn, say no, they go together, and explain how you would test cause. Saying I do not know, but here is how I would find out, builds trust.
