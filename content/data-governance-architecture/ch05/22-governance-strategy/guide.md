## Architecture is the design; strategy is the plan to get there

Chapters 1 through 4 covered what a governance architecture is made of: principles, reference architectures, operating models, metadata and catalog design, security and platform choices. None of that tells anyone when to build it, in what order, with whose budget, or how to know if it's working. That's what a governance **strategy** does — it turns an architecture into a sequenced, funded, sponsored plan.

A strategy is not the same document as the architecture. The architecture describes the target state and the principles that got you there. The strategy describes the path: what gets built in year one versus year three, who pays for it, who's accountable for it landing, and what "success" will look like when someone checks back in twelve months.

## The four questions a real strategy answers

Skip any one of these and the strategy is really just a wish list:

1. **Where are we now?** An honest maturity assessment — which capabilities from the Chapter 1 capabilities map already exist, which are informal or inconsistent, and which don't exist at all.
2. **Where are we going, and why?** The target state, tied explicitly to a business outcome (a regulatory deadline, a cost reduction, an AI initiative that needs trustworthy data) — not "better governance" as its own goal.
3. **What do we build first, and in what order?** A small number of sequenced priorities, not a parallel attempt at everything. Lesson 23 turns this into an actual roadmap.
4. **How is it funded and sponsored?** A named executive sponsor and a funding model — centrally funded, charged back to business units, or some mix — decided before the first initiative starts, not negotiated mid-year when money runs out.

## Three ways governance strategies fail

- **The strategy becomes a slideshow.** A polished deck gets presented once, approved, and then never referenced again because it was never translated into a roadmap, budget line, or owner.
- **No sponsor, no budget, no strategy.** A strategy written by a governance team with no executive sponsor attached is a proposal, not a strategy — it has no mechanism to get funded or defended when priorities compete.
- **Trying to govern everything at once.** Strategies that attempt centralized governance over every domain, every platform, and every policy simultaneously stall. The operating model lessons in Chapter 2 exist precisely because most real organizations need to choose where to start and where to stay light-touch.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Governance strategy | The sequenced, funded, sponsored plan for building out a governance architecture over time |
| Maturity assessment | An honest evaluation of which governance capabilities exist today versus the target state |
| Executive sponsor | The named leader accountable for the strategy's funding and organizational backing |
| Funding model | How governance work gets paid for — centrally funded, charged back to business units, or hybrid |
