# The Parameter-Shift Rule

Classical neural networks train with backpropagation, which needs access to a differentiable computation graph. A quantum circuit, measured on real hardware, gives you no such graph — only numbers from running the circuit. The parameter-shift rule is the real, exact technique that solves this: it computes an exact gradient using only circuit evaluations, no classical backprop required.

## What you'll learn

- Why backpropagation doesn't apply to a circuit run on real quantum hardware
- The exact parameter-shift formula for a gate generated by a Pauli operator
- A full, hand-verified numeric example: computing a gradient two ways and confirming they match
- Why the shift is exactly π/2, not an arbitrarily small number like classical finite differences
- The practical cost of parameter-shift gradients compared to classical backprop

## Why you can't just backpropagate through a quantum circuit

Backpropagation needs the ability to inspect and differentiate every intermediate step of a computation. When a classical simulator runs a circuit, you technically could backprop through the simulation's math. But on real quantum hardware, you only ever get measurement outcomes — you cannot "look inside" the circuit mid-execution. You need a method for computing a gradient using only full circuit evaluations (run the whole circuit, get one number), which is exactly what the parameter-shift rule provides — and unlike classical finite-difference approximations, it gives an *exact* gradient, not an approximation, for gates generated by an operator with eigenvalues ±1/2 (true for Pauli rotation gates RX, RY, RZ).

## The formula

For a parameterized gate of the form G(θ) = e^(-iθP/2), where P is a Pauli operator (so its eigenvalues are ±1), and a cost function f(θ) built from measuring an observable after this gate, the exact gradient is:

**df/dθ = [f(θ + π/2) − f(θ − π/2)] / 2**

Notice what this says: you evaluate the *entire circuit* twice — once with the parameter shifted up by exactly π/2, once shifted down by exactly π/2 — and the gradient is just half the difference. No infinitesimal limit, no approximation error from a finite step size. This is fundamentally different from classical numerical differentiation, where smaller steps give better (but still approximate) estimates.

## Full worked example

Take the exact circuit from Lesson 5: one qubit, RY(θ) applied to |0⟩, measuring ⟨Z⟩. We showed ⟨Z⟩(θ) = cos(θ) exactly. Let θ = π/3 (60°, ≈1.0472 radians).

**Step 1 — evaluate the shifted circuits:**

- f(θ + π/2) = cos(π/3 + π/2) = cos(5π/6) ≈ **−0.8660**
- f(θ − π/2) = cos(π/3 − π/2) = cos(−π/6) ≈ **0.8660**

**Step 2 — apply the parameter-shift formula:**

df/dθ ≈ (−0.8660 − 0.8660) / 2 = −1.7320 / 2 = **−0.8660**

**Step 3 — check against the known analytic derivative.** Since f(θ) = cos(θ), the calculus derivative is df/dθ = −sin(θ). At θ = π/3: −sin(π/3) ≈ **−0.8660**.

The two methods agree to numerical precision — this was confirmed by direct computation, not estimated. This is the whole point of the parameter-shift rule: it reproduces the exact calculus answer using only two full circuit evaluations, which is all real hardware can give you.

## Why exactly π/2, not "small"

Classical finite-difference gradient estimation uses a small step ε and computes [f(θ+ε) − f(θ−ε)] / (2ε), which converges to the true derivative only as ε → 0 — and on noisy hardware, making ε too small amplifies measurement noise disastrously. The parameter-shift rule is not a finite-difference approximation at all — π/2 is a specific, exact value determined by the gate's eigenvalue structure (±1/2 for a Pauli generator), not a tunable precision knob. This is why it's exact at a large, noise-friendly shift rather than fragile at a tiny one.

## The practical cost

Each parameter needs 2 full circuit evaluations to get its gradient. A circuit with k trainable parameters needs 2k circuit evaluations for one full gradient — compare this to classical backprop, which computes gradients for *all* parameters in roughly the cost of one extra forward pass, regardless of how many parameters there are. This 2k-evaluations cost is a genuine practical bottleneck for scaling variational circuits to many parameters, and it's one more reason (alongside barren plateaus, Lesson 8) that training large variational circuits is harder than training a similarly-sized classical network.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Parameter-shift rule | A method for computing an exact quantum-circuit gradient using two circuit evaluations shifted by ±π/2 |
| Pauli rotation gate | A gate like RX, RY, RZ generated by a Pauli operator with eigenvalues ±1, enabling the exact shift rule |
| Finite-difference approximation | A classical numerical gradient estimate using a small step size, which only approximates the true derivative |
| Circuit evaluation | One full run of a quantum circuit (real or simulated) producing one measurement/expectation-value result |
