## S1 · TITLE CARD

Last lesson drew the line between security and privacy. Privacy rules
exist to protect a specific category of data: PII, personally
identifiable information. Let's define it precisely.

## S2 · STEPS CARD (direct identifiers)

PII is information that can identify a specific person. The obvious
cases are direct identifiers — a full name, a Social Security number,
a passport number, an email address. Each one points at exactly one
person on its own, and these are the fields every privacy review
flags first.

## S3 · STEPS CARD (quasi-identifiers and Sweeney)

Quasi-identifiers are more dangerous precisely because they look
harmless. A ZIP code alone doesn't identify you. A birth date alone
doesn't either. But Latanya Sweeney's landmark research found that
just three fields together — five-digit ZIP code, full birth date,
and gender — uniquely identify a large majority of the US population.
Strip the name and you can still re-identify most people.

## S4 · STEPS CARD (PII vs personal data)

PII is a US-centric term, and it's narrower than it sounds. GDPR uses
a broader concept: personal data — any information relating to an
identified or identifiable person. That catches things PII often
doesn't: an IP address, a device ID, a cookie, location history. Under
GDPR, "is this PII" is the wrong question.

## S5 · OUTRO CARD

Next lesson draws a sharper line still: sensitive data, which can harm
a person if exposed, versus confidential data, which an organization
protects for business reasons — two overlapping but distinct categories
that set up how Chapter 2's classification labels actually work.
