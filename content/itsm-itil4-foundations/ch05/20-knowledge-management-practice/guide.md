## The purpose: better use of information, not more of it

ITIL 4 defines the purpose of knowledge management as maintaining and
improving the effective, efficient use of information and knowledge
across the organization. The emphasis is on *use* — a company can
have mountains of documentation and still manage knowledge badly if
nobody can find the right piece of it at the moment they need it.
Knowledge management isn't about producing more documents; it's about
making sure the useful knowledge that already exists reaches the
person who needs it, in a form they can actually apply, fast enough to
matter.

## The DIKW model

ITIL 4 uses the **DIKW model** to describe how raw material becomes
something useful: **Data** → **Information** → **Knowledge** →
**Wisdom**. Data is unprocessed facts — a server logged 400 failed
login attempts at 2 a.m. Information is data with context — those 400
attempts came from one IP address, outside business hours. Knowledge
is information combined with experience — this pattern usually means
a credential-stuffing attempt, and here's how the team has handled it
before. Wisdom is applying that knowledge with judgment to a specific
situation — deciding, given this organization's risk tolerance and
current threat landscape, exactly what to do about it right now. Each
layer adds something the previous one couldn't give you by itself.

## Knowledge as a byproduct, not a project

The practical trap in knowledge management is treating it as a
separate, heavyweight documentation project — assign someone to write
articles, and watch the backlog of "things we should document" grow
forever while nobody reads what's already written. ITIL 4 points
toward a better pattern: capture knowledge as a **byproduct** of work
that's already happening. When a major incident gets resolved, writing
up what caused it and how it was fixed costs very little extra effort
right after the fix, while the details are fresh — and that article
becomes the first thing the next responder finds when the same
symptom shows up again. The service desk, resolving the same question
for the tenth time, is also a knowledge management opportunity: that
tenth answer should become an article the ninety-first caller finds
through self-service, with no call needed at all.

## The real obstacle is behavior, not storage

Most organizations already own a wiki, a knowledge base, or a shared
drive — the technology to store knowledge is rarely the gap. The
actual obstacle is behavioral: will the person resolving an incident
take two extra minutes to write it up, and will the next person
actually search before picking up the phone? Knowledge management
succeeds or fails on exactly those two habits, tied closely into how
incident and service desk practices are actually run day to day.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Knowledge management | Maintaining and improving the effective, efficient use of information and knowledge across the organization |
| DIKW model | Data → Information → Knowledge → Wisdom — how raw facts become applied judgment |
| Knowledge article | A written record of a resolution or answer, meant to be found and reused by someone else later |
| Byproduct knowledge capture | Writing up what was learned as a small extra step during other work, instead of as a separate documentation project |
