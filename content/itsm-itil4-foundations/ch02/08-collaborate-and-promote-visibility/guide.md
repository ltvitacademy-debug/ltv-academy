# Lesson 8 — Collaborate & Promote Visibility

**Chapter 2 · The ITIL 4 Guiding Principles · Lesson 8 of 28**

## What you'll learn

- What "collaboration" means in this principle, beyond generic teamwork advice
- Why visibility of work is treated as a prerequisite for good collaboration, not a bonus
- The specific risk silos create for IT service management
- How to identify the "right" stakeholders at the "right" points in a given piece of work
- A concrete symptom of this principle being missing in an organization

## Working across the boundaries that organizations naturally create

Collaborate and Promote Visibility asks organizations to work together across boundaries,
rather than letting teams optimize only within their own function. Those boundaries show up
everywhere in a typical IT organization: a service desk team, a network team, an application
team, a security team, a vendor-management function — each with its own priorities, its own
backlog, and often its own tools. Left alone, each group naturally optimizes for its own
local metrics. A network team hits its own SLA while a customer-facing incident drags on,
because nobody owns the end-to-end picture across the boundary between teams. Collaboration,
in this principle's sense, specifically means working against that natural pull toward
silos — not simply "communicating more" in a vague, general sense.

## Visibility makes collaboration possible, not optional

Promote Visibility is the half of this principle that makes the first half achievable in
practice. Work and its outcomes need to be visible to the people who need to see them, at the
time they need to see them — not buried in one team's private ticketing queue or a spreadsheet
only one person maintains. When the state of a change, an incident, or a project is genuinely
visible, the people affected by it can act on accurate information instead of guessing or
asking around. When it's hidden, even well-intentioned collaboration breaks down, because
people are collaborating on different, outdated, or simply wrong pictures of what's actually
happening.

## A concrete symptom of this principle missing

One reliable sign that an organization is violating this principle: the same question gets
asked in three different meetings with three different answers, because each team is working
from its own private version of the current state. Another: a change gets approved by one team
without the team downstream, who'll actually be affected by it, ever hearing about it until it
breaks something. Both symptoms trace back to the same root cause — work that should have been
visible across a boundary stayed locked inside it instead.

## The right stakeholders at the right points

Collaboration doesn't mean involving everyone in everything; over-including people wastes their
time and slows decisions down just as badly as under-including them. The practical judgment
call this principle asks for is identifying which stakeholders are actually affected by, or
have relevant knowledge about, a specific piece of work — and bringing them in at the point
where their input or visibility genuinely changes the outcome, not as a courtesy afterthought.
A security reviewer consulted before a change is deployed changes what gets deployed; the same
reviewer informed after the fact only documents what already happened.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Silo | A team or function that optimizes for its own local goals, isolated from the end-to-end picture |
| Visibility | Making the state of work and its outcomes accessible to the people who need to see them, when they need it |
| Stakeholder | Anyone affected by, or holding relevant knowledge about, a specific piece of work |
| Boundary | The organizational line between teams or functions where collaboration most often breaks down |
