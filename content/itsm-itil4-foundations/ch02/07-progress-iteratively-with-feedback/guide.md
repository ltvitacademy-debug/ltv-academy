# Lesson 7 — Progress Iteratively With Feedback

**Chapter 2 · The ITIL 4 Guiding Principles · Lesson 7 of 28**

## What you'll learn

- Why ITIL 4 resists doing everything at once, even when a full redesign seems faster
- How this principle connects directly to Agile thinking
- What a feedback loop looks like inside an ITSM improvement effort
- The real risk of large, "big bang" initiatives that this principle is designed to avoid
- How iteration and the earlier principle, Start Where You Are, reinforce each other

## Resist the urge to do everything at once

Progress Iteratively With Feedback asks organizations to organize work into smaller,
manageable pieces that can be executed and completed in a timely manner, rather than
attempting one sweeping initiative that tries to fix everything simultaneously. A full
platform migration, a complete process overhaul, a total rewrite of every runbook — these
are exactly the kinds of projects that look efficient on a planning slide and turn risky the
moment real work starts. Big-bang initiatives compress all of their risk into one release
event: if something's wrong with the plan, you don't find out until most of the investment is
already spent, and by then there's little room left to course-correct cheaply.

## Smaller pieces, checked constantly

Instead, this principle pushes toward comprehending the whole, but doing something smaller
and well-defined, getting it done, checking the outcome, and adjusting before moving to the
next piece. That "checking" step is the feedback part of the principle's name, and it isn't
optional decoration — it's the mechanism that makes iteration safer than a single large
release. Feedback can come from monitoring data, from the people actually using the new
process, from a pilot group before a full rollout, or from a retrospective after a sprint of
work. Whatever its source, feedback has to actually change the next iteration's plan, or the
loop isn't really closed.

## Close cousin of Agile thinking

This principle is explicitly aligned with Agile and Lean thinking — short cycles, visible
progress, and the willingness to adjust direction based on what's actually learned rather
than what was originally planned. The connection isn't coincidental: ITIL 4 absorbed many
ideas from Agile software development when the framework was revised, recognizing that the
same logic that makes iterative software delivery safer also makes iterative service
improvement safer. A service desk rolling out a new knowledge base, for instance, might start
with one practice area, gather feedback from the agents actually using it, refine the
structure, and only then expand to the next area — rather than attempting to populate the
entire knowledge base before anyone uses any of it.

## How this builds on Start Where You Are

Progress Iteratively With Feedback only works well in combination with the previous principle.
You can't define a sensible first small piece of work without first understanding the current
state well enough to know what's actually worth tackling first. The two principles form a
sequence in practice: assess what exists, then make one deliberate, bounded change, measure
what happened, and let that result inform the next bounded change. Skipping the assessment step
and iterating blindly just produces a series of small, uninformed guesses instead of a genuine
feedback loop.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Iteration | A small, well-defined, time-boxed piece of work completed before the next piece begins |
| Feedback loop | The mechanism by which the outcome of one iteration informs the plan for the next |
| Big-bang release | A single large initiative that attempts to deliver everything at once, concentrating risk into one event |
| Pilot | A smaller-scale rollout used to gather real feedback before a full-scale rollout |
