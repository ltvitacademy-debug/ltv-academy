# Lesson 6 — Start Where You Are

**Chapter 2 · The ITIL 4 Guiding Principles · Lesson 6 of 28**

## What you'll learn

- What "Start Where You Are" actually tells you to do, and what it doesn't
- Why assessing the current state comes before any redesign decision
- The risk of discarding existing services, processes, or tools too quickly
- How to tell what's worth keeping from what's genuinely broken
- Why this principle is often misread as "never change anything"

## Assess before you redesign

Start Where You Are means: don't discard existing services, processes, measurements, or
tools without first investigating and understanding what's actually there. It's tempting,
especially for a new manager or a freshly hired consultant, to look at an unfamiliar process
and declare it broken on sight, then propose rebuilding it from scratch. ITIL 4 pushes back
on that instinct. Somewhere underneath the parts that look outdated or clumsy, there is
usually a reason the process exists the way it does — a past incident it was built to
prevent, a regulatory requirement it satisfies, a workaround for a limitation in some other
system. Understanding that history is the first real step, not an optional one.

## Measure, don't assume

The principle's practical instruction is to observe directly and measure, rather than rely on
assumptions about what's happening. If a service desk's average resolution time looks slow on
paper, the right first move isn't to redesign the workflow — it's to pull the actual data and
look at where time is genuinely being lost. Maybe most tickets resolve quickly and a handful
of outliers are dragging the average up; a redesign aimed at the "typical" ticket would miss
the real problem entirely. Starting where you are means the current state becomes evidence,
not a guess to be waved away in a slide deck.

## This is not "never change anything"

The most common misreading of this principle is that it counsels inaction — keep everything
as-is because change is risky. That's backwards. ITIL 4 is explicit that this principle does
not mean nothing should change; it means change should be informed by what already exists and
what it's already achieving. Some things uncovered during this assessment genuinely should be
scrapped: a step that exists because of a tool limitation that's since been fixed, a report
built for a stakeholder who left the company years ago. The principle's job is to make sure
those decisions are made with evidence, rather than guessed at from outside.

## What's worth keeping, what isn't

A practical way to apply this: for anything currently in place, ask whether it's still
meeting a real need, whether it's being used the way it was intended, and whether removing it
would cause harm nobody currently expects. Things that pass all three tests are worth keeping,
even if they look old-fashioned. Things that fail — a legacy approval step nobody remembers
the reason for, a tool two systems have already replaced in practice — are candidates for the
next principle in this chapter, Progress Iteratively With Feedback, which is how you actually
make the change once you understand what you're changing.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Current state | What a service, process, or tool is actually doing today, assessed directly rather than assumed |
| Start Where You Are | The principle of assessing and measuring the current state before deciding what to redesign |
| Risk-averse redesign | Rebuilding only what assessment shows is genuinely failing, rather than discarding everything unfamiliar |
| Evidence-based change | Changing a service based on direct observation and measurement, not assumption |
