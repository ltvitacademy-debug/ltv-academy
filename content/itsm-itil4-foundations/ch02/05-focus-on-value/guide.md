# Lesson 5 — Focus on Value

**Chapter 2 · The ITIL 4 Guiding Principles · Lesson 5 of 28**

## What you'll learn

- What "value" means in ITIL 4, and why it's defined by the consumer, not the provider
- Why this is listed first among the seven guiding principles
- The practical question this principle hands you for any activity, metric, or meeting
- How "value" differs for different stakeholders in the same service relationship
- A concrete way this principle changes day-to-day IT work, not just strategy slides

## Value is defined by the consumer, not by IT

ITIL 4 defines value as the perceived benefits, usefulness, and importance of something —
and critically, it's the service consumer who perceives it, not the service provider. A
beautifully engineered password-reset workflow that nobody asked for and nobody struggles
with delivers very little value, no matter how clever the engineering. A clunky workaround
that gets a finance team through month-end close on time delivers real value, however
inelegant it looks from the server room. **Focus on value** is the first of ITIL 4's seven
guiding principles precisely because every other principle, practice, and process exists to
serve this one. It's the filter everything else passes through.

## The question this principle hands you

In practice, Focus on Value collapses into one recurring question: **"How does this benefit
the service consumer?"** Before adding a new field to an intake form, before writing a new
KPI into a dashboard, before scheduling a recurring status meeting — ask who's actually
receiving value from it and whether they'd notice if it disappeared. If a report has been
generated every Friday for three years and nobody opens it, it isn't delivering value; it's
consuming it, in the time it takes someone to produce it. This isn't a one-time audit step.
It's a habit of asking the same question at every point in the organization's work, which is
exactly why it's a *guiding* principle rather than a single process or practice.

## Value looks different depending on who's asking

A single service can create different value for different stakeholders at the same time. For
an internal service desk, resolving a laptop issue quickly creates value for the employee who
can get back to work, value for the employee's manager whose team stays productive, and value
for the business as a whole in the form of protected output. These aren't always aligned —
a fix that's fast for the employee might be expensive for the provider, and a process that's
efficient for the provider might feel slow and impersonal to the consumer. Focus on Value asks
IT to hold multiple perspectives at once rather than optimizing only for the metric that's
easiest for the provider to measure.

## Why this comes before the other six

The other guiding principles — starting where you are, progressing iteratively, keeping
things simple — are all *methods*. Focus on Value is the *purpose* those methods serve. If a
team applies the other six principles rigorously but loses sight of who the output is for,
they can still build something polished and useless. Keeping this principle first is ITIL 4's
way of saying: don't let process excellence substitute for outcome relevance. Everything else
in the Service Value System — the practices, the value chain activities you'll see in Chapter
3, the governance layer — ultimately has to trace back to a benefit someone outside IT
actually experiences.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Value | The perceived benefits, usefulness, and importance of something, as judged by the consumer |
| Service consumer | The role that receives a service and perceives whether it created value |
| Service provider | The role that delivers the service and must understand the consumer's perspective to deliver value |
| Co-creation of value | ITIL 4's view that value is created jointly by the provider and the consumer, not handed over by one to the other |
