## The Service Value System, at a glance

Everything ITIL 4 teaches sits inside one container: the **Service Value System
(SVS)**. It describes how all the components and activities of an organization work
together to enable value creation, for the organization itself and for its customers.
The SVS takes in **opportunity and demand** — a new idea, a customer request, a
regulatory requirement, anything that could prompt the organization to act — and
converts it into **value**, through five interacting components: Guiding Principles,
Governance, the Service Value Chain, Practices, and Continual Improvement. No single
component does the whole job; they operate together, as one system, which is exactly
why ITIL 4 dropped the rigid, sequential v3 lifecycle in favor of this model.

## The five components

**Guiding Principles** are seven recommendations — things like "focus on value" and
"start where you are" — that guide decision-making in any circumstance, regardless of
which practice or value chain activity is in play. They're deliberately few and
general, so they apply everywhere instead of being tied to one process. Chapter 2 of
this course covers all seven in depth.

**Governance** is the means by which an organization is directed and controlled — the
mechanism through which an organizational body evaluates performance, directs action,
and monitors compliance with policies and objectives. Governance sits above the day-to-
day work, setting the boundaries the rest of the SVS operates inside.

**The Service Value Chain (SVC)** is the operational core of the SVS: six interconnected
activities — Plan, Improve, Engage, Design & Transition, Obtain/Build, and Deliver &
Support — that an organization performs to convert demand into value. It's deliberately
not a fixed sequence; organizations combine these activities in different orders
depending on the situation. Chapter 3 goes deep on all six.

**Practices** are the 34 sets of organizational resources — covered in the last lesson —
that the value chain activities actually draw on to get work done: incident
management, change enablement, service desk, and the rest.

**Continual Improvement** is both one specific practice and a thread that runs through
every other component. Guiding Principles, Governance, the Value Chain, and every
Practice are all expected to keep improving over time — it isn't a phase that happens
once at the end.

## One system, not five separate things

The point of the SVS isn't that these five components exist — it's that they exist
*together*, as one connected system. Governance sets direction before the Value Chain
acts. The Value Chain's activities draw on Practices to actually get work done. Guiding
Principles shape every decision made anywhere in that chain. And Continual Improvement
doesn't wait for a scheduled review — it's expected to be happening everywhere, all the
time. That's the real shift from ITIL v3: instead of a lifecycle you move through stage
by stage, the SVS is a system you operate continuously, all its parts active at once.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Service Value System (SVS) | The overall model describing how an organization's components and activities work together to create value |
| Service Value Chain (SVC) | The SVS's operational core: six interconnected activities converting demand into value |
| Governance | The means by which an organization is directed and controlled |
| Guiding Principles | Seven general recommendations that guide decision-making in any circumstance |
| Continual Improvement | Both a specific practice and a thread running through every other SVS component |
