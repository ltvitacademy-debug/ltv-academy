# Lesson 25 — Reinstating Retired Assets

**Chapter 5 · Transfers and Retirements · Lesson 25 of 33**

## What you'll learn

- What reinstatement actually reverses, and what it doesn't
- Why reinstatement has a practical time limit
- What happens to depreciation and gain/loss when a retirement is reinstated
- The difference between reinstating an error and simply returning an asset to use

## Undoing a retirement that shouldn't have happened

Retirements happen by mistake more often than you'd expect: the wrong asset number is selected, a unit count is entered incorrectly, or an asset thought to be scrapped is later found to still be in use elsewhere in the plant. **Reinstatement** is the transaction that reverses a retirement, restoring the asset to active, depreciating status as though the retirement had never been processed.

## What reinstatement restores

A successful reinstatement undoes the retirement's effects:

- The asset's status returns to in-service.
- Any gain or loss that was calculated and recorded as part of the retirement (Lesson 26 covers that calculation) is reversed.
- Depreciation resumes, generally picking back up as though the retirement gap never happened — the asset's depreciation schedule is restored to continuity.

This is meaningfully different from simply **adding the asset back** as a new addition. A fresh addition would start a brand-new depreciation schedule, with a new date placed in service and no connection to the asset's prior history. Reinstatement preserves the original asset's identity, its original date placed in service, and its accumulated depreciation history up to the point of the (erroneous) retirement.

## Why reinstatement is time-limited in practice

Reinstating a retirement is really only clean while the retirement sits in the **current open period** or shortly after — before accounting has been created and transferred for that retirement (Chapter 6). Once a retirement's gain or loss has been posted to the General Ledger and the period has closed, reversing it cleanly runs into the same one-way-calendar problem from Lesson 8: you generally can't reopen a closed period just to undo something that's already flowed through to the financial statements. Past that point, correcting a mistaken retirement typically requires a different combination of transactions rather than a simple reinstatement.

## Reinstatement versus "putting an asset back in service"

It's worth being precise about the distinction one more time: reinstatement is specifically for **reversing an erroneous or premature retirement** — the retirement itself was the mistake. If an asset was genuinely, correctly retired (sold, scrapped) and a company later acquires a similar but different physical asset to replace it, that's a brand-new addition (Chapter 3), not a reinstatement — there's no error to reverse, just a new purchase.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Reinstatement | Reversing a retirement, restoring the asset to in-service status and undoing its gain/loss calculation |
| Depreciation continuity | The principle that reinstatement restores an asset's original schedule rather than starting a new one |
