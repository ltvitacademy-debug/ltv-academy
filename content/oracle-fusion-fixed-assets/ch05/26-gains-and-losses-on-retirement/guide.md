# Lesson 26 — Gains and Losses on Retirement

**Chapter 5 · Transfers and Retirements · Lesson 26 of 33**

## What you'll learn

- The gain/loss formula every retirement calculates
- How proceeds of sale and cost of removal push the result in opposite directions
- What happens when an asset is fully depreciated at retirement
- Why this calculation is the actual financial consequence of everything in Chapter 4

## The formula

Every retirement, whether full or partial, by cost or by units, ultimately runs through the same calculation:

```
Gain or Loss = Proceeds of Sale - Cost of Removal - Net Book Value

Where Net Book Value = Cost - Accumulated Depreciation
                       (of the portion being retired)
```

A **positive** result is a gain (the company got more for the asset, net of removal costs, than its remaining book value); a **negative** result is a loss (the asset was worth less, net, than the books said it was).

## Working through an example

Meridian Fabrication Co. retires a delivery van with an original cost of $45,000 and $38,000 of accumulated depreciation, giving it a net book value of $7,000. Meridian sells the van for $9,500, and pays $300 to have it detailed and transported to the buyer.

```
Cost:                         $45,000
Accumulated depreciation:     $38,000
Net book value:                $7,000

Proceeds of sale:               $9,500
Cost of removal:                  $300

Gain/Loss = $9,500 - $300 - $7,000 = $2,200 (gain)
```

Meridian recognizes a $2,200 gain on this retirement — it got more for the van, after removal costs, than its remaining book value.

## When an asset is fully depreciated

If an asset's net book value is already zero at the time of retirement (it's been fully depreciated down to its salvage value, or has no salvage value and is fully reserved), the formula still works the same way — net book value is just zero in the equation. Scrapping a fully depreciated asset with $0 proceeds and $0 cost of removal produces a $0 gain or loss: nothing left on the books, nothing received, nothing lost. But if that same fully depreciated asset is instead *sold* for $1,200 scrap value, the entire $1,200 becomes a gain, since there's no remaining net book value to offset it.

## Why this calculation matters

This is the point where every earlier decision in this course shows up in a single number. The depreciation method chosen (Chapter 2), every cost adjustment (Lesson 19), every reclassification (Lesson 20) that changed the depreciation rate along the way — all of it determined the asset's net book value at the moment of retirement, which is the single biggest input to whether a retirement produces a gain or a loss. A company that depreciated too slowly relative to an asset's real decline in value will tend to show losses on retirement; a company that depreciated too aggressively will tend to show gains.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Gain/loss on retirement | Proceeds of sale minus cost of removal minus net book value of the retired portion |
| Fully depreciated | An asset whose net book value has reached zero (or its salvage value) |
