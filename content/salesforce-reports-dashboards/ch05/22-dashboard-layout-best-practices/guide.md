# Dashboard Layout Best Practices

It is easy to build a dashboard that is technically correct and practically useless: a
wall of twenty widgets, every one the same size, no obvious place to look first. The
Dashboard Builder will happily let you do it. This lesson is about the editorial side of
dashboards: what to put where so the person opening it understands the state of the
business within a few seconds.

## What you'll learn

- How readers scan a dashboard and how to lay it out for that scan
- Which widgets belong at the top, and which belong lower down
- How to keep titles, colors, and filters consistent
- A short checklist to review a dashboard before you publish it

## Decide the audience and the question first

Before you drag a single widget, write one sentence: "This dashboard helps _____ decide
_____." A sales manager deciding where to focus coaching this week needs a different
dashboard than an executive checking whether the quarter is on track. If a widget does
not serve that sentence, leave it off, or put it on a different dashboard.

## Read the page like a reader does

People generally scan a dashboard from the top left, then across and down. Use that
habit:

- **Top left: the headline.** Put your one to four most important numbers here, as
  metric or gauge widgets: total pipeline, won this quarter, progress to target.
- **Top of the page: filters.** Dashboard filters sit above the widgets, as you saw in
  the filters lesson, so viewers see immediately what they can change.
- **Middle: the main story.** One or two large charts, such as a funnel of pipeline by
  stage or a trend of closed won by month, that answer the dashboard's central question.
- **Lower down: the detail.** Lightning tables and secondary charts for people who want
  to drill in. Every widget has a **View Report** link, so lean on it instead of
  cramming detail in.

The Lightning Dashboard Builder places widgets on a grid, and widgets can span
several columns and rows. Use size to signal importance: the biggest widget should be the
most important one.

## Keep it small and consistent

- **Limit the widget count.** A common rule of thumb is about a half-dozen to ten. If
  you need more, you probably have two dashboards.
- **Title with the meaning.** "Open pipeline this quarter" reads faster than "Sum of
  Amount by Stage". Use the widget title and subtitle fields for the timeframe.
- **Match chart types to questions.** Bars compare, lines trend, funnels show stages.
  Do not use donuts for a dozen categories.
- **Use color consistently.** If Closed Won is green in one chart, do not make it blue in
  the next. Reserve red for problems.
- **Use text widgets sparingly.** A short text widget can explain a definition or link
  to context, such as what counts as "open pipeline".

## A pre-publish checklist

1. Does the headline row answer the main question without any scrolling?
2. Does every widget have a title that states its point?
3. Are filters at the top, and are they useful to this audience?
4. Would this still make sense on a smaller screen? Mobile is next lesson.
5. Have you opened it as a viewer, not just as the builder?

## Recap

Start with the audience and the question, put the headline metrics top left, filters on
top, the main story in the middle, and detail lower down. Keep widget counts modest,
titles meaningful, and colors consistent. Next: how dashboards behave on mobile.
