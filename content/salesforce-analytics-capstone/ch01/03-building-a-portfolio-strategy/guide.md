# Building a Portfolio Strategy

A résumé says what you claim you can do. A portfolio shows it. For a career changer without a Salesforce analyst job on the résumé, the portfolio is often the deciding factor: it lets a hiring manager watch you frame a business question, work through messy data, and reach a recommendation. This lesson sets the strategy for the three projects you are about to build, so that by the end you have something you can publish, link to, and talk through in an interview.

## What you'll learn

- What a hiring manager looks for in an analytics portfolio
- The structure every project write-up should follow
- Where to publish, and what each location is good for
- How to present your work without exposing real company data

## What a portfolio is proving

Hiring managers reviewing a Salesforce analytics portfolio are usually asking four questions:

1. Can this person understand a business problem, not just a tool?
2. Can they get correct answers from Salesforce data, including when it is messy?
3. Can they present findings so a busy leader can act on them?
4. Are they honest about limits, assumptions, and what they would do next?

A folder of tool screenshots answers none of these. A project with a clear question, a visible method, a defensible finding, and a stated limitation answers all four.

## The one-page project structure

Give every project the same skeleton so a reviewer can skim it in two minutes:

- **The question.** One or two sentences in the stakeholder's words ("Which reps are at risk of missing quota, and why?").
- **The data.** Which Salesforce objects, roughly how many records, and how you prepared them.
- **The method.** The report types, filters, formulas, SOQL, or dashboard components you used, and why.
- **The findings.** Three to five specific, numbered conclusions, each with the number behind it.
- **The recommendation.** What you would do next, and what decision it supports.
- **Limitations.** Sample size, assumptions, anything you could not verify. Naming these builds trust rather than weakening the work.

The three projects in this course are built to slot straight into this structure: a sales pipeline analysis, a customer service analysis, and an executive CRM dashboard.

## Where to publish

- **A GitHub repository.** One README per project, plus your SOQL files and a data dictionary. Recruiters and engineers both know how to read it, and it doubles as practice for the Git skills later in this path.
- **A short PDF or slide deck per project.** This is what you send to a hiring manager who will not click around a repository.
- **Screenshots and a walkthrough.** Salesforce dashboards live inside an org, so captured images are how you show them. Annotate them with the takeaway, not just the chart.
- **Tableau Public.** Good for visual work built from data you fully own. Everything published there is public, so only put fictional or properly de-identified data in it.
- **LinkedIn and your résumé.** A single line for each project with a link and one measurable result.

Two or three polished projects beat ten half-finished ones.

## Presenting without exposing real data

This is where career changers go wrong most often. Never publish real customer, prospect, employee, or revenue data from a current or former employer. Even a "harmless" screenshot can breach a confidentiality agreement and end an interview.

Safer approaches:

- **Build in a free Salesforce Developer Edition org** with data you generate yourself, and label every number as illustrative. That is exactly what this course does with the fictional company.
- **Anonymize deliberately.** If you describe real work, generalize the company ("a mid-market software company"), round or scale figures, and remove names, account names, and identifiers.
- **Describe, do not display,** anything you cannot show. "I reduced a stalled-deal backlog by building an aging report" is still a story you can tell in an interview.
- **Check before you post.** When in doubt about an employer's rules, ask, or leave it out.

Being disciplined about data is itself a signal. Employers hire analysts to handle sensitive data carefully.

## Recap

A portfolio proves judgment, not tool familiarity. Use one consistent structure: question, data, method, findings, recommendation, limitations. Publish where reviewers will actually look, keep the number of projects small and polished, and protect real data by using your own Developer Edition org or fully anonymized descriptions.

## Check yourself

Take a project idea from your own experience. Write the question, and list what you would have to remove or change so it could be published without exposing any real company data.
