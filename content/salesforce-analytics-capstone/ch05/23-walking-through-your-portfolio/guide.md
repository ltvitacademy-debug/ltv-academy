# Walking Through Your Portfolio

"Walk me through a project you're proud of" is one of the most common prompts in analyst interviews, and it is where your three Alder & Vale Systems projects earn their keep. A portfolio does not speak for itself. You have to tell it. This lesson gives you a four-part story structure for each project and a plan for practicing it. Figures are illustrative examples from the projects.

## What you'll learn

- A four-part story for each project: problem, approach, result, what you would change
- How to pick which project to lead with
- How to show your work without reading the screen aloud
- How to handle questions about weaknesses and shortcuts
- How to rehearse

## The four-part story

Use the same order every time so you never lose your place.

1. **Problem:** who needed what, and why it mattered. One or two sentences.
2. **Approach:** the objects, reports and choices you made, and one reason for each.
3. **Result:** the number, the finding, the decision it supported.
4. **What I'd change:** one honest improvement.

Aim for about two minutes per project, and leave the interviewer room to interrupt.

## A worked example: Project 1

- **Problem:** "The sales team at a mid-market software company wanted to know where deals were being lost in the pipeline."
- **Approach:** "I connected leads, accounts, contacts and opportunities, then built conversion, rep performance and deal size analyses. I used summary reports and dashboards because the VP already lived inside Salesforce."
- **Result:** "The analysis showed a stage where deals stalled, and I recommended a follow-up process for those deals."
- **What I'd change:** "I hard-coded the quota into a formula. Next time I would store it in a field so it updates itself."

Notice the example says what you did and why, names one decision, and admits one flaw. Say only what your own project actually showed.

## Choosing the lead project

You have three, each with a different strength:

- **Project 1, Sales Pipeline:** shows you can connect objects and analyze conversion.
- **Project 2, Customer Service:** shows case volume, SLA and agent performance thinking.
- **Project 3, Executive CRM Analytics:** shows independence, data cleaning, and communication to a VP.

Lead with the one that best fits the job description. For a sales operations role, start with the pipeline; for an executive-facing analyst role, start with Project 3. Have a one-sentence summary ready for all three.

## Show, do not narrate

If you share your screen or a write-up, do not read it line by line. Point to the answer first ("the finding is here"), then explain the one decision behind it. Keep your one-page result handy so you can return to the headline. A dashboard should be open and load quickly, and you should know its running user and refresh time.

## Questions you will probably get

- **"What was the hardest part?"** Pick something real, such as combining leads, opportunities and cases, and explain how you solved it.
- **"What would you do differently?"** This is the fourth beat of your story, so you are already prepared.
- **"Did you have real customer data?"** Answer honestly: the projects use a fictional company and illustrative data, and you built them to practice realistic decisions.
- **"Why native dashboards instead of Tableau?"** Give the trade-offs you learned rather than a fixed rule.

## Rehearse

Record yourself telling each story in two minutes. Listen for filler, jargon and missing numbers. Then practice with a friend who interrupts. Adjust the order or length based on what confuses them.

## Recap

Each project becomes a two-minute story with a problem, an approach, a result and an honest improvement. Lead with the project that fits the role, be candid about what the data was, and rehearse aloud. Next, you will prepare for the salary conversation.
