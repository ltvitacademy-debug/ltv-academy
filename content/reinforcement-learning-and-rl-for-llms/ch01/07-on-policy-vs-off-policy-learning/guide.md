# On-Policy vs. Off-Policy Learning

This is lesson 7 of the Reinforcement Learning & RL for LLMs course, the final lesson of Chapter 1, RL Foundations. You now have MDPs, value functions, the Bellman equation, exploration, and episode structure. This lesson introduces the last conceptual split before Chapter 2's algorithms: on-policy vs. off-policy learning — the distinction that separates SARSA from Q-learning, and matters directly for how RLHF reuses collected data.

## What you'll learn

- The difference between the behavior policy and the target policy
- What makes a learning method on-policy vs. off-policy
- Why SARSA is the canonical on-policy example and Q-learning the canonical off-policy example
- Why off-policy methods can reuse old data, and why that matters for efficiency

## Two policies, not one

Up to now it's been easy to imagine "the policy" as a single thing. But many algorithms actually involve two roles:

- The **behavior policy** — the policy actually used to generate actions and collect experience in the environment (often an exploratory policy, like epsilon-greedy).
- The **target policy** — the policy being learned about and evaluated; the one whose value function the algorithm is trying to estimate, and which it will eventually use at deployment.

A learning method is **on-policy** if the behavior policy and target policy are the same (or forced to stay close). It's **off-policy** if they're allowed to differ — the agent can learn about one policy's value while actually behaving according to a different one.

## SARSA: on-policy

Recall SARSA's update from the main lesson sequence:

```
Q(S_t,A_t) ← Q(S_t,A_t) + α [R_{t+1} + γQ(S_{t+1},A_{t+1}) − Q(S_t,A_t)]
```

The next action A_{t+1} used in this update is the action the agent *actually takes* next, chosen by its own exploratory (e.g. epsilon-greedy) behavior policy. SARSA is learning the value of the policy it is actually following, including its exploration. That's what makes it on-policy — behavior policy and target policy are the same policy.

## Q-learning: off-policy

Q-learning's update looks almost identical, but with one critical change:

```
Q(S_t,A_t) ← Q(S_t,A_t) + α [R_{t+1} + γ max_a Q(S_{t+1},a) − Q(S_t,A_t)]
```

Instead of plugging in the action the agent actually took next, Q-learning plugs in `max_a Q(S_{t+1},a)` — the best possible action at the next state, regardless of what the agent's exploratory behavior policy actually chose. Q-learning is learning about the greedy target policy while behaving according to a different, exploratory behavior policy. That's what makes it off-policy.

## Why the distinction matters in practice

Off-policy methods have a major practical advantage: because they don't require the data to have come from the exact policy being evaluated, they can learn from **any** past experience — data collected by an older version of the policy, by a different exploration strategy, or even by a human demonstrator. This is called **experience replay** when implemented as a buffer of past transitions that gets re-sampled during training, and it's central to why Deep Q-Networks (DQN) and many LLM-training RL setups can reuse previously generated data instead of requiring fresh rollouts for every update. On-policy methods, by contrast, generally need data collected under the current policy, which usually means discarding old data once the policy changes.

## Connecting back to RLHF

Many practical RLHF pipelines use policy-gradient methods like PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization), which sit closer to the on-policy side of this spectrum — they're designed to update the policy using data generated by a recent version of itself, with mechanisms to tolerate a small amount of staleness. Understanding the on-policy/off-policy distinction here is exactly what makes design choices like PPO's "stay close to the data-generating policy" constraint make sense later.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Behavior policy | The policy actually used to collect experience in the environment |
| Target policy | The policy being learned about / evaluated |
| On-policy | Behavior policy and target policy are the same |
| Off-policy | Behavior policy and target policy are allowed to differ |
| Experience replay | Reusing previously collected transitions during off-policy training |

## Recap

On-policy methods like SARSA learn the value of the policy actually being followed; off-policy methods like Q-learning learn about a different (often greedy) target policy while behaving exploratorily, which is what lets them reuse old experience via replay.
