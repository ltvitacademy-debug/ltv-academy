## Two kinds of Tableau interview questions

A real Tableau interview almost never stays in one lane — an
interviewer moves from a conceptual question straight into a
tool-specific one in the same five minutes, on purpose, because it's
testing two different things.

**Conceptual questions** test whether you understand *why*, independent
of any one screen in Tableau:

| Question | What it's really testing |
|---|---|
| "When would you use a relationship instead of a join, or a blend?" | Whether you understand how each handles granularity and cross-database analysis differently (Ch. 8) |
| "What's the difference between an LOD expression and a table calculation?" | Whether you know LOD computes at a fixed level of detail independent of the view, while a table calc depends on the table's structure (Ch. 12-13) |
| "How would you decide between Tableau Server and Tableau Cloud for a client?" | Whether you understand the hosting/control tradeoff from Lesson 87, not just that "they're different" |
| "What makes a dashboard 'good' versus just functional?" | Whether you can talk about clutter, color intent, and layout — not just chart types |

**Tool-specific questions** test whether you've actually operated the
tool:

| Question | What it's really testing |
|---|---|
| "Walk me through publishing a workbook and setting its refresh schedule." | Whether you've done the exact workflow from Lessons 88-89, not just read about it |
| "How do you find out why a dashboard is slow?" | Whether you know Performance Recording exists and can read a Timeline/Events view (Lesson 90) |
| "Show me a FIXED LOD expression you've written and explain it." | Whether you can point to something real — like your Project 2 RFM fields — instead of describing LOD in the abstract |
| "How do you set up a filter action between two sheets?" | Whether you've actually wired one up and can describe what breaks when it's configured wrong |

Prepare for these two categories differently. Conceptual questions
reward explaining a tradeoff out loud, in your own words, to someone
who's never heard of it. Tool-specific questions reward pointing at
something real you built — which is exactly why Chapter 17 exists.

## Publishing your portfolio to Tableau Public

Lesson 87 covered why Tableau Public is free and fully public — that's
precisely what makes it the right home for your three projects. Publish
all three (Executive Sales Dashboard, Customer Segmentation Dashboard,
Enterprise Performance Dashboard) there, and write a short, honest
one-paragraph description for each one, stating the business scenario
and the specific techniques it demonstrates (calculated fields, LOD
expressions, parameters, actions). Then practice two versions of
talking through each project:

- **The 30-second version** — one sentence on the scenario, one on
  what the dashboard shows, one on the single most technically
  interesting thing in it.
- **The 3-minute version** — a full walkthrough: the scenario, the
  data, the key design decisions, and a live demo of the interactive
  parts (filter actions, parameters, navigation).

## Framing your resume around these three projects

Resume bullets built around real, specific work outperform generic
skill lists every time. Instead of "Proficient in Tableau," write
something concrete and provable:

- "Built an executive sales dashboard with region-level filter actions
  and Top-N product analysis, published to Tableau Public."
- "Designed a customer segmentation model using FIXED LOD expressions
  for RFM (Recency, Frequency, Monetary) analysis, driving a scatter
  plot with parameter-controlled axes."
- "Built a multi-sheet enterprise dashboard combining what-if
  parameters, LOD expressions, and table calculations with a dedicated
  mobile layout."

Every one of those bullets is defensible in an interview, because you
actually built the thing it describes.
