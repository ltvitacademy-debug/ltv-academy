# Lesson 4 — The MDM Business Case

**Chapter 1 · MDM Foundations · Lesson 4 of 25**

## What you'll learn

- The real costs organizations absorb from unmanaged master data
- The business drivers that typically get an MDM program funded
- Why "a single view of the customer" is the pitch most executives respond to
- How to frame an MDM business case without resorting to invented numbers

## The cost of doing nothing

MDM programs rarely get funded because someone found the topic interesting. They get funded because bad master data is already costing the business in ways people can point to:

- **Duplicate outreach.** The same customer gets mailed, called, or emailed multiple times because three systems each think they have the only record of them — expensive, and it damages trust with the customer.
- **Mismatched records that block automation.** A matching process that can't reliably tell "Robert Chen" and "Bob Chen" are the same person means someone has to resolve it by hand, every time, forever.
- **Inconsistent reporting.** Two departments report different revenue-per-customer numbers because they're each counting a different, incomplete set of "the same" customers. Leadership stops trusting either number.
- **Failed system integrations.** Every new system that needs to know "who is this customer" has to solve the matching problem itself, from scratch, unless there's a shared master record to rely on.

## The drivers that get this funded

**A single, trusted view of the customer (or product, or vendor).** This is the pitch that resonates most broadly: sales, support, billing, and marketing all working from the same answer to "who is this, and what do we know about them?" instead of five slightly different answers.

**Mergers and acquisitions.** Combining two companies almost always means combining two separate sets of customers, products, and vendors that were never designed to be compared to each other. MDM is frequently the practical mechanism for doing that integration without just picking one side's data and discarding the other's.

**Regulatory and compliance pressure.** Industries with strict identity, reporting, or anti-money-laundering requirements often can't demonstrate compliance without a trustworthy, de-duplicated view of who their customers actually are.

**Operational efficiency.** Every hour a data steward or analyst spends manually reconciling "is this the same vendor" by hand is an hour MDM automation is meant to recover.

## Building the case honestly

A credible MDM business case names the *categories* of cost and benefit above and ties them to things a specific organization can actually observe — a known duplicate-mailing incident, a reporting discrepancy leadership has already noticed, a current M&A integration. It does not need to (and, without real measurement, should not) assert a specific percentage return on investment or a named industry statistic that hasn't been independently verified for that organization. The strongest business cases in practice are the ones built from the company's own, citable pain — not a borrowed number from somewhere else.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Single view (360-degree view) | One consistent, trusted representation of a master data entity, assembled across all the systems that touch it |
| Business case | The documented justification — costs avoided, value created — for investing in an MDM program |
| Data steward | The role (introduced in Data Governance Foundations) accountable for the quality of a specific master data domain |
