# Script — Research Project Management

## Segment 1 (title)

Research timelines break the assumptions most project management is built on. A typical software task has a known shape — implement this feature, in roughly this much time. A research task often doesn't: "does this approach work" can take a day or three weeks, and the honest estimate going in is genuinely wide. Managing research well means tracking different things than a sprint board tracks.

## Segment 2 (steps)

Track hypotheses instead of tasks: a specific, falsifiable claim, the experiment that would falsify it, and the compute budget allocated to testing it. Compute budget is often the real constraint, more binding than calendar time, since a GPU-day spent on one experiment isn't available for another. And the single most useful habit is a pre-committed decision point — set before starting — that triggers a decision to continue, pivot, or stop based on specific evidence.

## Segment 3 (code)

A hypothesis log looks less like a kanban board and more like a running record: the claim being tested, exactly what evidence would falsify it, the GPU-day budget allocated, and current status against that budget. This surfaces two things a task list hides — what would actually change the team's mind, and how much runway is left before a decision has to be made regardless of the outcome so far.

## Segment 4 (code)

A time-boxed decision point is a concrete, checkable commitment written down where the whole team can see it — not an unstated intuition carried by whoever's running the experiment. Without one, a direction that isn't working can quietly consume weeks past the point where the evidence already justified stopping, simply because no checkpoint forced the decision.

## Segment 5 (outro)

Next lesson turns to a related collaboration skill: working with research scientists as an engineer, and the specific dynamics of partnering with a stakeholder whose priorities and working style differ from a typical engineering counterpart's.
