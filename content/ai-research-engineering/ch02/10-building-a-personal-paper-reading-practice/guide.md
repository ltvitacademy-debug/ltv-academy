# Building a Personal Paper-Reading Practice

Everything in this chapter so far — reading well, finding the load-bearing claim, reproducing results, and handling failure — only compounds if you keep doing it. The field moves fast enough that a single burst of reading effort fades in relevance within months. This closing lesson is about the habits that make paper reading something you sustain for years, not a skill you deploy once for a project and then drop.

## What you'll learn

- Why a reading log matters more than reading more papers
- How spaced revisits turn a one-time read into lasting understanding
- What reading groups add that solo reading can't
- How to follow the field without reading everything, through a small, deliberately chosen set of sources
- How to triage incoming papers so you're not driven by notification volume

## Keep a reading log

The single highest-leverage habit is also the simplest: write down, for every paper you read past the skimming pass, what the load-bearing claim was, what you thought of the evidence for it, and whatever you'd want to remember six months later. This doesn't need to be elaborate — a plain text file, a simple spreadsheet, or notes in whatever tool you already use is enough. What matters is that it exists and that you actually write in it, because the value isn't in the paper, it's in your own compressed judgment about the paper, which is exactly what you'll have forgotten the specifics of later.

## Revisit papers on a spaced schedule

Reading a paper once and never returning to it means your understanding decays the same way any unreinforced memory does. Periodically revisiting papers that turned out to matter — when you start a related project, when a new paper cites it, or just on a rough schedule — refreshes the understanding and often reveals things you missed the first time, now that you have more context from everything you've read since. This matters especially for the handful of papers that end up being foundational to whatever you actually work on; those are worth more than one read.

## Reading groups

A reading group forces two things solo reading doesn't: a deadline that gets you to actually finish the paper, and other people's objections to claims you might have accepted too easily. Explaining a paper out loud to people who will ask "wait, how do you know that's actually the cause" is one of the fastest ways to discover that you hadn't actually located the load-bearing claim, or had accepted a comparison you shouldn't have. If your team or lab doesn't already run one, even an informal weekly slot with two or three colleagues covers most of the benefit.

## Following the field without drowning in it

The volume of ML papers posted to arXiv daily makes "read everything" impossible, and treating it as a goal leads to skimming badly rather than reading well. A more sustainable approach is choosing a small number of sources you trust and checking them on a fixed cadence rather than continuously: a handful of labs or researchers whose work you follow directly, a curated newsletter or digest, and the relevant subcategories on arXiv itself rather than the entire firehose. The goal isn't comprehensive coverage — it's making sure the handful of things that actually matter for your work reach you, while everything else is allowed to pass by unread.

## Triage, not FOMO

Every incoming paper gets exactly the skimming pass from Lesson 5 — five to ten minutes — and nothing more, unless it clears that bar on its own merits. The fear of missing something important is what drives people into either reading shallowly across everything or burning out trying to keep up with all of it. A reading practice that survives years is one where "I didn't read that one" is a completely normal and expected outcome, not a failure.

## Key terms

- **Reading log** — a running record of the load-bearing claim and your judgment of the evidence for each paper you read past the skim
- **Spaced revisit** — returning to a paper later, often with more context, rather than reading it exactly once
- **Reading group** — a recurring group discussion that adds a deadline and outside scrutiny to your own reading
- **Source curation** — deliberately following a small set of labs, researchers, or digests instead of attempting comprehensive coverage of everything published
