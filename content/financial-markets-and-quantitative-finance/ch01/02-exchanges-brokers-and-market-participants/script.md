# Script — Exchanges, Brokers & Market Participants

## Segment 1 (title)

Lesson one mapped what gets traded. This lesson maps who trades it and where. By the end, you'll be able to name the role each type of market participant plays, from the venue a trade happens on to the firm that guarantees it settles.

## Segment 2 (steps)

A stock exchange like the New York Stock Exchange or Nasdaq is a centralized, regulated venue where buyers and sellers trade under a common rulebook. Not everything trades there. An ECN is an automated system that matches orders electronically away from a public exchange. A dark pool goes a step further, matching large institutional orders privately so a big trade doesn't move the price before it's filled. Together this off-exchange trading is called over the counter, or OTC.

## Segment 3 (steps)

A broker acts as an agent, executing a trade on a client's behalf for a commission, without taking the other side themselves. A dealer acts as a principal, trading from its own account and taking on the position in between. Zoom out further and you get sell-side firms — banks, broker-dealers, market makers — who sell products and services, versus buy-side firms — mutual funds, pension funds, hedge funds — who manage money and buy securities with it.

## Segment 4 (steps)

A market maker continuously quotes a price to buy and a price to sell, standing ready to trade either side. That provides liquidity — other participants can trade immediately instead of waiting for a natural counterparty. The market maker earns the small gap between those two quotes, the bid-ask spread, as compensation for holding inventory and the risk of trading against better-informed counterparties. Once a trade is agreed, a clearinghouse guarantees it settles by inserting itself between buyer and seller through a process called novation — so each side faces the clearinghouse's credit risk instead of a stranger's.
