# How Quants Get Paid

Lessons 28 and 29 covered where quants work and what they actually do. This closing lesson of Chapter 6 covers compensation structure — described descriptively, not as a promise of any specific outcome. Pay in this field varies enormously by firm type, role, and individual performance, and no course can guarantee what any individual will earn.

## What you'll learn

- The typical base-salary-plus-bonus structure, and what the bonus is usually tied to
- The hedge fund "2 and 20" fee model, and why it shapes how top performers can be paid
- Why compensation varies significantly by firm type: bank, hedge fund, or prop shop
- Why this lesson describes structure, not guaranteed dollar amounts

## The basic structure: base plus bonus

Across almost all of the firm types from Lesson 28, total compensation is typically structured as a base salary plus an annual bonus. The base is relatively stable and predictable; the bonus is the variable component, and it's where most of the difference between a strong year and a weak year shows up in someone's paycheck. The bonus is commonly tied to some combination of individual performance, the performance of the desk or team, and the overall performance of the firm or fund — the exact mix depends on the firm and the role.

## The hedge fund "2 and 20" model

Many hedge funds charge their investors using a "2 and 20" fee structure: a 2% annual management fee on assets under management (covering operating costs regardless of performance), plus a 20% performance fee on profits generated above some benchmark or high-water mark. This structure is relevant to compensation because it's part of why a successful fund can pay its top researchers and traders well in strong years — a fund that generates significant profit keeps a meaningful share of it through the performance fee, and some of that flows through to the people who generated it. It's a structural fact about the business model, not a guarantee of what any individual employee receives.

## Why compensation varies so much by firm type

- **Banks** tend to offer the most standardized, predictable compensation bands, with bonus pools influenced by overall bank and division performance.
- **Hedge funds** tend to have wider variance — a fund having a strong year, especially one structured so profits flow to a relatively small team, can pay very differently than a fund having a flat or losing year.
- **Prop trading firms** often tie compensation closely to the P&L an individual trader or researcher directly generates, since the firm is trading its own capital rather than managing a diversified pool of outside investor money.

## A note on framing

This lesson describes how compensation is *structured* across the industry — base-plus-bonus, fee models, and the drivers behind bonus size — rather than promising or implying any specific dollar outcome for any individual. Actual pay depends on the firm, the market environment, individual and team performance, and negotiation, and varies widely from person to person even within the same role and firm type.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Base salary | The stable, predictable portion of total compensation |
| Bonus | The variable portion, tied to individual, desk, and/or firm performance |
| "2 and 20" | A common hedge fund fee model: 2% management fee, 20% performance fee |
| High-water mark | The benchmark profit level a fund must exceed again before charging a new performance fee |

## Recap

Pay across this field is structured as base plus bonus almost everywhere, hedge funds layer a "2 and 20" fee model on top that shapes how much a successful fund can pay its team, and the exact numbers vary enormously by firm type, role, and performance — described here as structure, not as a promise.
