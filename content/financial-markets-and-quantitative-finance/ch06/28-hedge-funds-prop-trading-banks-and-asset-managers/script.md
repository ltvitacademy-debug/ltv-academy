# Script — Hedge Funds, Prop Trading, Banks & Asset Managers

## Segment 1 (title)

Chapter 5 was about risk theory. Chapter 6 turns to something more practical if you're considering this field: who actually hires quants, and what does each type of firm do differently? This lesson maps the landscape of employers before the next two lessons go deeper on the roles and pay inside them.

## Segment 2 (steps)

The broadest, most useful split in the industry is buy-side versus sell-side. Buy-side firms manage money — asset managers, pension funds, and hedge funds — buying and holding or trading assets on behalf of their own or their clients' capital. Sell-side firms facilitate markets for clients — banks and broker-dealers — making markets, executing trades, and earning revenue from spreads and fees rather than purely from investment performance.

## Segment 3 (steps)

Hedge funds pool capital from qualified investors and run a range of strategies. Long/short equity holds long positions in stocks expected to rise and short positions in stocks expected to fall. Global macro takes positions on currencies, rates, and commodities based on macroeconomic views across many countries. Statistical arbitrage uses quantitative models to find many small, short-term pricing relationships and trades them systematically at scale. And market making or market-neutral strategies profit from providing liquidity or from relative-value positions designed to be insensitive to overall market direction, while event-driven strategies trade around specific corporate events like mergers or bankruptcies.

## Segment 4 (steps)

Not every firm manages outside money. Proprietary trading firms trade the firm's own capital — no fund to raise, no investor reporting, often a narrow focus traded very actively. Bank trading desks primarily service client order flow while taking on some proprietary risk within regulatory limits like the Volcker Rule from the last chapter. And asset managers and pension funds typically run long-only mandates benchmarked against an index, aiming to match or beat that benchmark rather than pursuing the more varied strategies common at hedge funds — and together they represent the largest pool of invested capital in the world.
