## What you'll learn

## Buy-side vs. sell-side

This is the broadest and most useful split in the industry:

- **Buy-side** — firms that manage money: asset managers, pension funds, and hedge funds. They buy and hold (or trade) assets on behalf of their own capital or their clients' capital, aiming to generate returns.
- **Sell-side** — firms that facilitate markets for clients: banks and broker-dealers. A sell-side trading desk makes markets, executes trades for clients, and provides research and financing, earning revenue from spreads, fees, and commissions rather than purely from directional investment performance.

## Hedge fund strategy families

Hedge funds pool capital from qualified investors and pursue a wide range of strategies, usually charging both a management fee and a performance fee (more on that in Lesson 30). Common strategy families:

- **Long/short equity** — holds long positions in stocks expected to rise and short positions in stocks expected to fall, often aiming to reduce exposure to the overall market direction.
- **Global macro** — takes positions based on macroeconomic views across currencies, rates, and commodities, often across many countries at once.
- **Statistical arbitrage** — uses quantitative models to find many small, short-term pricing relationships between related instruments, trading them systematically at scale.
- **Market making / market neutral** — profits from providing liquidity (buying at the bid, selling at the offer) or from relative-value positions designed to be insensitive to overall market direction.
- **Event-driven** — trades around specific corporate events such as mergers, bankruptcies, or spinoffs, where the catalyst and timeline are somewhat known in advance.

## Proprietary trading firms

Proprietary ("prop") trading firms trade the firm's own capital, not client money — there's no fund to raise, no investor reporting, and typically a narrower focus on specific markets or strategies (equities, futures, options market making) traded very actively. This is a meaningfully different business model from a hedge fund, which exists specifically to manage outside investors' money.

## Investment bank trading desks

A bank's trading desk primarily services client order flow — executing trades, providing liquidity, and structuring products for clients — while also taking on some proprietary risk within regulatory limits (the Volcker Rule from Lesson 27 specifically restricts how much of this banks can do). Banks also run large research, sales, and financing operations alongside trading.

## Asset managers and pension funds

Asset managers and pension funds typically run long-only mandates, holding portfolios benchmarked against an index (such as the S&P 500) and aiming to match or beat that benchmark over time, rather than pursuing the more varied, often market-neutral strategies common at hedge funds. This is the largest pool of invested capital in the world by assets under management, even though it gets less attention in finance media than hedge funds.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Buy-side | Firms that manage money: asset managers, pension funds, hedge funds |
| Sell-side | Firms that facilitate markets for clients: banks, broker-dealers |
| Long/short equity | A hedge fund strategy holding long and short stock positions together |
| Statistical arbitrage | Systematic trading of many small, short-term quantitative pricing relationships |
| Proprietary trading firm | A firm trading its own capital, not client money |

## Recap

Buy-side firms manage money, sell-side firms facilitate markets, prop trading firms trade their own capital with no outside investors, and asset managers typically run long-only, benchmark-relative mandates.
