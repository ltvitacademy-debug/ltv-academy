# Regulation & Risk Governance Overview

The last four lessons covered how risk is measured and reduced. This closing lesson of Chapter 5 covers the rules and oversight structure around all of it — the post-crisis regulations that shape how much capital and liquidity firms must hold, and the governance structure that's supposed to make sure risk-taking stays inside the limits a firm has actually agreed to. These are US-centric examples; other jurisdictions have their own versions of the same ideas.

## What you'll learn

- The core ideas behind Basel III: risk-weighted capital, the Liquidity Coverage Ratio, and the Net Stable Funding Ratio
- What the Dodd-Frank Act was built to address after 2008, at a high level
- The "three lines of defense" model of risk governance
- The role of a board risk committee and a Chief Risk Officer, and which US regulators oversee this space

## Basel III, in brief

Basel III is the international regulatory framework (developed by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision) that sets minimum capital and liquidity standards for banks, built after lessons learned in the 2008 financial crisis. Three core ideas worth knowing:

- **Risk-weighted capital ratios** — banks must hold capital as a percentage of their *risk-weighted* assets, not simply total assets, so riskier assets require more capital backing than safer ones.
- **Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)** — requires banks to hold enough high-quality liquid assets to survive a 30-day acute stress scenario, directly targeting the funding liquidity risk covered in Lesson 25.
- **Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR)** — requires banks to fund their longer-term assets with sufficiently stable funding sources over a one-year horizon, reducing reliance on short-term funding that can vanish quickly in a crisis.

## The Dodd-Frank Act, at a high level

The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act was passed in the US in 2010 in response to the 2008 crisis. Two pieces worth knowing by name: it introduced **mandatory central clearing** for many standardized derivatives (moving bilateral, opaque exposures onto clearinghouses that stand between counterparties and reduce the chance one default cascades into others), and the **Volcker Rule**, which restricts banks from certain kinds of proprietary trading with their own capital. This is a high-level overview, not the full statute — Dodd-Frank is large and covers much more than these two pieces.

## Risk governance: the three lines of defense

Beyond capital and liquidity rules, firms are expected to organize risk oversight internally using a model commonly called the "three lines of defense":

1. **The business line** — the trading desk or business unit that takes the risk in the first place and owns it day to day.
2. **An independent risk and compliance function** — sits apart from the business line, sets limits, and monitors whether the business line is operating within them.
3. **Internal audit** — periodically verifies that the first two lines are actually functioning as designed, independent of both.

## Board oversight and key regulators

A firm's board typically has a dedicated **risk committee**, and a senior executive — the **Chief Risk Officer (CRO)** — is responsible for the firm's overall risk framework and reports into that governance structure. In the US, the main regulators relevant to this chapter's topics include the **SEC** (securities markets and broker-dealers), the **CFTC** (derivatives and futures markets), and the **Federal Reserve** (bank holding companies and systemic risk, including the CCAR/DFAST stress tests from Lesson 24). Other countries have their own regulators performing analogous roles.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Basel III | International framework setting bank capital and liquidity standards post-2008 |
| Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) | Requires enough liquid assets to survive a 30-day stress scenario |
| Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR) | Requires stable funding for longer-term assets over a one-year horizon |
| Three lines of defense | Business line, independent risk/compliance function, and internal audit |

Basel III sets the capital and liquidity floor, Dodd-Frank reshaped US derivatives clearing and proprietary trading after 2008, and the three lines of defense give firms an internal structure for keeping risk-taking inside agreed limits.
