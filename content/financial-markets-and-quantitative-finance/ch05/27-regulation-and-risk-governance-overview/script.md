# Script — Regulation & Risk Governance Overview

## Segment 1 (title)

The last four lessons covered how risk is measured and reduced. This closing lesson of Chapter 5 covers the rules and oversight around all of it — the post-crisis regulations shaping how much capital and liquidity firms must hold, and the governance structure meant to keep risk-taking inside agreed limits. These are US-centric examples; other jurisdictions have their own versions of the same ideas.

## Segment 2 (steps)

Basel III is the international framework setting minimum capital and liquidity standards for banks, built after the 2008 crisis. Three ideas worth knowing: risk-weighted capital ratios, so riskier holdings require more capital backing. The Liquidity Coverage Ratio, requiring enough liquid assets to survive a thirty-day stress scenario — a direct answer to the funding liquidity risk from a couple lessons ago. And the Net Stable Funding Ratio, requiring longer-term assets to be funded with stable sources over a one-year horizon.

## Segment 3 (steps)

In the US, the Dodd-Frank Act was passed in 2010 in response to the crisis, and it's worth knowing two pieces by name. It introduced mandatory central clearing for many derivatives, moving exposures that used to sit bilaterally between two firms onto clearinghouses that reduce the chance one default cascades into others. And it introduced the Volcker Rule, restricting banks from certain kinds of proprietary trading with their own capital. This is a high-level overview — Dodd-Frank covers far more than these two pieces.

## Segment 4 (steps)

Internally, firms organize risk oversight using what's commonly called the three lines of defense. The business line — the desk taking the risk — owns it day to day. An independent risk and compliance function sits apart, setting limits and monitoring whether they're respected. And internal audit periodically checks that the first two lines are actually functioning as designed. A board risk committee and a Chief Risk Officer sit atop that structure, and in the US, the SEC, the CFTC, and the Federal Reserve are the main regulators touching this chapter's topics.

## Segment 5 (outro)

Basel III sets the capital and liquidity floor, Dodd-Frank reshaped US derivatives clearing and proprietary trading, and the three lines of defense give firms an internal structure for staying inside their own limits. That closes Chapter 5. Up next, Lesson 28: hedge funds, prop trading, banks, and asset managers — Chapter 6 turns from risk theory to the landscape of firms that actually hire quants.
