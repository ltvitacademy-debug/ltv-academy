# Script — Equities & Corporate Actions

## Segment 1 (title)

Chapter one covered how any security trades, mechanically. Chapter two turns to the securities themselves, starting with equities — the asset class most people think of first. This lesson covers what a share of stock actually represents, and the events, called corporate actions, that change a stock's price or share count outside of normal trading.

## Segment 2 (steps)

Common stock is the standard form of ownership: one vote per share, and a claim on whatever profit is left after everything else is paid. Preferred stock trades that upside away for safety — a fixed dividend paid before common shareholders get anything, and priority if the company is liquidated, but usually no vote. A dividend distributes profit to shareholders, either as cash per share or as additional shares issued proportionally.

## Segment 3 (steps)

A stock split multiplies the share count and divides the price by the same factor — more shares, lower price, same total value, usually done to bring a high price back into a normal range. A buyback runs the other way: the company spends cash to repurchase and retire its own shares, so the remaining shares each represent a claim on more earnings. A merger combines two companies into one. A spin-off does the opposite, separating part of a business into its own independently traded company.

## Segment 4 (steps)

None of those events reflect a real change in the business that day, but they do change the raw price or share count. Look at unadjusted historical prices around a two-for-one split and you'd see the price cut in half overnight and wrongly conclude the stock crashed. That's why data providers compute adjusted prices, rescaling history backward so splits and dividends don't create artificial jumps — it's why "adjusted close" is a standard column in nearly every price dataset you'll work with.

## Segment 5 (outro)

Common stock votes and shares the upside; preferred trades that for safety; and every corporate action needs the historical price series adjusted around it. Up next, lesson seven: fixed income and yield curves — the other side of the market, lending money instead of owning it.
