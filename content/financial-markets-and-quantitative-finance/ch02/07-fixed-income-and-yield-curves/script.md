# Script — Fixed Income & Yield Curves

## Segment 1 (title)

Lesson six covered equities, ownership. This lesson covers the other major asset class from lesson one: fixed income, where you're a lender instead of an owner. Bonds are the backbone of corporate and government finance, and the yield curve they trace out is one of the most closely watched signals in markets.

## Segment 2 (steps)

A bond is a loan. Face value, or par, is what the issuer repays at maturity, typically a thousand dollars for a corporate bond. The coupon rate is the stated annual interest rate paid on that face value. Maturity is the date the bond is repaid and ceases to exist. Yield to maturity is the return you'd earn holding the bond to maturity and reinvesting every coupon at that same rate.

## Segment 3 (code)

Yield to maturity is the single discount rate that makes the present value of a bond's remaining coupons and face value equal its current price. That relationship runs in reverse: when a bond's price rises, its yield falls, and when price falls, yield rises — they're two sides of the same equation.

## Segment 4 (steps)

Plot the yield of comparable bonds against their time to maturity and you get the yield curve. A normal, upward-sloping curve has longer maturities yielding more, the most common shape, since investors want extra compensation for locking money up longer. An inverted curve has short yields exceeding long ones — unusual, and historically a signal markets expect significant rate cuts ahead. A flat curve sits in between, often a transition as expectations shift.

## Segment 5 (outro)

Coupon, maturity, yield to maturity, and the curve those yields trace across maturities — that's the vocabulary of fixed income. Up next, lesson eight: bond pricing, duration, and convexity, turning these concepts into the formulas quants actually use.
