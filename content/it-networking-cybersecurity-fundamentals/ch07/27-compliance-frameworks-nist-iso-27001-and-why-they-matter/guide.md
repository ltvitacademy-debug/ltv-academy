## A framework is a structure for organizing security work, not a product

By this point in the chapter you've seen layered technical controls and the written policies that drive them. Compliance frameworks are the next level up: they give an organization a structured, widely recognized way to organize *all* of that — the controls, the policies, the ongoing process — so it can be measured, audited, and compared against what other organizations do. A framework isn't software you install; it's closer to a checklist-plus-process that an organization can be assessed against, by its own auditors or by outside ones.

## The NIST Cybersecurity Framework: five functions

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework, published by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology, organizes cybersecurity activity into five core functions:

```
Identify   Know your assets, systems, data, and the risks to them
Protect    Put safeguards in place — access control, training, the technical layers from Lesson 25
Detect     Find security events as they happen — the monitoring/logging layer
Respond    Contain and act on a detected incident — the incident response policy from Lesson 26
Recover    Restore normal operations and capabilities after an incident
```

Notice how directly these map onto what you already know: "Protect" is the layered defense model, and "Respond" is the incident response policy. NIST doesn't invent new security ideas — it organizes the ones you've already learned into a common structure that any organization, in any industry, can use to describe where it stands and what gaps it has.

## ISO 27001: an international standard for an information security management system

ISO/IEC 27001 is an international standard, published jointly by the International Organization for Standardization and the International Electrotechnical Commission, that specifies requirements for an **Information Security Management System (ISMS)** — the ongoing, organization-wide process of managing information security risk, not just a one-time list of technical settings. Where NIST's framework is a flexible set of functions an organization can adopt loosely, ISO 27001 is a formal standard an organization can be independently **certified** against: an outside auditor reviews the organization's ISMS and issues a certificate if it meets the standard's requirements. That certificate becomes something the organization can show to customers and partners as proof.

## Why organizations actually pursue compliance

Compliance isn't pursued for its own sake — there are three concrete business reasons behind it. **Legal requirement**: certain industries (healthcare, finance, government contracting) are legally required to meet specific security standards, and failing to comply can mean fines or lost licenses. **Customer trust**: a certification like ISO 27001 gives customers independent proof that an organization takes security seriously, without the customer having to audit it themselves. **Contractual obligation**: many large companies, especially in supply chains, will only sign contracts with vendors who can prove a given compliance standard, making certification a prerequisite for winning the business in the first place. In practice, most organizations pursue compliance for some mix of all three.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| NIST Cybersecurity Framework | A structure organizing cybersecurity work into five functions: Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover |
| ISO 27001 | An international standard specifying requirements for an Information Security Management System (ISMS) |
| ISMS | Information Security Management System — the ongoing, organization-wide process of managing security risk |
| Certification | An outside auditor's formal confirmation that an organization meets a standard's requirements |
| Compliance | Meeting the requirements of a legal, contractual, or industry security standard |
