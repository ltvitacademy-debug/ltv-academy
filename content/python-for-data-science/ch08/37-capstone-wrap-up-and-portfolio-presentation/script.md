You have a cleaned dataset, tested code, two answered questions, and a chart. But an analysis nobody understands changes nothing, and a project nobody can find doesn't help your career. Let's finish the job.

First, lead with the answer. Stakeholders don't want your process. They want your conclusion, then the evidence. Structure your findings in five parts. A one-sentence headline. Two or three numbers or a chart as evidence. The so-what, meaning the action it suggests. The caveats, meaning what could make you wrong. And next steps. Our headline is that revenue is concentrated in the North, in Electronics, and in the fourth quarter, and that discounts didn't raise items per order while returns were higher. The caveats make it more credible, not less.

Second, never retype numbers by hand. Compute them from the cleaned file into a summary dictionary. Orders, revenue, the fourth-quarter share, the top region, and the top category all come straight from code, so rerunning the pipeline reproduces them every time.

Third, write a README. It's the front door of your project. State the question, the data, the key findings, exactly how to run it, and your assumptions and limits. Being honest that this data is illustrative is a strength. Hiring managers value clear limits over inflated claims.

Fourth, run the portfolio checklist. Does a fresh clone reproduce your results? Are requirements and seeds in place? Do the tests pass? Is the notebook clean, and does Restart and Run All succeed? Is the repository free of secrets and private data? Then publish it, and practice a five-minute walkthrough. In interviews, explain your decisions, not your syntax.

And that's the course. You started by setting up Python, and you finished with a tested, reproducible, end-to-end analysis. You can clean messy data, pull data from files, databases, and APIs, and write code others can trust.
