Random numbers let you shuffle, sample, create practice data, and simulate. Simulation means answering a what-if question by running an experiment thousands of times on the computer, instead of deriving a formula.

Modern NumPy uses a generator. Create one with default rng, and pass a seed. Computer random numbers are pseudo-random, generated by a formula, and the seed picks the starting point. Use the same seed and you get the identical numbers again. That makes your analysis reproducible, so a teammate gets exactly your results.

The generator draws from many distributions. Integers gives whole numbers, and like range, it excludes the upper end, so one to seven gives dice rolls from one to six. Normal draws from a bell curve, where loc is the mean and scale is the standard deviation.

Choice samples from an array, with replacement by default, so an item can repeat. Pass replace equals false for no repeats, or p to weight the choices, like a biased coin. Permutation returns a shuffled copy, and shuffle reorders an array in place. Random train and test splits are built on this idea.

Now a simulation. What is the chance that two dice add up to seven? Roll them one hundred thousand times as a two-dimensional array, sum across each row, and take the mean of rolls equals seven. True counts as one, so the mean is the fraction of sevens. We got point one six seven seven five, close to the exact one sixth.

Most simulations follow four steps. Define the random process. Repeat it many times using arrays. Summarize the results. And check against a case where you know the answer. The same recipe works for business questions, like churn.

Simulation also powers the bootstrap. With one small sample, resample it with replacement thousands of times and watch how much the mean moves. The middle ninety-five percent of those means gives a rough interval. With only eight values, treat it as an illustration, not a rigorous result.
